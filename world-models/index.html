World Models

A world model is a learned simulator. Instead of teaching an agent what to do, you teach it what happens next — and then let it search over imagined futures to decide. This page starts with no assumptions and ends at the open problems: why the field abandoned pixel prediction for latent prediction, what breaks when the horizon gets long, and why hierarchy is the current best answer.

What you should walk away with

  • A world model is a learned transition function. Given where you are and what you do, it predicts where you end up. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that one sentence.
  • Predicting pixels is the wrong objective. Most of the bits in an image are irrelevant to control. Modern models predict in a learned latent space instead, which is both cheaper and more accurate on the things that matter.
  • Planning cost is exponential in the horizon. This is the single constraint that shapes every design decision in the field, and the reason hierarchy keeps reappearing.
  • Errors compound. A model that is 99% accurate per step is 37% accurate after 100 steps. Long-horizon world modelling is fundamentally a problem of error accumulation, not of model capacity.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The idea in one paragraph

Section titled “The idea in one paragraph”

Before you reach for a cup, you have already imagined reaching for it. Not vividly, and not in pixels — but somewhere in your head there is a fast, approximate answer to the question “if I move my arm like this, what happens?” You use that answer to reject bad plans before your body executes them. A world model is the machine-learning version of that faculty: a neural network trained to answer “what happens next?”, which an agent can then query thousands of times per second to decide what to do.

Why this is a big deal

Section titled “Why this is a big deal”

The dominant alternative is to learn a policy — a direct mapping from situation to action. Policies are trained by trial and error, and they work well, but they have three properties people find limiting:

  1. They are task-specific. A policy trained to stack blocks cannot open a drawer. It has learned “what to do”, and what to do changes with the task.
  2. They need a reward signal. Someone has to define what success means, numerically, for every task.
  3. They need enormous amounts of on-task experience, usually collected by the robot itself, which is slow and expensive in the real world.

A world model has none of these properties, because it never learns what to do. It learns how the world behaves — which is the same whether you are stacking blocks or opening drawers. Then, at deployment, you tell it the goal (“here’s a photo of the drawer open”) and it searches for an action sequence that gets there. No reward function, no task-specific training. That capability is called zero-shot planning, and it is the reason the field cares.

The one-line summary

Policies learn what to do. World models learn what happens. The second is reusable across tasks; the first is not.

The catch, stated plainly

Section titled “The catch, stated plainly”

Everything good about world models comes from imagining futures. Everything hard about them comes from the same place:

  • Imagined futures drift. Each prediction step feeds the next, so small errors compound into nonsense. Ten steps is usually fine. A hundred is usually not.
  • There are too many futures to check. If there are 10 plausible actions at each step, then looking 10 steps ahead means 10 billion possible plans. You cannot check them all, ever.

Keep both of these in your head. Almost every technique in the rest of this page is an attack on one of them.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The three components

Section titled “The three components”

Nearly every world-model system, from 2018 to now, decomposes the same way. Ha and Schmidhuber’s original paper named the parts, and the names stuck:

  1. Vision (V) — an encoder that turns a high-dimensional observation (a 224×224×3224 \times 224 \times 3224×224×3 image, say — 150,000 numbers) into a compact state vector ztz_tzt​ (maybe 512 numbers). This is where almost all the compression happens.

  2. Memory (M) — the predictor. Given the current state and an action, it outputs the next state: z^t+1=f(zt,at)\hat{z}_{t+1} = f(z_t, a_t)z^t+1​=f(zt​,at​). Historically an RNN, now usually a transformer. This is the world model, strictly speaking.

  3. Controller (C) — decides the action. This is either a small learned policy (Dreamer-style) or a search procedure that rolls out many candidate action sequences through MMM and picks the best (MuZero-style, and the MPC family).

Reconstruction versus latent prediction

Section titled “Reconstruction versus latent prediction”

Here is the single most consequential design decision in the field, and it is worth being precise about it.

Reconstructive models train the encoder and predictor so that the predicted latent can be decoded back into the next image. The loss is measured in pixel space. This is what the 2018 World Models paper, PlaNet, and the Dreamer line all do.

Joint-embedding predictive models (JEPAs) never reconstruct anything. They train the predictor to match the encoder’s own output on the future frame. The loss is measured entirely in latent space. V-JEPA 2 and DINO-WM are the current representatives.

The argument for the second approach is an information argument, and it’s clean:

Why pixel prediction wastes capacity

Consider a robot arm on a desk in front of a window. To predict the next frame in pixels, the model must predict the leaves moving on the tree outside — which is high-entropy, effectively random, and completely irrelevant to whether the gripper closes on the cup. A reconstruction loss spends model capacity and gradient signal on exactly the parts of the image that are hardest to predict and least useful. Latent prediction lets the encoder simply discard the tree.

The counter-argument, which you should also hold: latent-only objectives have a degenerate solution. If the encoder outputs a constant for every input, the predictor’s job becomes trivial and the loss goes to zero. This is called representation collapse, and preventing it is the entire technical difficulty of the JEPA line — handled through asymmetric architectures, stop-gradients, EMA target encoders, and variance/covariance regularisation. Reconstruction has no such failure mode, which is precisely why it was tried first.

How planning actually works

Section titled “How planning actually works”

Suppose you have a trained predictor and a goal. Planning by Model Predictive Control (MPC) is the standard recipe, and it is simpler than it sounds:

  1. Sample a few hundred candidate action sequences, each maybe 10–30 steps long.

  2. Roll out each one through the world model, entirely in imagination. No robot moves. This is a batched forward pass.

  3. Score each imagined trajectory by how close its final latent state is to the goal latent state — typically ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ or cosine distance in embedding space.

  4. Refine. Keep the best-scoring sequences, fit a distribution to them, resample from it. Repeat a handful of times. (This is the Cross-Entropy Method; MPPI is the other common choice.)

  5. Execute only the first action of the best sequence. Then observe what actually happened and go back to step 1.

Step 5 is the part people miss, and it is what makes MPC robust. You plan 20 steps ahead but commit to one step, so model error only has one step to hurt you before you re-observe reality and re-plan. This is called the receding horizon, and it is why MPC works at all with imperfect models.

The exponential wall

Sampling fixes nothing about the underlying combinatorics. With AAA plausible actions and horizon HHH, the space of plans is AHA^HAH. Doubling the horizon squares the search space. This is not an implementation problem you can engineer around — it is the reason flat planners fail on multi-stage tasks, and the reason the field keeps returning to hierarchy.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

The setup

Section titled “The setup”

Model the environment as a partially observed Markov decision process. At time ttt the agent receives observation oto_tot​, emits action ata_tat​. We learn:

Planning as optimisation

Section titled “Planning as optimisation”

Given a goal observation ogo_gog​ with embedding zg=Eθ(og)z_g = E_\theta(o_g)zg​=Eθ​(og​), planning solves

a1:H⋆=arg⁡min⁡a1:H  d(z^t+H, zg),z^t+k+1=Pϕ(z^t+k, at+k).a^{\star}_{1:H} = \arg\min_{a_{1:H}} \; d\big(\hat{z}_{t+H},\, z_g\big), \qquad \hat{z}_{t+k+1} = P_\phi(\hat{z}_{t+k},\, a_{t+k}).a1:H⋆​=arga1:H​min​d(z^t+H​,zg​),z^t+k+1​=Pϕ​(z^t+k​,at+k​).

Note what is absent: there is no reward function anywhere. The objective is entirely “get the latent state close to the goal latent state”. This is why the approach is zero-shot — swapping the task means swapping ogo_gog​, nothing more.

Why long horizons break: the compounding-error argument

Section titled “Why long horizons break: the compounding-error argument”

Let ϵ\epsilonϵ be the per-step prediction error and assume the predictor is LLL-Lipschitz in its state argument. Rolling out kkk steps, the error at the end is bounded by

∥z^t+k−zt+k∥  ≤  ϵ∑i=0k−1Li  =  ϵ Lk−1L−1(L≠1).\|\hat{z}_{t+k} - z_{t+k}\| \;\leq\; \epsilon \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} L^{i} \;=\; \epsilon\,\frac{L^{k} - 1}{L - 1} \quad (L \neq 1).∥z^t+k​−zt+k​∥≤ϵi=0∑k−1​Li=ϵL−1Lk−1​(L=1).

Read the two regimes off that expression:

  • If L<1L < 1L<1 the sum converges to ϵ/(1−L)\epsilon/(1-L)ϵ/(1−L) — error stays bounded forever. Contractive dynamics are forgiving.
  • If L>1L > 1L>1 error grows geometrically. This is the normal case for anything interesting, because contact, collision and object interaction are locally expansive.

The practical consequence is that you cannot fix long-horizon planning by making ϵ\epsilonϵ smaller. Halving the per-step error buys you one extra step of horizon under geometric growth. You need to change kkk, not ϵ\epsilonϵ — which means changing the temporal resolution at which you plan.

Hierarchy, stated formally

Section titled “Hierarchy, stated formally”

That last observation is the whole argument for hierarchical world models, and it is worth seeing why the numbers work.

Train two predictors sharing one latent space:

  • a low-level predictor Plo(zt,at)P^{\text{lo}}(z_t, a_t)Plo(zt​,at​) operating at primitive timestep resolution;
  • a high-level predictor Phi(zt,mt)P^{\text{hi}}(z_t, m_t)Phi(zt​,mt​) operating at stride ccc, where mtm_tmt​ is a macro-action — a latent compression of ccc primitive actions, produced by a learned action encoder Ω:at:t+c↦mt\Omega: a_{t:t+c} \mapsto m_tΩ:at:t+c​↦mt​.

Because both operate in the same latent space, a state predicted by the high-level model is directly usable as a subgoal for the low-level model. No translation layer, no learned skill vocabulary, no task-specific reward. That shared-space property is the load-bearing idea.

The search cost changes from AHA^{H}AH to

MH/c⏟high level  +  Hc⋅Ac⏟low level, once per segment\underbrace{M^{H/c}}_{\text{high level}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{H}{c}\cdot A^{c}}_{\text{low level, once per segment}}high levelMH/c​​+low level, once per segmentcH​⋅Ac​​

where MMM is the size of the macro-action space. For H=32H = 32H=32, A=10A = 10A=10, c=8c = 8c=8: flat search is 103210^{32}1032; hierarchical is roughly M4+4⋅108M^{4} + 4 \cdot 10^{8}M4+4⋅108. Even generously sized macro-action spaces leave you many orders of magnitude better off. And the compounding-error bound now applies with k=ck = ck=c at the low level and k=H/ck = H/ck=H/c at the high level, so neither exponent is the full horizon.

This is not free

Hierarchy buys tractability by assuming the task decomposes — that a good plan can be described as a sequence of reachable intermediate states. Tasks where the intermediate states are not meaningful, or where the right subgoal spacing varies wildly within a single episode, do not benefit. Choosing ccc is currently a hyperparameter, and a sensitive one.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where the field actually is, as of 2026

Section titled “Where the field actually is, as of 2026”

Zero-shot manipulation works, at moderate reliability. V-JEPA 2-AC was pre-trained on over a million hours of internet video, then adapted with fewer than 62 hours of unlabelled robot trajectories from the DROID dataset. Deployed on Franka arms in labs it had never seen, with no reward and no task-specific training, it reached roughly 65–80% success on reach, grasp, and pick-and-place from a single goal image. That is a genuine result and it was not possible three years earlier. It is also nowhere near reliable enough to deploy.

Flat planning fails on multi-stage tasks, and the failure is total, not graceful. The HWM results are stark: a single-level V-JEPA2-AC planner scores 0% on end-to-end pick-and-place from a single goal image, and only succeeds when a human manually decomposes the task into greedy subgoals. The hierarchical version reaches 70% on the same task. A jump from 0 to 70 is not a tuning difference; it says the flat formulation is structurally unable to represent the problem.

Compute, not accuracy, is often the binding constraint. Hierarchical planners in that same work match or exceed flat-planner success at roughly 3× lower planning cost. Planning-time compute is a first-class design axis now, in a way it was not when the benchmarks were Atari.

Open problems worth knowing about

Section titled “Open problems worth knowing about”

  • Uncertainty is mostly unmodelled. Almost no deployed latent world model represents how confident it is in a given prediction. Without that, a planner cannot distinguish a genuinely good plan from one that exploits a region where the model is simply wrong — the classic model-exploitation failure. This is, in my reading, the largest gap between current systems and reliable ones.

  • Subgoal spacing is hand-set. The stride ccc is a hyperparameter. Humans adapt their planning granularity continuously — coarse when walking across a room, fine when threading a needle. Nothing does this well yet.

  • Evaluation is weak. Success rate on a handful of tabletop tasks is a low- resolution signal, and the benchmark suites differ enough between papers that cross-paper comparison is genuinely unreliable. Be sceptical of leaderboard-style claims in this subfield specifically.

  • The reconstruct-versus-latent question is empirically open. See the note in Tier 2. The theoretical argument favours latent prediction; the benchmark evidence is split by regime.

  • Nobody has shown compositional generalisation. Every current system plans within the distribution of dynamics it was trained on. Reasoning about a novel object with novel physics — the thing humans do effortlessly — remains out of reach.
