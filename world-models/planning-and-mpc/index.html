Planning and MPC

Model-predictive control is the trick that makes planning with a learned, imperfect model work at all: search over imagined action sequences, execute only the first action, then throw the rest away and re-plan from what actually happened. Discarding 95% of your own computation sounds wasteful. It is the single feature that keeps compounding model error from destroying you.

What you should walk away with

  • Executing one step of a long plan is the whole method. The receding horizon means model error only gets one timestep to hurt you before reality corrects the state. Open-loop execution of the same plan fails catastrophically with the same model.
  • Sampling doesn’t defeat the exponential, it just makes it tolerable. The plan space is AHA^HAH. CEM and MPPI find good regions cheaply when the objective is smooth in action space, and both degrade sharply when it isn’t.
  • The planner will find your model’s bugs. Optimising against a learned model means actively searching for inputs where it is wrong. This is model exploitation, and it is the reason a better model can produce a worse planner.
  • Planning-time compute is a design axis, not an implementation detail. How much you spend at decision time trades against model quality, horizon, and success rate. Modern papers report it as a first-class number, and older ones mostly didn’t.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Driving in fog

Section titled “Driving in fog”

You are driving in fog with about fifty metres of visibility. You plan a route as far as you can see — that gap, then the bend, then probably a straight. Then you drive five metres, look again, and re-plan.

Why not drive the whole fifty? Because your view was blurry at the far end, and after five metres you can see better. The plan was never meant to be executed; it was a way of deciding what to do right now.

That is model-predictive control. Plan far, act barely, re-plan constantly. The far-out part of the plan is not a commitment — it is context that makes the immediate decision sensible.

Why this rescues bad models

Section titled “Why this rescues bad models”

A learned world model is wrong, and its wrongness compounds: each prediction feeds the next, so errors multiply. Twenty steps into an imagined rollout you may be somewhere entirely fictional.

If you executed all twenty steps of a plan built on that rollout, you would be acting on fiction by the end. But you execute one step, then observe where you actually are and re-plan from there. The model’s error gets one timestep to hurt you, and then reality resets the accumulated drift to zero.

The thing to actually remember

MPC does not make the model accurate. It makes accuracy matter less, by never letting error accumulate for more than one step. This is why a model good enough for one-step prediction can support long-horizon behaviour it could never predict.

How you search

Section titled “How you search”

You cannot check every plan. With ten plausible actions per step and a twenty-step horizon, that is 102010^{20}1020 sequences.

So you sample. Guess a few hundred plans at random, score them in imagination, keep the best ones, and guess again near those. Repeat a handful of times. It is a hill-climb over plans, and it works well when good plans have good neighbours — which is usually but not always true.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The MPC loop

Section titled “The MPC loop”

Cross-Entropy Method

Section titled “Cross-Entropy Method”

The workhorse. It is a distribution-fitting loop, not a gradient method.

  1. Initialise a Gaussian over action sequences: mean μ∈RH×∣A∣\mu \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times |A|}μ∈RH×∣A∣, diagonal std σ\sigmaσ. On re-planning, shift last step’s solution forward by one and reuse it — this warm start is most of why MPC is affordable.

  2. Sample NNN sequences from N(μ,σ2)\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)N(μ,σ2), clipped to the action bounds. Typically N=100N = 100N=100–100010001000.

  3. Evaluate each by rolling it through the model and scoring the terminal state against the goal. This is one batched forward pass, not NNN passes.

  4. Select the top KKK — the elites. Usually K/N≈0.1K/N \approx 0.1K/N≈0.1.

  5. Refit μ,σ\mu, \sigmaμ,σ to the elites, usually with momentum: μ←αμelite+(1−α)μ\mu \leftarrow \alpha\mu_{\text{elite}} + (1-\alpha)\muμ←αμelite​+(1−α)μ.

  6. Repeat 4–10 times, then return μ\muμ.

Why it works. Each iteration moves the sampling distribution toward the good region and shrinks it. It is derivative-free, so it tolerates non-differentiable models, discontinuous costs, and contact dynamics — which is exactly the regime where gradient-based trajectory optimisation struggles.

Where it fails. A diagonal Gaussian cannot represent “go left or right, but not straight”. Faced with a bimodal optimum it fits a mean between the two modes and produces a plan that is worse than either. This is the characteristic CEM failure and it shows up whenever there is an obstacle to go around.

MPPI

Section titled “MPPI”

Same loop, different update. Instead of a hard top-KKK cut, MPPI weights every sample by the exponential of its (negative) cost:

wi=exp⁡ ⁣(−1λ Si)∑jexp⁡ ⁣(−1λ Sj),μ←∑iwi a(i).w_i = \frac{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,S_i\big)}{\sum_j \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,S_j\big)}, \qquad \mu \leftarrow \sum_i w_i\, a^{(i)} .wi​=∑j​exp(−λ1​Sj​)exp(−λ1​Si​)​,μ←i∑​wi​a(i).

The soft weighting uses information from every rollout rather than discarding 90% of them, and the temperature λ\lambdaλ interpolates continuously between “average everything” and “take the argmax”. MPPI has a derivation from stochastic optimal control via the path-integral formulation, which CEM does not, and it is the standard choice in real-time robotics.

iCEM: the practical fixes

Section titled “iCEM: the practical fixes”

Pinneri et al. identified why vanilla CEM is sample-inefficient in real-time settings and fixed it with a handful of changes that are now standard:

  • Coloured noise. Sample temporally correlated (pink) noise instead of white. White noise produces jittery action sequences that mostly cancel out; correlated noise produces smooth, physically plausible motions and explores far more effectively per sample.
  • Elite memory. Carry elites across re-planning steps rather than discarding.
  • Decaying sample count. Spend more samples on early iterations, fewer later.
  • Clipping at the action bounds rather than resampling.

The coloured-noise point is the one worth internalising: the sampling distribution should match the smoothness of plausible action sequences, and for physical systems that is not white.

Why the receding horizon works

Section titled “Why the receding horizon works”

Let ϵ\epsilonϵ bound the one-step model error and let fϕf_\phifϕ​ be LLL-Lipschitz in its state argument. After kkk imagined steps:

∥z^t+k−zt+k∥  ≤  ϵ∑i=0k−1Li  =  ϵ Lk−1L−1,L≠1.\|\hat z_{t+k} - z_{t+k}\| \;\leq\; \epsilon \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} L^{i} \;=\; \epsilon\,\frac{L^{k}-1}{L-1}, \qquad L \neq 1 .∥z^t+k​−zt+k​∥≤ϵi=0∑k−1​Li=ϵL−1Lk−1​,L=1.

For L>1L > 1L>1 this grows geometrically. Now compare two execution strategies:

Open loop — execute all HHH actions. The state error at execution time reaches the bound at k=Hk = Hk=H, i.e. O(ϵLH)O(\epsilon L^{H})O(ϵLH).

Receding horizon — execute one action, then re-observe. The state error at execution is the k=1k=1k=1 bound, O(ϵ)O(\epsilon)O(ϵ), every single step. The plan’s tail was computed on drifting predictions, but the tail is never executed; it only influenced which first action looked best.

The gap between O(ϵLH)O(\epsilon L^H)O(ϵLH) and O(ϵ)O(\epsilon)O(ϵ) is the entire value of MPC, and it is exponential in the horizon.

What re-planning does not fix

It bounds execution error, not decision error. If the model is badly wrong ten steps out, the ranking of first actions can still be wrong — you will confidently take a bad first step. Re-planning stops you compounding it, but a systematically biased model produces systematically bad decisions no amount of re-planning repairs.

CEM, formally

Section titled “CEM, formally”
μ(m+1), σ(m+1)  =  MLE({a(i):i∈E(m)}),\mu^{(m+1)},\, \sigma^{(m+1)} \;=\; \text{MLE}\Big(\big\{a^{(i)} : i \in \mathcal{E}^{(m)}\big\}\Big),μ(m+1),σ(m+1)=MLE({a(i):i∈E(m)}), E(m)=top-⁡K({−S(a(i))}i=1N),a(i)∼N(μ(m),diag⁡(σ(m))2).\mathcal{E}^{(m)} = \operatorname{top-}K\Big(\big\{-S(a^{(i)})\big\}_{i=1}^{N}\Big), \qquad a^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mu^{(m)}, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^{(m)})^2\big).E(m)=top-K({−S(a(i))}i=1N​),a(i)∼N(μ(m),diag(σ(m))2).

CEM is derived as minimising the KL divergence between the sampling distribution and the optimal importance-sampling distribution q⋆∝1{S(a)≤γ} p(a)q^\star \propto \mathbb{1}\{S(a) \leq \gamma\}\,p(a)q⋆∝1{S(a)≤γ}p(a) — hence “cross-entropy”. For a Gaussian family the KL minimiser is just the MLE on the elites, which is why the update is so simple.

The diagonal covariance is the load-bearing approximation. It cannot represent correlations between action dimensions or across time, and cannot represent multimodality at all. iCEM’s coloured noise is a partial fix for the temporal part; nothing in standard CEM addresses multimodality.

MPPI, formally

Section titled “MPPI, formally”

MPPI derives from the stochastic Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. Under a control-affine system with the noise entering through the control channel, the optimal control admits a path-integral form, and the free-energy relation gives the update

uk⋆  =  uk+∑i=1Nexp⁡ ⁣(−1λS(τ(i))) δuk(i)∑i=1Nexp⁡ ⁣(−1λS(τ(i))),u^{\star}_k \;=\; u_k + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda} S(\tau^{(i)})\big)\,\delta u^{(i)}_k}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda} S(\tau^{(i)})\big)},uk⋆​=uk​+∑i=1N​exp(−λ1​S(τ(i)))∑i=1N​exp(−λ1​S(τ(i)))δuk(i)​​,

with δu(i)\delta u^{(i)}δu(i) the injected noise on rollout iii and S(τ)S(\tau)S(τ) the trajectory cost.

Two readings of λ\lambdaλ:

  • λ→0\lambda \to 0λ→0: weights concentrate on the single best rollout. High variance, effectively random-shooting.
  • λ→∞\lambda \to \inftyλ→∞: uniform weights. The update is the mean of the noise, which is zero — no learning.

The derivation requires assumptions (control-affine dynamics, noise in the control channel) that learned latent world models generally violate. MPPI is nonetheless used in that setting and works; it is being applied outside its proof’s hypotheses, which is worth knowing when it behaves oddly.

Model exploitation

Section titled “Model exploitation”

The failure mode that makes planning against learned models genuinely hard. Writing f⋆f^\starf⋆ for true dynamics:

a⋆=arg⁡min⁡a  Jfϕ(a)but we wantarg⁡min⁡a  Jf⋆(a).a^{\star} = \arg\min_a \; J_{f_\phi}(a) \quad\text{but we want}\quad \arg\min_a \; J_{f^\star}(a).a⋆=argamin​Jfϕ​​(a)but we wantargamin​Jf⋆​(a).

The optimiser is free to find aaa where Jfϕ(a)≪Jf⋆(a)J_{f_\phi}(a) \ll J_{f^\star}(a)Jfϕ​​(a)≪Jf⋆​(a) — that is, regions where the model is optimistically wrong. And it is actively searching for them, because low predicted cost is exactly what it selects for.

This produces a counterintuitive effect: a model with lower average error can produce a worse planner, if its errors are concentrated in the region the optimiser reaches. Average predictive accuracy is the wrong metric; what matters is accuracy on the distribution the planner induces, which depends on the planner.

The principled fix is uncertainty-aware planning — penalise trajectories the model is unsure about:

J~(a)=Jfϕ(a)+β U(a),\tilde{J}(a) = J_{f_\phi}(a) + \beta\, \mathcal{U}(a),J~(a)=Jfϕ​​(a)+βU(a),

with U\mathcal{U}U an epistemic uncertainty estimate, usually from an ensemble. This works, and almost no deployed latent world model does it, because calibrated uncertainty in a learned latent space is itself unsolved.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Planning compute is now reported as a headline number. Older papers reported success rate; current ones report success rate versus planning time, because the trade-off is steep and practitioners need it. Hierarchical planners matching flat planners at roughly 3× lower cost is the kind of result that only becomes legible once compute is on the axis.

Learned value functions have largely replaced long rollouts. TD-MPC and TD-MPC2 plan over a short horizon and bootstrap with a learned terminal value, which sidesteps compounding error by simply not rolling out far. TD-MPC2 scales this to a single agent across many tasks and embodiments — currently among the strongest results in model-based continuous control.

Planning on frozen pretrained features works. DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024) builds a world model on top of frozen DINOv2 features rather than training the encoder jointly, and achieves zero-shot planning to image goals. This is significant because it decouples representation learning from dynamics learning — you can improve either independently.

Flat planning fails on multi-stage tasks, and the failure is total. The result from the hierarchical planning literature is stark: a single-level planner scores 0% on end-to-end pick-and-place from one goal image, versus 70% for the hierarchical version. Not a tuning gap — a structural inability.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Uncertainty is essentially unmodelled in deployed systems. Without it, planners cannot distinguish a genuinely good plan from an exploit of a model error. This is, in my reading, the largest gap between current systems and reliable ones.

  • CEM’s unimodality is unaddressed. Real tasks have multimodal solutions — around the left or the right of an obstacle — and the standard sampler is structurally unable to represent them.

  • The horizon is a hyperparameter and a sensitive one. Too short and the planner is myopic; too long and error dominates. Nothing sets it adaptively from model confidence, which is the obviously right thing to do.

  • The learned-cost problem. In goal-conditioned latent planning, “distance in embedding space” is a proxy for “progress toward the goal”, and it is not a metric on task progress. Two states equidistant from the goal in latent space may be very differently far in reality.

  • Real-time guarantees are absent. MPC in classical control comes with stability and feasibility guarantees. Sampling-based planning against a learned latent model has none, which is a real barrier to deployment anywhere safety matters.
