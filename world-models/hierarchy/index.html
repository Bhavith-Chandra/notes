Hierarchical Planning

Long-horizon planning fails for two compounding reasons: the search space grows exponentially in the horizon, and prediction error grows geometrically along a rollout. Hierarchy attacks both with the same move — plan coarsely over long spans, finely over short ones, and never let either exponent see the full horizon. The idea is thirty years old; what changed recently is doing it entirely inside a learned latent space, with no hand-designed skills.

What you should walk away with

  • Hierarchy fixes two problems with one mechanism. Search cost drops from AHA^HAH to roughly MH/c+(H/c)AcM^{H/c} + (H/c)A^cMH/c+(H/c)Ac, and the error bound applies with exponent ccc or H/cH/cH/c instead of HHH. Both improvements come from the same decomposition.
  • The shared latent space is the load-bearing idea in the modern version. If both levels predict in the same space, a state predicted by the high level is directly usable as a subgoal for the low level. No skill vocabulary, no translation layer, no task-specific reward.
  • The gain is not incremental. A flat planner scoring 0% and a hierarchical one scoring 70% on the same task is not a tuning difference — it says the flat formulation cannot represent the problem.
  • Hierarchy assumes the task decomposes. It buys tractability by assuming a good plan can be written as a sequence of reachable intermediate states. When that assumption fails, or when the right subgoal spacing varies within an episode, you get nothing.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Planning a journey

Section titled “Planning a journey”

You are in London and need to get to a specific meeting room in Tokyo. You do not plan this as a sequence of footsteps.

You plan: get to Heathrow, fly to Haneda, get across the city, find the building. Four steps. Then, when you are actually walking to the station, you plan the footsteps — but only those, and only then.

Two things are happening. You planned at a coarse resolution over the whole journey, and you plan at a fine resolution only over the next small piece. You never once considered the sequence of all footsteps from London to Tokyo, and if you tried, you would fail — there are too many, and your prediction of where your foot lands in hour nine is worthless.

Why the flat version fails

Section titled “Why the flat version fails”

Two separate failures, both fatal, and they compound.

The search explodes. With ten plausible actions at each step, a twenty-step plan has 102010^{20}1020 possibilities. Doubling the horizon squares the space. No amount of sampling makes a dent in that once the horizon is long enough.

The imagination drifts. Every predicted step feeds the next, so error multiplies. Ten steps is usually fine; a hundred is usually fiction. Scoring a plan by where you think you’ll end up is meaningless if the ending is invented.

Hierarchy fixes both at once, and that dual fix is why it keeps reappearing across thirty years of otherwise unrelated literatures.

What “coarse” means when there are no words

Section titled “What “coarse” means when there are no words”

The hard part is that a machine has no vocabulary of steps. It cannot plan “get to the airport” unless something gives it the concept.

The classical answer was to hand-design a library of skills, or learn one. That works and it is fragile — the skills are task-specific and someone has to decide what they are.

The modern answer is to skip skills entirely. Train two predictors that share one internal representation: a slow one that predicts where you’ll be in eight steps, and a fast one that predicts the next step. Because they speak the same language, the slow model’s prediction is directly usable as a target for the fast model. The “subgoal” is just a point in latent space — nobody named it, nobody designed it.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The two-level architecture

Section titled “The two-level architecture”

The pieces

Section titled “The pieces”

  1. Two predictors, one space. A low-level model Plo(z,a)P^{\text{lo}}(z, a)Plo(z,a) stepping one primitive action, and a high-level model Phi(z,m)P^{\text{hi}}(z, m)Phi(z,m) stepping ccc primitive steps at once. Crucially both operate on the same zzz.

  2. An action encoder. Ω:at:t+c↦mt\Omega: a_{t:t+c} \mapsto m_tΩ:at:t+c​↦mt​ compresses a chunk of ccc primitive actions into a single latent macro-action. This is what makes the high-level search space MMM rather than AcA^cAc.

  3. High-level search. Plan over macro-actions to reach the goal embedding. Only H/cH/cH/c steps, so both the search and the error compounding are over the short exponent.

  4. Subgoal extraction. Take the first predicted state z^t+c\hat z_{t+c}z^t+c​ from the high-level plan. That is the subgoal. Nothing was designed or named.

  5. Low-level search. Plan ccc primitive actions to minimise d(z^t+c,z)d(\hat z_{t+c}, z)d(z^t+c​,z). Execute the first one.

  6. Re-plan. Both levels, from the newly observed state. The receding-horizon logic from MPC applies at both levels.

How this differs from hierarchical RL

Section titled “How this differs from hierarchical RL”

The classical literature — options, FeUdal, HIRO — is also hierarchical, and the difference is worth being precise about because the words overlap.

Hierarchical RLHierarchical latent planning
Low level isa learned policy (a skill)a search procedure
Subgoals arelearned embeddings or designed statesstates predicted by the high-level model
Needs rewardyes, per levelno
Task transferretrain or relabel skillsswap the goal image
Levels communicate viaa learned interfacea shared latent space directly

Options (Sutton, Precup & Singh, 1999) established the formal framework: temporally extended actions with an initiation set, a policy, and a termination condition, turning the MDP into a semi-MDP. Everything since is a way of learning options rather than specifying them.

FeUdal Networks (2017) had a Manager emitting directional goals in a learned latent space and a Worker rewarded for moving in that direction. The direction-not- position choice was deliberate and is the paper’s most cited idea.

HIRO (2018) used raw state observations as subgoals, which made the levels trivially compatible but only works when the state space is low-dimensional and interpretable. Its real contribution was off-policy correction — relabelling old high-level transitions when the low-level policy changes underneath them.

Director (2022) planned in the latent space of a learned world model, which is the closest classical ancestor of the current approach.

The through-line

Every one of these is answering “what should the subgoal be?”. Hand-designed states, learned directions, raw observations, learned codes. The latent-world-model answer is the least designed of them: the subgoal is whatever the long-horizon model predicts, because that is by construction both reachable and on the way.

The complexity argument

Section titled “The complexity argument”

Flat. Search space AHA^HAH; error bound O(ϵLH)O(\epsilon L^H)O(ϵLH) for Lipschitz constant L>1L > 1L>1.

Hierarchical. The high level searches MH/cM^{H/c}MH/c; the low level searches AcA^cAc once per segment, and there are H/cH/cH/c segments:

MH/c⏟high  +  Hc Ac⏟low.\underbrace{M^{H/c}}_{\text{high}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{H}{c}\, A^{c}}_{\text{low}} .highMH/c​​+lowcH​Ac​​.

Minimising over ccc (treating M≈AM \approx AM≈A for a rough comparison) gives an optimum near c≈Hc \approx \sqrt{H}c≈H​, where both terms are O(AH)O(A^{\sqrt{H}})O(AH​) — the exponent drops from HHH to H\sqrt{H}H​.

With H=32H = 32H=32, A=10A = 10A=10, c=8c = 8c=8: flat is 103210^{32}1032; hierarchical is M4+4×108M^4 + 4 \times 10^8M4+4×108. Even with a generous macro-action space the second is many orders of magnitude smaller, and — the part that matters — each factor is individually within sampling range, which 103210^{32}1032 never is.

Error. The low level compounds over ccc steps, the high level over H/cH/cH/c steps:

err  =  O ⁣(ϵloLc)  +  O ⁣(ϵhiLH/c).\text{err} \;=\; O\!\big(\epsilon_{\text{lo}} L^{c}\big) \;+\; O\!\big(\epsilon_{\text{hi}} L^{H/c}\big).err=O(ϵlo​Lc)+O(ϵhi​LH/c).

Neither exponent is HHH. This is the second, independent benefit, and it is why hierarchy helps even when compute is not the constraint.

Where the argument leaks

The bound treats ϵhi\epsilon_{\text{hi}}ϵhi​ as comparable to ϵlo\epsilon_{\text{lo}}ϵlo​, and it isn’t: predicting ccc steps ahead in one shot is a harder problem than predicting one step, so ϵhi>ϵlo\epsilon_{\text{hi}} > \epsilon_{\text{lo}}ϵhi​>ϵlo​. The hierarchy trades a smaller exponent for a larger base. It wins when LHL^{H}LH dominates, which is the long-horizon regime — and it can lose at short horizons, which is exactly what the empirical results show.

Choosing the stride

Section titled “Choosing the stride”

The optimum balances the two error terms. Differentiating

g(c)=ϵloLc+ϵhiLH/cg(c) = \epsilon_{\text{lo}} L^{c} + \epsilon_{\text{hi}} L^{H/c}g(c)=ϵlo​Lc+ϵhi​LH/c

and setting g′(c)=0g'(c) = 0g′(c)=0 gives an implicit condition

ϵloLc  =  ϵhiHc2LH/c,\epsilon_{\text{lo}} L^{c} \;=\; \epsilon_{\text{hi}} \frac{H}{c^2} L^{H/c},ϵlo​Lc=ϵhi​c2H​LH/c,

which has no closed form and — more to the point — depends on ϵlo,ϵhi,L\epsilon_{\text{lo}}, \epsilon_{\text{hi}}, Lϵlo​,ϵhi​,L, none of which are known. In practice ccc is a hyperparameter chosen by sweep, and results are sensitive to it.

This is the field’s most obvious unaddressed problem. Humans vary planning granularity continuously — coarse crossing a room, fine threading a needle — and nothing here does.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

The empirical case is unusually clear-cut. On end-to-end pick-and-place from a single goal image, a flat V-JEPA2-AC planner scores 0%; the hierarchical version scores 70%. The flat planner succeeds only when a human manually decomposes the task into greedy subgoals — which is to say, only when a human supplies the hierarchy by hand. A 0-to-70 jump is not a tuning result; it says the flat formulation cannot represent the problem class.

Compute, not just accuracy. Hierarchical planning matches or exceeds flat success at roughly 3× lower planning cost, and up to 4× in some settings. Two independent wins from one change is rare, and it is the strongest argument for the approach.

Model-agnostic, which is the surprising part. The hierarchical wrapper improves several different latent world models — V-JEPA2-AC, DINO-WM, PLDM — without modification. That suggests the gain comes from the planning structure rather than from any particular representation, which is a stronger claim than a single-model result would support.

Two levels, not more. Everything deployed is two-level. Three-plus-level hierarchies are natural on paper and have not produced convincing results, which is itself worth explaining and unexplained.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • The stride is hand-set and sensitive. No method adapts ccc to the task, to the current state, or to model confidence. This is the clearest gap.

  • Tasks that do not decompose get nothing. Hierarchy assumes a good plan is a sequence of reachable intermediate states. Where intermediate states are not meaningful, or where the useful spacing varies within an episode, the machinery adds cost and no benefit.

  • Subgoal reachability is unverified. The high-level model predicts a state; nothing checks the low level can actually get there in ccc steps. If it cannot, the low level chases an impossible target and wastes the segment. HIRO’s off-policy correction addressed a version of this in the RL setting; the planning setting largely does not.

  • Deeper hierarchies don’t work yet, for reasons nobody has articulated clearly.

  • The uncertainty problem is inherited and worsened. The high-level model is less accurate by construction, and neither level represents its own confidence, so the planner cannot tell a good subgoal from a hallucinated one.
