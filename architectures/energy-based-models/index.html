Energy-Based Models

An energy-based model assigns a single number to every possible configuration and declares low numbers likely. That is the most permissive way to define a probability distribution — any function of the input gives you a valid one — and the permissiveness is paid for by a single intractable integral. Forty years of work on energy-based models is forty years of trying to avoid computing that integral, and the eventual escape route turned out to be so effective that it stopped being called energy-based modelling and started being called diffusion.

What you should walk away with

  • One idea: a scalar score per configuration. Low energy means plausible, high energy means implausible. No architectural constraint at all — no invertibility, no bottleneck, no ordering. That freedom is the family’s defining advantage.
  • One problem: the partition function. Turning energies into probabilities requires dividing by a sum over every possible configuration. In high dimensions that integral is intractable, and essentially every technique here is a way of not computing it.
  • The escape is to model the gradient instead. ∇xlog⁡p(x)=−∇xE(x)\nabla_x \log p(x) = -\nabla_x E(x)∇x​logp(x)=−∇x​E(x) — the normaliser has no xxx in it, so it vanishes under differentiation. This single observation converts an intractable problem into a tractable regression, and it is the whole basis of score matching and diffusion.
  • Every classifier you have trained is already an EBM. The logits are negative energies; softmax is the Boltzmann distribution with the normaliser computed over the (small, tractable) label set. EBMs only look exotic because the configuration space is usually large.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

A landscape, not a formula

Section titled “A landscape, not a formula”

Imagine a hilly landscape stretched over the space of all possible images. Real photographs sit in the valleys. Static, noise and nonsense sit on the peaks.

That surface is the energy function E(x)E(x)E(x). It takes any configuration — any image, any sentence, any protein fold — and returns one number. Low is good.

To generate, you roll downhill. Start somewhere random, follow the slope toward lower energy, and you arrive in a valley — that is, at something that looks like real data.

To evaluate, you read off the height. Given an image, its energy tells you how plausible the model thinks it is.

Why this is the most flexible thing you can do

Section titled “Why this is the most flexible thing you can do”

Every other generative family constrains its architecture to make something tractable:

  • A normalizing flow must be invertible with a computable Jacobian determinant.
  • An autoregressive model must impose an ordering and factorise along it.
  • A VAE needs a tractable posterior to bound the likelihood.

An EBM needs none of that. Any neural network that outputs one number defines a valid energy function and therefore a valid probability distribution. You can use any architecture, any depth, any structure.

That is a genuinely remarkable amount of freedom, and it has one bill attached.

The bill: comparing to everything

Section titled “The bill: comparing to everything”

Energy tells you a configuration is good. It does not tell you how likely it is, because likelihood is relative — you need to know how good everything else is.

To turn energies into probabilities you divide by the total:

p(x)=e−E(x)∑all possible x′e−E(x′)p(x) = \frac{e^{-E(x)}}{\sum_{\text{all possible } x'} e^{-E(x')}}p(x)=∑all possible x′​e−E(x′)e−E(x)​

That denominator is called the partition function, written ZZZ, and it sums over every configuration in the space.

For a 32×3232\times3232×32 colour image there are 2563072256^{3072}2563072 configurations — a number with roughly seven thousand digits. You cannot compute ZZZ. You cannot approximate ZZZ well. ZZZ is the entire difficulty of this field.

The trade, in one line

Other families constrain the architecture so the normaliser is computable. EBMs keep total architectural freedom and give up the normaliser. Neither is free — and knowing which constraint you are paying is the useful thing to carry away.

The escape

Section titled “The escape”

Here is the observation that rescues everything, and it is worth pausing on because it is genuinely elegant.

You cannot compute p(x)p(x)p(x). But suppose you only need to know which direction increases probability — which way is downhill on the landscape. Then:

log⁡p(x)=−E(x)−log⁡Z\log p(x) = -E(x) - \log Zlogp(x)=−E(x)−logZ

and ZZZ is a constant — it does not depend on xxx. So when you differentiate with respect to xxx:

∇xlog⁡p(x)=−∇xE(x)\nabla_x \log p(x) = -\nabla_x E(x)∇x​logp(x)=−∇x​E(x)

The intractable term vanishes. The slope of the landscape is computable even though its absolute height is not.

That quantity — the gradient of the log-density — is called the score, and learning it directly is what diffusion models do. Diffusion is not a rival to energy-based modelling; it is energy-based modelling that stopped trying to compute ZZZ.

The landscape, and what training does to it

Section titled “The landscape, and what training does to it”

The three ways to avoid ZZZ

Section titled “The three ways to avoid ZZZ”

Every training method here is one of three strategies. Getting this taxonomy straight is most of understanding the field.

  1. Approximate the negative phase with MCMC. Run a Markov chain to get samples from the model, use them for the second term. Contrastive divergence, PCD, Langevin-based methods. Correct in principle, expensive and fragile.

  2. Change the objective so ZZZ cancels. Score matching compares gradients rather than densities, and gradients do not see ZZZ. No sampling required at all.

  3. Turn it into classification. Noise-contrastive estimation asks the model to distinguish data from noise; the normaliser becomes a learnable parameter. Turns an estimation problem into a supervised one.

Strategy 1 — MCMC

Section titled “Strategy 1 — MCMC”
  • Langevin dynamics
  • Contrastive divergence
  • Persistent CD
  • Modern deep EBMs

The standard sampler for continuous EBMs. Walk downhill on the energy, with noise:

xt+1=xt−η2∇xEθ(xt)+η ϵt,ϵt∼N(0,I).x_{t+1} = x_t - \frac{\eta}{2}\nabla_x E_\theta(x_t) + \sqrt{\eta}\,\epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).xt+1​=xt​−2η​∇x​Eθ​(xt​)+η​ϵt​,ϵt​∼N(0,I).

Read the two terms. The gradient term is gradient descent on energy — it walks toward valleys. The noise term prevents you from getting stuck in the first valley you find, and it is what makes this sampling rather than optimisation.

As η→0\eta \to 0η→0 and t→∞t \to \inftyt→∞ this converges to the true distribution. Both limits are the problem: small steps mean slow mixing, and “infinite time” in practice means “you stopped early and your samples are wrong”.

Hinton’s 2002 shortcut, and the reason RBMs were trainable at all.

Instead of running the chain to convergence, start it at a data point and run kkk steps — often k=1k=1k=1. The intuition: the data distribution is already close to the model distribution if training is going well, so a short chain from data lands somewhere useful.

∇θLCD-k≈∇θEθ(xdata)−∇θEθ(xk),\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CD-}k} \approx \nabla_\theta E_\theta(x_{\text{data}}) - \nabla_\theta E_\theta(x_k),∇θ​LCD-k​≈∇θ​Eθ​(xdata​)−∇θ​Eθ​(xk​),

with xkx_kxk​ the state after kkk steps from xdatax_{\text{data}}xdata​.

It is a biased estimator and it is not the gradient of anything. CD does not minimise any objective function — it is a gradient-like update that happens to work in practice, and there are known cases where it converges to the wrong answer. Hinton was explicit about this. It worked well enough to power the 2006 deep learning revival, which is a reasonable defence.

Tieleman’s fix for CD’s main weakness. CD restarts the chain from data every iteration, so it never explores regions far from the data — which is exactly where spurious low-energy valleys form.

PCD keeps the chain going across parameter updates. Maintain a set of persistent “fantasy particles”; each iteration, run a few MCMC steps from where they were last time rather than from data.

The justification is that parameters change slowly, so the chain from the previous model is a good starting point for the current one. Better exploration, better models, and it introduces a new failure — if the particles get stuck, the negative phase becomes stale and the model is free to place mass where the particles are not.

Du & Mordatch (2019) made MCMC-based training work on real images, and the paper is mostly a catalogue of what it takes:

  • A replay buffer of past samples, reinitialised from noise a small fraction of the time. Effectively PCD with a large particle population.
  • Spectral normalisation on the energy network, bounding its Lipschitz constant so the Langevin steps behave.
  • Gradient clipping on the Langevin updates themselves.
  • An L2 penalty on the energy magnitudes, preventing the two phases from diverging to ±∞\pm\infty±∞ while keeping their difference constant.

Nijkamp et al. (2019) then dissected what these models are actually doing, and the finding is uncomfortable: with short-run MCMC, the model does not learn the data distribution. It learns an energy landscape for which the specific short chain you run produces good samples. Change the chain length at test time and sample quality collapses.

The Nijkamp result, stated plainly

Short-run MCMC training does not give you an EBM whose Gibbs distribution matches the data. It gives you a learned sampler — an energy function tuned so that KKK steps of Langevin from noise lands on something good. That object is useful, and it is not the thing the maths says you are estimating.

The diagnostic is simple and rarely reported: run the chain much longer at test time. If samples degrade, you have a short-run sampler, not a density model.

Strategy 2 — Score matching

Section titled “Strategy 2 — Score matching”

The elegant escape. Instead of matching densities, match their gradients.

Since ∇xlog⁡pθ(x)=−∇xEθ(x)\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x)∇x​logpθ​(x)=−∇x​Eθ​(x) and ZZZ has no xxx in it, the score is computable with no sampling and no normaliser.

The problem it creates. The obvious objective compares your score to the data’s score, and you do not know the data’s score. Hyvärinen’s result is that integration by parts eliminates it, leaving an objective in terms of quantities you can compute — at the price of needing the trace of a Hessian, which is expensive in high dimensions.

Two practical resolutions:

  • Denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011). Add noise to the data and match the score of the noised distribution, which is known in closed form. This turns score matching into denoising regression — and it is exactly the diffusion objective.
  • Sliced score matching (Song et al., 2019). Project onto random directions and match one-dimensional scores, replacing the Hessian trace with cheap Hessian-vector products.

Strategy 3 — Noise-contrastive estimation

Section titled “Strategy 3 — Noise-contrastive estimation”

Turn density estimation into binary classification.

Mix your real data with samples from a known noise distribution, and train a classifier to tell them apart. Treat log⁡Z\log ZlogZ as just another learnable parameter. If the classifier is optimal, its decision function recovers the density ratio between data and noise — and since you know the noise density, you recover the data density.

Why this is more than a trick. It is the same structure as InfoNCE in contrastive learning, and the same structure as a GAN discriminator. All three learn a density ratio by classification. Recognising that connection makes a lot of otherwise separate literatures look like one idea.

The catch. The noise distribution must be close enough to the data that discrimination is hard. If real and noise samples are trivially separable, the classifier learns a shortcut and the estimate is poor — the same requirement as hard negatives in contrastive learning.

Contrastive divergence, and what it actually minimises

Section titled “Contrastive divergence, and what it actually minimises”

CD-kkk replaces the model expectation with samples from a kkk-step chain initialised at data. Hinton’s original motivation is that CD approximately minimises

CDk=KL(p0 ∥ pθ)−KL(pk ∥ pθ),\mathrm{CD}_k = \mathrm{KL}\big(p_0\,\|\,p_\theta\big) - \mathrm{KL}\big(p_k\,\|\,p_\theta\big),CDk​=KL(p0​∥pθ​)−KL(pk​∥pθ​),

with p0p_0p0​ the data and pkp_kpk​ the distribution after kkk MCMC steps. As k→∞k\to\inftyk→∞ the second term vanishes and CD becomes maximum likelihood.

The honest statement, which the original paper makes. The CD update is not the exact gradient of CDk\mathrm{CD}_kCDk​ — a term is dropped, and that dropped term is not always negligible. So CD is a heuristic update rule rather than a descent direction on a well-defined objective, and there exist constructed cases where it converges to a non-optimal point.

Improved CD (Du et al., 2020) identified the dropped term explicitly and showed that including a KL-divergence correction — which pushes samples toward regions the model over-weights — substantially improves both stability and sample quality. The missing term was doing real work.

Denoising score matching — and the bridge to diffusion

Section titled “Denoising score matching — and the bridge to diffusion”

This is the whole history in one line

Energy-based models were flexible, principled and untrainable because of ZZZ. Score matching removed ZZZ but needed a Hessian. Denoising score matching removed the Hessian but only gave you the noised distribution. Running it at many noise levels and chaining them together recovers the clean distribution — and that is diffusion.

So the modern generative-modelling mainstream is the energy-based programme, with three obstacles removed in sequence. Diffusion did not defeat EBMs; it is what EBMs became once the estimation problem was solved.

Sliced score matching

Langevin dynamics, and why it converges

Section titled “Langevin dynamics, and why it converges”

The overdamped Langevin SDE

dxt=−∇xE(xt) dt+2 dWtdx_t = -\nabla_x E(x_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,dW_tdxt​=−∇x​E(xt​)dt+2​dWt​

has stationary distribution p(x)∝e−E(x)p(x)\propto e^{-E(x)}p(x)∝e−E(x). To see it, write the Fokker–Planck equation for the density ρ(x,t)\rho(x,t)ρ(x,t):

∂ρ∂t=∇ ⁣⋅ ⁣(ρ ∇E)+∇2ρ.\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\rho\,\nabla E\big) + \nabla^2\rho .∂t∂ρ​=∇⋅(ρ∇E)+∇2ρ.

Setting ∂tρ=0\partial_t\rho = 0∂t​ρ=0 and substituting ρ=e−E/Z\rho = e^{-E}/Zρ=e−E/Z:

∇ ⁣⋅ ⁣(e−E∇E)+∇2e−E=∇ ⁣⋅ ⁣(e−E∇E)−∇ ⁣⋅ ⁣(e−E∇E)=0.\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(e^{-E}\nabla E\big) + \nabla^2 e^{-E} = \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(e^{-E}\nabla E\big) - \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(e^{-E}\nabla E\big) = 0 .∇⋅(e−E∇E)+∇2e−E=∇⋅(e−E∇E)−∇⋅(e−E∇E)=0.

So the Boltzmann distribution is stationary, and the drift and diffusion terms exactly balance: drift pushes probability mass downhill, diffusion spreads it back.

The Euler–Maruyama discretisation is the update rule from earlier. Discretisation introduces bias of O(η)O(\eta)O(η), which Metropolis–Hastings correction removes (MALA) at the cost of an accept/reject step — usually skipped in deep learning, where the bias is accepted.

The practical failure is mixing time. For a multimodal ppp with well-separated modes, the expected time to cross between modes scales as eΔEe^{\Delta E}eΔE where ΔE\Delta EΔE is the barrier height. Exponential in the barrier. This is why Langevin sampling from a deep EBM on images does not reliably explore the full distribution, and no amount of tuning fixes an exponential.

Your classifier is already an EBM

Section titled “Your classifier is already an EBM”

The observation behind JEM, and it reframes a lot.

A standard classifier outputs logits fθ(x)[y]f_\theta(x)[y]fθ​(x)[y] and forms pθ(y∣x)=exp⁡(fθ(x)[y])/∑y′exp⁡(fθ(x)[y′])p_\theta(y\mid x) = \exp(f_\theta(x)[y]) / \sum_{y'}\exp(f_\theta(x)[y'])pθ​(y∣x)=exp(fθ​(x)[y])/∑y′​exp(fθ​(x)[y′]).

Eθ(x,y)=−fθ(x)[y],pθ(x,y)=e−Eθ(x,y)Zθ.E_\theta(x,y) = -f_\theta(x)[y], \qquad p_\theta(x,y) = \frac{e^{-E_\theta(x,y)}}{Z_\theta}.Eθ​(x,y)=−fθ​(x)[y],pθ​(x,y)=Zθ​e−Eθ​(x,y)​.

The logsumexp of your classifier’s logits is an energy function over inputs, and it was there all along — softmax discards it, because softmax is invariant to adding a constant to all logits and that constant is exactly what carries the density information.

JEM trains this joint model with the standard cross-entropy for p(y∣x)p(y\mid x)p(y∣x) plus a Langevin-based EBM objective for p(x)p(x)p(x), and reports improved calibration, out-of-distribution detection and robustness alongside competitive accuracy.

The connection to note. Softmax classification is a Boltzmann distribution where ZZZ is tractable, because the configuration space is the label set — ten classes, not 2563072256^{3072}2563072 images. EBMs are not exotic; they are what happens when the configuration space is too large to normalise over.

Modern Hopfield networks

Section titled “Modern Hopfield networks”

The classical Hopfield network stores patterns {ξμ}\{\xi^\mu\}{ξμ} as minima of

E(x)=−12x⊤Wx,W=∑μξμ(ξμ)⊤,E(x) = -\tfrac12 x^{\top}Wx, \qquad W = \sum_{\mu}\xi^\mu(\xi^\mu)^{\top},E(x)=−21​x⊤Wx,W=μ∑​ξμ(ξμ)⊤,

and retrieves by descending the energy. Its capacity is famously poor: about 0.14 d0.14\,d0.14d patterns for ddd neurons before spurious minima dominate.

Krotov & Hopfield’s insight is that capacity depends on the sharpness of the energy’s dependence on overlap. Replace the quadratic with a rapidly-growing function FFF:

E(x)=−∑μF((ξμ)⊤x),E(x) = -\sum_{\mu}F\big((\xi^\mu)^{\top}x\big),E(x)=−μ∑​F((ξμ)⊤x),

and capacity grows with the sharpness of FFF — polynomial FFF of degree nnn gives capacity O(dn−1)O(d^{n-1})O(dn−1), and exponential FFF gives capacity exponential in ddd.

Ramsauer et al.’s continuous version takes the exponential case:

E(x)=−lse⁡(β, X⊤x)+12x⊤x+const,E(x) = -\operatorname{lse}\big(\beta,\, X^{\top}x\big) + \tfrac12 x^{\top}x + \text{const},E(x)=−lse(β,X⊤x)+21​x⊤x+const,

with lse⁡\operatorname{lse}lse the log-sum-exp and XXX the stored patterns. The update that minimises this by concave–convex procedure is

xnew=X softmax⁡(βX⊤x),x^{\text{new}} = X\,\operatorname{softmax}\big(\beta X^{\top}x\big),xnew=Xsoftmax(βX⊤x),

which is the transformer attention update, with xxx the query and XXX the keys/values.

So attention is one step of retrieval in an exponential-capacity associative memory. That equivalence is exact, it is not an analogy, and it means every transformer contains an energy-based associative memory whose energy function is written down above. It also shows retrieval converges in one step for well-separated patterns, which is why a single attention layer suffices.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

The energy-based programme won by changing its name. Diffusion and score-based models are trained with denoising score matching, which is an energy-based estimation method, and they dominate high-quality generation. The word “energy” is mostly absent from those papers, which obscures a real intellectual continuity.

Explicit EBMs remain niche and have a defensible niche. Where you need compositionality — combining several constraints by adding their energies, which requires no retraining — EBMs do something no other family does cleanly:

Ecombined(x)=E1(x)+E2(x)  ⟺  pcombined∝p1⋅p2.E_{\text{combined}}(x) = E_1(x) + E_2(x) \iff p_{\text{combined}} \propto p_1 \cdot p_2 .Ecombined​(x)=E1​(x)+E2​(x)⟺pcombined​∝p1​⋅p2​.

Composing constraints is a product of experts, and it is free.

Short-run MCMC training is understood to be training a sampler, not a density. Nijkamp et al.’s diagnosis has been broadly accepted. Papers in this area now generally report whether the energy landscape is valid independent of the chain, and many acknowledge it is not.

Modern Hopfield networks made the attention–memory equivalence exact, and the practical consequences are still being worked out. It gives a clean account of what attention layers store and retrieve, and connects transformer capacity to a well-studied associative-memory literature.

JEM-style hybrid training is a live and underused idea. Getting calibration and OOD detection improvements essentially for free from a training-time change is attractive, and adoption has been limited by the instability of the Langevin component.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Sampling in high dimensions is unsolved. Mixing time is exponential in energy barriers. Every practical method sidesteps this rather than solving it — diffusion by annealing the noise level, short-run methods by not mixing at all.

  • Evaluation is genuinely hard. You cannot compute the likelihood, so you cannot report it. Annealed importance sampling estimates ZZZ with high variance and unclear reliability in high dimensions. Sample-based metrics measure the sampler as much as the model.

  • CD’s bias is characterised but not eliminated. The improved-CD correction helps and is not exact.

  • Compositionality is promising and shallow. Adding energies composes constraints in principle; in practice the composed distribution is often dominated by whichever energy has the largest scale, and calibrating relative scales is unsolved.

  • Discrete EBMs lag. Langevin needs gradients, so discrete spaces need Gibbs sampling or gradient-free variants, and both scale poorly. This is the same obstacle discrete diffusion faces.

Where EBMs are the right choice

Section titled “Where EBMs are the right choice”

A decision procedure

Combining independently-trained constraints? EBMs, and nothing else is close — sum the energies and you have the product of the distributions, with no retraining.

Need calibration or OOD detection from a classifier? JEM-style joint training. The logsumexp energy is already in your network.

Structured prediction with hard constraints? Energy-based learning was designed for this — LeCun’s tutorial is about ranking structured outputs, not about generative modelling.

Want the best samples? Not an EBM. Use diffusion, which is the same idea with the estimation problem solved.

Want an exact likelihood? Not an EBM. Use a normalizing flow or an autoregressive model.

Studying associative memory or the theory of attention? Modern Hopfield networks, where the equivalence is exact and the capacity results are sharp.
