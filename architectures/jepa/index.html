Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures

A JEPA predicts the representation of one part of a signal from the representation of another part. It never reconstructs the input, never uses negative examples, and never requires you to hand-specify what to be invariant to. Those three absences are the entire design — and they buy a problem in exchange: with nothing forcing the encoder to distinguish inputs, the optimal solution is to output a constant. Preventing that is what the whole literature is about.

What you should walk away with

  • The defining move is where the loss lives. Not pixel space (autoencoders), not a similarity ranking against negatives (contrastive) — a distance between two embeddings, one of which is produced by a copy of the encoder that receives no gradient.
  • Collapse is the global optimum, not a bug. If the encoder outputs a constant, the prediction is trivially perfect. Every architectural choice in a JEPA exists to make that solution unreachable, and none of them come with a guarantee.
  • There are exactly four anti-collapse mechanisms in use. Contrastive repulsion, asymmetry (stop-gradient plus EMA), redundancy reduction, and explicit variance floors. Knowing which one a method uses tells you its failure mode immediately.
  • Masking beats augmentation as a way to define the prediction task. Augmentations encode your assumptions about what should be ignored. I-JEPA’s mask-based formulation removes that hand-specification, which is the strongest argument for the family.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The idea

Section titled “The idea”

Cover the bottom half of a photograph. You can describe what is probably down there — more grass, the rest of the dog, a shadow — without being able to paint it. You know the gist, not the pixels.

That distinction is the whole point. Asking a model to paint the missing half forces it to commit to details it cannot possibly know: the exact texture of each blade of grass, the precise noise in the shadow. It will get them wrong, and the gradient from getting them wrong is mostly noise.

A JEPA asks for the gist instead. Predict what the hidden part means, in the model’s own internal language, and don’t ask about pixels at all.

Why this is the more sensible request

Section titled “Why this is the more sensible request”

Consider what a pixel-reconstruction loss is actually demanding. A photo of a lawn contains millions of bits describing individual grass blades — high entropy, essentially random, and completely irrelevant to any question you’d ask about the image. A reconstruction objective spends model capacity and gradient signal on exactly the content that is hardest to predict and least useful.

Latent prediction lets the encoder simply throw the grass away. The predictor’s target is whatever the encoder chose to keep, so unpredictable detail can be discarded rather than modelled.

The one-sentence version

Autoencoders ask “can you rebuild it?” Contrastive methods ask “can you tell these two apart?” JEPAs ask “given this part, what does the rest look like to you?” — and letting the model define “to you” is the trick.

The catch, which is severe

Section titled “The catch, which is severe”

There is an obvious way to cheat. If the encoder ignores its input and outputs the same vector for everything, then predicting one embedding from another is trivial: predict that constant. The loss goes to zero. The representation is worthless.

This is representation collapse, and it is not an edge case you engineer around — it is the objective’s global minimum. A JEPA is, structurally, an optimisation problem whose best answer is useless, plus a set of tricks that make the best answer unreachable.

That framing is worth holding onto, because it explains why this literature looks the way it does: a stream of papers each proposing a different way to break the degenerate solution, with limited theory saying which should work.

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The architecture

The five ways it collapses

Section titled “The five ways it collapses”

“Collapse” is used loosely to mean several distinct failures. Distinguishing them is diagnostically useful, because they look different in the metrics.

  1. Complete collapse. Every input maps to the same vector. Loss ≈ 0, embedding variance ≈ 0. Unmistakable.

  2. Dimensional collapse. Embeddings vary, but only within a low-dimensional subspace of the space you allocated. A 768-dim encoder effectively using 30 dimensions. Loss looks fine; the singular-value spectrum of the embedding matrix has a cliff. This is the common one and it is easy to miss.

  3. Cluster collapse. Everything maps to a small number of points. Variance is non-zero, so naive checks pass, but within-class structure is gone.

  4. Informational collapse. Dimensions are individually varied but heavily correlated with one another — the effective rank is far below the nominal one. This is precisely what Barlow Twins and VICReg’s covariance term target.

  5. Shortcut collapse. The encoder solves the task with a spurious feature that happens to be predictable — colour histogram, JPEG artefacts, border pixels. Not degenerate by any variance measure; just useless downstream.

Always check the spectrum, not just the variance

Total embedding variance can look healthy while the representation lives on a handful of directions. Compute the singular values of a batch of embeddings and look at the decay. If a small number of components carry nearly all the energy, you have dimensional collapse regardless of what the loss curve says.

The lineage, briefly

Section titled “The lineage, briefly”
  • CPC → BYOL
  • Barlow / VICReg
  • DINO → I-JEPA → V-JEPA

CPC (2018) introduced the predict-in-latent-space framing with InfoNCE, and is the direct ancestor. It still used negatives.

BYOL (2020) was the surprise: it removed negatives entirely and still worked. The community initially suspected batch normalisation was providing implicit contrast; follow-up work showed BYOL works without BN given careful initialisation, so the asymmetry really is doing the work.

SimSiam (2020) stripped it further — no EMA, no large batches, just stop-gradient and a predictor — and still no collapse. The paper frames the mechanism as an implicit expectation-maximisation, which remains the best available intuition but not a proof.

These attack collapse statistically rather than architecturally, and are worth knowing because their objectives are transparent in a way the asymmetry methods are not.

Barlow Twins computes the cross-correlation matrix between embeddings of two views and pushes it toward the identity: diagonal to 1 (invariance), off-diagonal to 0 (decorrelation). One loss, one hyperparameter, no negatives, no asymmetry.

VICReg makes the three concerns explicit and separable: Variance (hinge each dimension’s std above a floor), Invariance (MSE between views), Covariance (penalise off-diagonal covariance). Because the terms are separate, you can diagnose which one is failing — which is why VICReg is a good teaching example even where it is not the top performer.

DINO (2021) is self-distillation with centring and sharpening of the teacher output to prevent collapse. Its famous result is emergent: attention maps segment objects with no segmentation supervision at all. DINOv2 scaled the recipe and produced the general-purpose visual features most practitioners now reach for.

I-JEPA (2023) made the decisive change: drop augmentations, use masking. Predict the representations of several target blocks from one context block. No hand-specified invariances at all — which removes the standard objection to contrastive methods, that you chose the invariances yourself and may have chosen wrong.

V-JEPA (2024) carried this to video, where the temporal dimension supplies the prediction task naturally. V-JEPA 2 (2025) added an action-conditioned predictor and reached zero-shot robot planning, which is where this page connects to world models.

Three details that are easy to skim past and shouldn’t be:

ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ rather than ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​. Embedding dimensions have no natural scale, and squared error is dominated by whichever few dimensions happen to have large magnitude. ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ weights dimensions more evenly.

The predictor is conditioned on mim_imi​. Without knowing where the target is, the best prediction is the average over all possible targets — which is a blurring pressure toward collapse. Positional conditioning makes the task well-posed.

Why the asymmetry works — the argument, and its limits

Section titled “Why the asymmetry works — the argument, and its limits”

Consider the degenerate solution Eθ(⋅)≡cE_\theta(\cdot) \equiv cEθ​(⋅)≡c. For the loss to vanish we need Eθˉ(⋅)≡cE_{\bar\theta}(\cdot) \equiv cEθˉ​(⋅)≡c too. But θˉ\bar\thetaθˉ is an exponential moving average, so it reaches ccc only after the student has been approximately constant for O(1/(1−λ))O(1/(1-\lambda))O(1/(1−λ)) steps — thousands of steps at λ=0.999\lambda = 0.999λ=0.999. Throughout that window the loss is non-zero and its gradient pushes the student away from ccc.

Formally, write the student’s update as gradient descent on ∥Pϕ(Eθ(x))−sg⁡[Eθˉ(x′)]∥\|P_\phi(E_\theta(x)) - \operatorname{sg}[E_{\bar\theta}(x')]\|∥Pϕ​(Eθ​(x))−sg[Eθˉ​(x′)]∥. Because of the sg⁡\operatorname{sg}sg, the target is a constant from the optimiser’s perspective. The student is chasing a point it cannot move. Collapse would require the student and teacher to converge to a constant simultaneously, and the EMA lag makes that trajectory dynamically unstable.

This is an argument about dynamics, not a theorem

There is no proof that the asymmetry prevents collapse, and empirically it fails when λ\lambdaλ is too small — the teacher tracks the student closely enough that the pair can descend to a constant together. The mechanism is real, the guarantee is not, and papers in this area are generally honest about that. SimSiam’s EM interpretation is the most developed theoretical account and it is still an interpretation.

Read the roles directly off the algebra:

  • vvv is a hinge: it is zero once each dimension’s standard deviation exceeds γ\gammaγ (typically 1), and grows only when a dimension starts to die. This directly forbids complete and dimensional collapse.
  • ccc penalises off-diagonal covariance, forcing dimensions to carry non-redundant information. This is the term aimed at informational collapse.
  • sss is the only term that wants the two views to agree — on its own it would collapse immediately.

The three-way balance is the whole method, and the weights matter: α\alphaα too low and variance dies, ν\nuν too high and the embedding is forced to be artificially isotropic.

C\mathcal{C}C is the cross-correlation between the two views’ embeddings, computed over the batch. Driving it to the identity says: dimension iii of view A should agree with dimension iii of view B (invariance) and be uncorrelated with every other dimension (decorrelation).

The information-theoretic reading is that this maximises information content subject to invariance — the same objective as the information bottleneck, approached through second-order statistics rather than density estimation. Note the O(d2)O(d^2)O(d2) cost in embedding dimension, which is why these methods use large projector heads but bounded embedding widths.

Relation to the information bottleneck

Section titled “Relation to the information bottleneck”

All of these optimise, implicitly, the same trade-off:

max⁡θ  I(Z;Y)−β I(Z;X)\max_\theta \; I(Z; Y) - \beta\, I(Z; X)θmax​I(Z;Y)−βI(Z;X)

with YYY the unknown downstream target. The families differ in how they keep I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) from hitting zero without access to YYY:

  • reconstruction sets a floor on I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) by requiring invertibility;
  • contrastive learning adds an explicit repulsive term;
  • redundancy reduction enforces a rank condition on second-order statistics;
  • asymmetry relies on a dynamical obstruction.

Only the first three admit a static characterisation. The fourth — the most used — does not, and that is the honest state of the theory.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

JEPAs work, and the reasons remain partly empirical. I-JEPA and V-JEPA produce strong general-purpose features without augmentations or negatives, and V-JEPA 2 extended this to action-conditioned prediction supporting zero-shot robot planning at roughly 65–80% success on manipulation tasks in unseen labs. That is a real capability that did not exist three years earlier.

The reconstruct-versus-predict argument is not settled. Masked autoencoders reconstruct raw pixels and remain competitive, which the theoretical argument says they shouldn’t be. The usual reconciliation — that a 75% mask ratio makes the task semantic enough that pixel detail stops dominating the gradient — is plausible and unproven. Treat “latent prediction is strictly better” as directional.

DINOv2 is the practical default, and it is a self-distillation method rather than a JEPA proper. Worth noting because the taxonomy and the leaderboard don’t line up: the most-used general visual encoder is from the DINO line, not the JEPA line, even though they share the asymmetry mechanism.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No theory of collapse prevention. Given a proposed asymmetry, nothing predicts whether it suffices. Every new method establishes this by running it.

  • The predictor’s role is unclear. SimSiam showed the predictor head is necessary and the EMA is not. Why a small MLP between student output and loss changes the optimisation landscape so decisively is unexplained.

  • Masking introduces its own biases. I-JEPA’s pitch is that it avoids hand-specified invariances — but block masking encodes assumptions about spatial scale and locality. Whether those are meaningfully weaker assumptions than augmentations is asserted more than demonstrated.

  • Embedding spaces still aren’t interoperable. Two JEPAs trained identically from different seeds produce incomparable spaces. This forces hierarchical systems to train levels jointly in a shared space rather than composing independently trained encoders.

  • Evaluation remains circular. Linear probing is the standard metric and is known to be a poor proxy; alignment/uniformity is better but defined relative to an augmentation family, which is precisely what these methods claim to avoid needing.
