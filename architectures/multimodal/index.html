Multimodal Models

A language model consumes token embeddings. An image is a grid of pixels. The entire field of vision-language modelling is about bridging that gap, and the surprising empirical finding is that the bridge can be almost trivial — a single linear layer, trained on a modest amount of data, is enough to make a frozen vision encoder legible to a frozen language model. What is not trivial is resolution, and that turns out to be where most real capability differences live.

What you should walk away with

  • Two ways in: projection or cross-attention. Project image features into the token space and prepend them, or leave the text stream alone and let it attend to image features through inserted layers. The first is simpler and now dominant; the second is cheaper at long context.
  • The connector can be a single linear layer. LLaVA’s result — that a linear projection plus modest instruction tuning produces a capable VLM — was the finding that made this accessible. It works because CLIP-style encoders already produce language-aligned features.
  • Resolution is the hidden bottleneck. A 336×336 image at patch 14 gives 576 tokens and cannot resolve small text or fine detail. Nearly every “the model can’t read this chart” failure is a resolution failure, not a reasoning failure.
  • Contrastive and generative pretraining teach different things. CLIP learns to match images and text, which is retrieval. It does not learn to describe, which is why VLMs bolt a generative decoder onto a contrastively-trained encoder.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The translation problem

Section titled “The translation problem”

A language model has one input format: a sequence of vectors, each standing for a token. It has no notion of pixels.

So to show it an image you must turn the image into vectors of the same kind — vectors that sit in the same space as word embeddings and mean something to the same machinery.

The remarkable part is how little work this takes. Run the image through a pretrained vision encoder, multiply the output by one learned matrix, and paste the result into the prompt where words would go. The language model treats them as if they were tokens, and it broadly works.

Why one matrix is enough

Section titled “Why one matrix is enough”

This only works because of what the vision encoder already learned.

A CLIP-style encoder was trained to place images near their captions in a shared space. So its output is already organised by linguistic meaning — a photo of a dog sits near wherever “a photo of a dog” sits. The encoder did the hard alignment work during its own pretraining.

The projection layer therefore has an easy job: not to create meaning, but to rotate an already-meaningful space into the particular coordinates the language model happens to use. That is close to a change of basis, and a linear map can do it.

The consequence worth drawing

Your VLM’s ceiling is largely set by the vision encoder, not the connector. If the encoder never learned to distinguish two things, no projection layer or instruction-tuning recipe will recover the distinction. Debug the encoder first.

What actually breaks

Section titled “What actually breaks”

Ask a modern VLM to describe a photograph and it does well. Ask it to read the axis labels on a chart, count seventeen objects, or tell you what a small sign in the background says, and it often fails.

The reason is almost always resolution. A 336×336 input divided into 14×14 patches gives 576 patches — and a caption-sized piece of text in the corner of the original photo occupies a fraction of one patch. The information is gone before any reasoning begins.

This is why so much recent work is about seeing more pixels rather than reasoning better: tiling the image, running the encoder at higher resolution, or processing crops alongside a global view.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Two architectures

Section titled “Two architectures”

The lineage

Section titled “The lineage”

  1. CLIP (2021) — contrastive image/text pretraining. Produces a shared embedding space, excellent for retrieval and zero-shot classification. Cannot generate text.

  2. Flamingo (2022) — freeze a vision encoder and a language model, insert gated cross-attention layers between them. First convincing few-shot vision-language model. Architecturally invasive.

  3. BLIP-2 (2023) — insert a small trainable transformer (the Q-Former) between frozen encoder and frozen LLM, using a fixed set of learned query vectors to extract a compact summary. Bridges the gap with very few trainable parameters.

  4. LLaVA (2023) — replace the Q-Former with one linear layer, train on GPT-generated instruction data. Comparable results at a fraction of the complexity. This is what made VLMs reproducible in academic labs, and the projection approach has dominated since.

  5. High-resolution era (2024–) — the bottleneck moves from alignment to perception. Tiling, dynamic resolution, and multi-encoder designs.

Handling resolution

Section titled “Handling resolution”

Vision transformers are trained at a fixed resolution, and naively upscaling breaks the position embeddings. The practical approaches:

  • Tiling
  • Interpolated positions
  • Resampling
  • Multiple encoders

Split a high-resolution image into a grid of tiles, each at the encoder’s native resolution, and encode each separately. Add a downscaled global view so the model retains overall layout.

A 2×2 tiling plus global view at 576 tokens each gives 2,880 image tokens — which is why high-resolution VLMs consume so much context. NVLM’s contribution here was 1-D tile tagging: mark tile positions explicitly in the token stream, so the model knows how the pieces fit together.

This is the dominant approach, and its cost is linear in tile count.

Interpolate the ViT’s position embeddings to the new grid size and fine-tune briefly. Cheaper than tiling, and quality degrades as you move further from the training resolution — the encoder’s learned features were tuned for a particular patch-to-object scale.

Use a fixed number of learned queries to attend over however many patches the encoder produced, compressing to a constant token count regardless of input size. The Q-Former and Perceiver Resampler both do this.

Fixed cost, and it is a bottleneck by construction: 32 or 64 query vectors cannot carry the information in a dense 4K document scan. Good for photographs, poor for text-heavy images.

Run several vision encoders with different strengths — CLIP for semantics, a self-supervised encoder like DINOv2 for spatial structure, an OCR-focused encoder for text — and concatenate their features.

Cambrian-1’s systematic study found vision-encoder choice matters far more than the connector design, and that combining encoders with complementary training objectives beats any single one. Cost is running several encoders per image.

Most VLM failures are perception, not reasoning

Before concluding a model “cannot reason about” a chart, check whether it can read the chart. Crop the region of interest and ask again at full resolution. In practice a large share of apparent reasoning failures resolve, which tells you the information never reached the language model.

The projection connector

Why linear suffices. If the vision encoder’s output space V\mathcal{V}V and the LM’s embedding space E\mathcal{E}E both encode semantics, and the semantic structure is approximately preserved up to a linear transformation, then a matrix suffices to align them. CLIP training is what makes the antecedent approximately true — it explicitly optimised V\mathcal{V}V for agreement with a text encoder.

The prediction this makes, and it holds: a non-language-aligned encoder needs a more expressive connector. Purely self-supervised encoders like DINOv2 do worse with a bare linear projection than CLIP encoders do.

Gated cross-attention

Section titled “Gated cross-attention”

Flamingo inserts layers computing

h←h+tanh⁡(α)⋅CrossAttn⁡(Q=hWQ, K=ZWK, V=ZWV),h \leftarrow h + \tanh(\alpha)\cdot \operatorname{CrossAttn}\big(Q = hW_Q,\ K = ZW_K,\ V = ZW_V\big),h←h+tanh(α)⋅CrossAttn(Q=hWQ​, K=ZWK​, V=ZWV​),

with α\alphaα a learned scalar initialised to zero. Since tanh⁡(0)=0\tanh(0) = 0tanh(0)=0, the inserted layer is exactly the identity at initialisation and the frozen language model is bit-for-bit unchanged. The gate opens only as training finds the visual information useful.

This is the same trick as LoRA’s zero-initialised BBB and the same reason: start as a no-op so the pretrained model is not disrupted at step zero. It recurs across the field and is worth recognising as a pattern.

Compute comparison per layer, for LLL text tokens and PPP image tokens:

cross-attention=O(L⋅P⋅d),concatenation=O((L+P)2d).\text{cross-attention} = O(L \cdot P \cdot d), \qquad \text{concatenation} = O\big((L+P)^2 d\big).cross-attention=O(L⋅P⋅d),concatenation=O((L+P)2d).

Cross-attention is linear in PPP; concatenation is quadratic in the total. For large PPP — high-resolution or video — cross-attention is substantially cheaper, which is why the Flamingo approach persists for video despite projection winning for single images.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Projection won for images; cross-attention persists for video. The simplicity of prepending tokens beat architectural surgery for the single-image case. Video has enough frames that quadratic cost in total tokens becomes prohibitive, so the cheaper cross-attention formulation remains competitive there.

Vision encoder choice dominates connector design. Cambrian-1’s systematic sweep is the clearest evidence: swapping the encoder moves benchmarks far more than swapping the connector, and combining encoders trained with different objectives beats any one of them. The field spent two years optimising the wrong component.

Native multimodal pretraining is displacing the bolt-on recipe. Rather than gluing a trained vision encoder to a trained LLM, frontier models increasingly train on interleaved image-text data from the start. Better integration, far more expensive, and it forecloses the swap-in-a-better-encoder option.

Resolution is where the visible capability differences now are. Chart reading, document understanding, OCR, and fine-grained counting all improved primarily through seeing more pixels rather than through better reasoning.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Fine-grained spatial reasoning is weak. Counting, relative position, and precise localisation remain poor relative to how well these models describe scenes. Patch-level features may simply be the wrong granularity.

  • Modality imbalance. Vision-language models are overwhelmingly text-driven; ablating the image often changes the answer less than it should. Benchmarks that can be solved from the text alone are common and inflate reported performance.

  • No good multimodal evaluation. Many VQA benchmarks are answerable from language priors. Reported scores frequently measure how well the model guesses what a question about an image usually asks.

  • Video is largely unsolved. Long videos have too many frames to encode densely; frame sampling discards temporal detail. Nothing handles hour-long video well.

  • Cross-modal hallucination. Models describe objects that are not present, particularly plausible co-occurring ones — a bicycle near a bike rack. The language prior overrides the visual evidence and there is no reliable fix.
