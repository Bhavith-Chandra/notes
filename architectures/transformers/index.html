Transformers

A transformer is a stack of one idea: let every position look at every other position and decide, per position, what to pull in. That idea is differentiable dictionary lookup, it costs O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2), and the entire engineering history since 2017 is a fight with that exponent. Almost nothing about the core has changed; almost everything around it has.

What you should walk away with

  • Attention is a soft dictionary lookup. Queries ask, keys advertise, values are returned — with a softmax deciding how much of each. Once you see it that way, multi-head, cross-attention, and causal masking are all trivial variations.
  • The quadratic cost is not an implementation flaw. Every position attending to every other is what gives transformers their power; it is also why context length is expensive. Techniques that beat O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) do so by giving something up, and it is always worth asking what.
  • The 2017 architecture is not the 2026 architecture. Post-norm → pre-norm, learned positions → RoPE, ReLU → SwiGLU, MHA → GQA. None of these were in the original paper and all of them are in every serious model today.
  • FlashAttention changed nothing mathematically and everything practically. It computes the same function. It just stops moving intermediate results to slow memory — a reminder that on modern hardware, memory traffic, not FLOPs, is usually the binding constraint.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The problem attention solves

Section titled “The problem attention solves”

Read this sentence: “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big.”

What does it refer to? The trophy. Now change one word: “…because it was too small.” Now it is the suitcase.

To resolve it, you had to look at a word seven positions away and let its meaning change how you interpret the pronoun. That is the whole problem. A model processing words strictly left to right, carrying a fixed-size memory, has to have guessed in advance that “trophy” would matter later and kept it around. Most of the time it guesses wrong, and information about distant words decays.

Attention removes the guessing. At the moment the model processes it, it reaches back and looks at every other word directly, weighting each by how relevant it is right now. Nothing has to be predicted in advance and nothing decays with distance — position 1 and position 500 are equally reachable.

The lookup metaphor, which is exact rather than loose

Section titled “The lookup metaphor, which is exact rather than loose”

Think of a dictionary. You have a key you look up and you get a value back. dictionary["cat"] → "a small furry animal". Exact match, one result.

Attention is the soft version:

  • Every position emits a query: what am I looking for?
  • Every position emits a key: what do I have on offer?
  • Every position emits a value: what I’ll hand over if you pick me.

Instead of an exact match, you compare your query against every key, turn those similarities into percentages, and take a weighted blend of all the values. The pronoun it emits a query meaning roughly “I need a noun that could be big”; the key at trophy matches it better than the key at suitcase; so the returned value is mostly trophy-flavoured.

Why soft matters

Hard lookup is not differentiable — you cannot take a gradient through “pick entry 4”. The softmax makes the choice a smooth blend, so the model can learn what to look for by gradient descent. Differentiability is the reason for the blend, not some deep claim about how meaning works.

The bill

Section titled “The bill”

Every position compares itself against every other position. Ten words, a hundred comparisons. A thousand words, a million. A hundred thousand words, ten billion — per layer, per head.

This is the single fact that shapes everything downstream: why context windows were tiny for years, why they got expensive rather than free, why there is an entire research industry in “efficient attention”, and why state-space models keep getting a second look. Hold onto it.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

One attention head, end to end

Section titled “One attention head, end to end”

  1. Project. Multiply the input by three learned matrices to get QQQ, KKK, VVV. Same input, three different views of it.

  2. Score. QK⊤QK^\topQK⊤ — every query dotted with every key. This is the n×nn \times nn×n matrix, and it is where both the power and the cost live.

  3. Scale. Divide by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​. Not a tuned hyperparameter; see the formal tier for why it is forced.

  4. Mask. Add −∞-\infty−∞ to positions a query is not allowed to see. In a decoder that is everything in the future; in an encoder, nothing.

  5. Normalise. Softmax each row, so the weights for one query sum to 1.

  6. Blend. Multiply the weights by VVV. Each output position is a weighted average of all value vectors.

Why multiple heads

Section titled “Why multiple heads”

One attention head produces one set of weights, so it can express one notion of relevance. But “relevant” is not one thing — a word might need to attend to its syntactic head, its coreferent, and the topic of the paragraph, all at once, and one softmax cannot do three things.

Multi-head attention runs hhh independent heads on lower-dimensional slices (dk=d/hd_k = d/hdk​=d/h) and concatenates. Total compute is roughly unchanged; expressive capacity is not. Interpretability work has found heads that do recognisable jobs — tracking previous occurrences of a token, attending to the immediately preceding word — though the tidy “one head, one function” story is much weaker than early papers suggested, and most heads are not cleanly interpretable at all.

What a block actually contains

Section titled “What a block actually contains”

A transformer block is attention plus a position-wise MLP, each wrapped in a residual connection and a normalisation:

Two things worth noticing:

The MLP holds most of the parameters. With the standard 4×4\times4× expansion, the MLP is roughly two-thirds of a block’s weights. Attention gets the attention; the MLP does much of the work, and interpretability research increasingly points at MLP layers as where factual knowledge lives.

The residual stream is the real object. Every block adds to a running vector rather than replacing it. The modern mental model is a shared bus that blocks read from and write to, which is why you can often ablate a block with surprisingly little damage.

Pre-norm vs post-norm is not cosmetic

The 2017 paper put the normalisation after the residual add. Training deep post-norm transformers requires a learning-rate warmup, and without it they diverge. Xiong et al. (2020) showed pre-norm keeps gradient magnitudes stable at initialisation and removes the need for warmup entirely. Every large model since is pre-norm. If you read the original paper and implement it literally, you will hit this.

What changed since 2017

Section titled “What changed since 2017”
  • Positions
  • KV cache & GQA
  • FlashAttention
  • Activations

Attention is permutation-equivariant — shuffle the input and the output shuffles identically. It has no idea what order anything is in. Position information has to be injected.

  • Sinusoidal / learned absolute (2017). Add a position vector to the input embedding. Simple; extrapolates poorly beyond trained lengths.
  • RoPE (2021). Rotate the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to position. Because a dot product between two rotated vectors depends only on the difference of the angles, attention becomes naturally relative. This is the current default in essentially every open LLM.
  • ALiBi (2021). Skip embeddings entirely; add a linear penalty to attention scores proportional to distance. Extrapolates to longer sequences than trained on, which was its selling point.

RoPE won on quality; ALiBi’s extrapolation argument was partly absorbed via RoPE scaling tricks.

At generation time, each new token re-attends to every previous token. Recomputing their keys and values every step would be quadratic waste, so they are cached.

The cache is the memory problem in deployment. Its size is

2⋅nlayers⋅nheads⋅dhead⋅ntokens⋅bytes2 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot n_{\text{heads}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot n_{\text{tokens}} \cdot \text{bytes}2⋅nlayers​⋅nheads​⋅dhead​⋅ntokens​⋅bytes

which for a large model at long context runs to tens of gigabytes — often more than the weights.

  • MQA (Shazeer, 2019). All heads share one key/value head. Cache shrinks by a factor of hhh. Quality drops noticeably.
  • GQA (2023). Group heads; each group shares a K/V head. A middle setting (say 8 groups for 64 heads) recovers most of the quality at most of the saving. This is the standard today.

The naive implementation materialises the full n×nn \times nn×n attention matrix in GPU high-bandwidth memory, writes it, reads it back for the softmax, writes again. For long sequences that traffic dominates runtime — the arithmetic is not the bottleneck, the memory movement is.

FlashAttention tiles the computation, keeps blocks in fast on-chip SRAM, and uses a streaming (online) softmax so it never needs the whole row at once. Memory goes from O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) to O(n)O(n)O(n) and wall-clock drops several-fold.

It computes exactly the same function. No approximation, no quality change. That is what makes it unusual: nearly every other efficiency technique on this page trades something away.

The 2017 MLP was Linear → ReLU → Linear. Modern models use a gated variant, usually SwiGLU:

SwiGLU(x)=(Swish(xW1)⊙xW2)W3.\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \big(\text{Swish}(xW_1) \odot xW_2\big)W_3 .SwiGLU(x)=(Swish(xW1​)⊙xW2​)W3​.

The elementwise product is a multiplicative gate: one branch decides how much of the other to let through. It reliably beats ReLU at equal parameter count, which is why the hidden dimension is usually scaled by 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ to keep the parameter count matched when comparing.

Shazeer’s paper is candid that there is no theory here — the abstract attributes the gains to “divine benevolence”. It is empirical, it replicates, and it is universal now.
Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

Scaled dot-product attention

Section titled “Scaled dot-product attention”
Attention⁡(Q,K,V)=softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk+M)V,\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V,Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​+M)V,

And a saturated softmax has vanishing gradient: for p=softmax⁡(z)p = \operatorname{softmax}(z)p=softmax(z) the Jacobian is diag⁡(p)−pp⊤\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\topdiag(p)−pp⊤, which goes to zero as ppp approaches one-hot. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​ restores unit variance and keeps the softmax in its responsive regime. The constant is forced by the algebra, not chosen.

Two readings people often miss:

Attention is not always the dominant cost. Comparing O(n2d)O(n^2 d)O(n2d) against O(nd2)O(n d^2)O(nd2), attention dominates only when n>dn > dn>d. For d=4096d = 4096d=4096 and n=2048n = 2048n=2048, the MLPs cost more. The quadratic term matters at long context, not universally.

The O(1)O(1)O(1) sequential depth is the real 2017 contribution. An RNN needs nnn sequential steps; a transformer needs one. That is what made training parallelisable across the sequence dimension, and parallelisability — not accuracy — is why transformers displaced RNNs.

RoPE, formally

Section titled “RoPE, formally”

Rotate qqq and kkk by an angle proportional to absolute position. For a two-dimensional slice and rotation matrix RθR_\thetaRθ​:

q~m=Rmθ qm,k~n=Rnθ kn.\tilde{q}_m = R_{m\theta}\, q_m, \qquad \tilde{k}_n = R_{n\theta}\, k_n .q~​m​=Rmθ​qm​,k~n​=Rnθ​kn​.

Then, using Ra⊤Rb=Rb−aR_a^\top R_b = R_{b-a}Ra⊤​Rb​=Rb−a​:

q~m⊤k~n=qm⊤Rmθ⊤Rnθkn=qm⊤R(n−m)θ kn.\tilde{q}_m^\top \tilde{k}_n = q_m^\top R_{m\theta}^\top R_{n\theta} k_n = q_m^\top R_{(n-m)\theta}\, k_n .q~​m⊤​k~n​=qm⊤​Rmθ⊤​Rnθ​kn​=qm⊤​R(n−m)θ​kn​.

The score depends only on the relative offset n−mn - mn−m. Absolute position is injected but cancels in the dot product — you get relative-position behaviour without a relative-position bias term, and without changing the shape of anything. That elegance is why it won.

The full scheme applies this to d/2d/2d/2 two-dimensional slices with geometrically spaced frequencies θi=10000−2i/d\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}θi​=10000−2i/d, giving each slice a different wavelength — fast-rotating slices resolve nearby positions, slow ones carry long-range order.

Scaling laws, and the correction

Section titled “Scaling laws, and the correction”

Kaplan et al. (2020) found test loss follows a power law in parameters NNN, data DDD, and compute CCC:

L(N)≈(NcN)αN,αN≈0.076.L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \qquad \alpha_N \approx 0.076 .L(N)≈(NNc​​)αN​,αN​≈0.076.

Their conclusion was that under a fixed compute budget you should mostly grow the model. Hoffmann et al. (2022) re-ran the analysis with a learning-rate schedule matched to each run’s length and got a materially different answer: parameters and tokens should scale roughly in proportion, about 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T tokens) beat Gopher (280B, 300B tokens) — a model four times smaller trained on nearly five times more data.

The methodological lesson generalises

The Kaplan/Chinchilla discrepancy came down to a confound in the experimental setup, not to a disagreement about the data. Fixed-schedule sweeps systematically penalised the longer runs. This is worth remembering whenever you read a scaling result: the fitted exponents are only as good as the training protocol held constant beneath them.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where things stand, as of 2026

Section titled “Where things stand, as of 2026”

The core is stable; the periphery is not. Attention itself is unchanged from 2017. What surrounds it — normalisation placement, position encoding, activation, KV-head sharing, kernel implementation — has turned over completely. If you learn transformers from the original paper alone, roughly half of what you learn is obsolete in the details while the central idea is exactly right.

State-space models are a real alternative, and have not displaced transformers. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) achieves linear-time sequence modelling with a selective state-space mechanism — the state update is input-dependent, which is what earlier SSMs like S4 lacked and what let Mamba close much of the quality gap on language. It matches or beats similarly sized transformers on several benchmarks with far better long-sequence scaling.

The honest read as of 2026: SSMs are strong, genuinely better on very long sequences, and weaker at exact retrieval from context. A transformer can attend directly to any past token; a fixed-size recurrent state cannot store everything, so tasks that require copying specific distant content favour attention. This is why the most convincing results are hybrids that interleave a few attention layers into an SSM stack — the attention layers handle recall, the SSM layers handle bulk sequence processing. Anyone claiming attention is obsolete is ahead of the evidence.

Memory bandwidth is the binding constraint, not FLOPs. FlashAttention’s success made this explicit and it has shaped design since: the useful question about a new technique is usually “how much does it move between memory tiers?”, not “how many multiplications?”.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Long context is unsolved, not solved. Models advertise million-token windows but retrieval quality degrades in the middle of long contexts, and the KV cache cost is brutal. The advertised number is a capacity, not a competence.

  • Nobody knows what most attention heads do. The tidy induction-head story covers a small fraction. Superposition — features sharing dimensions — means individual heads and neurons are usually not the right unit of analysis, which is what motivates sparse-autoencoder approaches to interpretability.

  • Position encoding remains unsatisfying. RoPE works but extrapolates poorly without ad-hoc scaling. There is no principled account of how positional information should be represented.

  • The MLP is under-theorised. It holds most parameters and most knowledge, and attracts a small fraction of the research attention. Why the 4×4\times4× expansion? Why gating helps? Both open.
