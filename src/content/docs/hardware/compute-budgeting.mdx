<Abstract>
"How much compute do I need?" has an arithmetic answer, and the arithmetic is not
hard. Three numbers — parameters, tokens, and the utilisation you can realistically
achieve — determine GPU-hours, and GPU-hours times a rental rate determines
dollars. A fourth number, memory, determines whether the job runs at all. This
page gives you the formulas, their derivations, the places where they lie, and
several fully worked examples you can adapt.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens.** The factor 6 is 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward, and it is derivable in two lines rather than remembered.
- **Memory is a different budget from compute, and it is usually what stops you first.** Full fine-tuning with Adam in mixed precision costs about 16 bytes per parameter before you have stored a single activation. A 7B model needs ~112 GB of optimizer-side state — more than any single GPU has.
- **Model FLOPs utilisation (MFU) is the honest efficiency number.** Well-tuned large-scale training reaches 35–55%. Plan with 40%, not with peak. Using peak makes every estimate wrong by a factor of two to three.
- **Chinchilla's ~20 tokens per parameter is a training-cost optimum, not a deployment optimum.** If you will serve the model heavily, the total-cost optimum is a smaller model trained on far more tokens — which is why released models are systematically "over-trained" relative to Chinchilla.
- **For inference sizing, the KV cache is the number that surprises people.** At long context and moderate batch it can exceed the weights, and it grows linearly in both batch and sequence length.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The whole calculation, on one page

Before any derivation, here is the shape of the answer.

<Steps>

1. **Count the work.** Training a model with $N$ parameters on $D$ tokens costs about $6ND$ floating-point operations. That single formula does most of the job.

2. **Divide by what your hardware actually delivers.** Not its peak. Take peak dense FLOP/s for your precision and multiply by a realistic utilisation — 40% is a defensible planning figure for a well-engineered run, and 20–30% is common for a first attempt.

3. **That gives you GPU-seconds.** Divide by the number of GPUs for wall-clock, multiply by the hourly rate for dollars.

4. **Separately, check that it fits.** Compute tells you how long; memory tells you whether it runs. Memory is the constraint that stops most projects, and it is the one people check last.

</Steps>

A concrete instance, carried through: a 7B model on 1 trillion tokens is
$6 \times 7\times10^9 \times 10^{12} = 4.2 \times 10^{22}$ FLOPs. On 64 H100s at
40% MFU that is $4.2\times10^{22} / (64 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.4)$
≈ 1.66 million seconds ≈ **19 days**, and at roughly $3.50 per GPU-hour, about
**$102,000**.

Every number in that sentence is one you can compute yourself in a minute. The
rest of this page explains where each comes from and where it goes wrong.

## Why the factor is 6

This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because the same reasoning
tells you how to modify it.

Consider one parameter in one matrix multiplication, and one token flowing
through it.

- **Forward:** the parameter is multiplied by an input and the result accumulated. One multiply, one add → **2 FLOPs**.
- **Backward, gradient with respect to the input:** the parameter is used again in a multiply-accumulate → **2 FLOPs**.
- **Backward, gradient with respect to the parameter:** another multiply-accumulate → **2 FLOPs**.

Total **6 FLOPs per parameter per token**. Multiply by $N$ parameters and $D$
tokens and you have $C \approx 6ND$.

<Aside type="tip" title="The immediate corollaries">
Because the derivation is structural, the modifications follow directly:

- **Forward only** (inference prefill, or evaluating a frozen model): $2ND$, one third the cost.
- **Frozen backbone with a small trainable head** (linear probing, LoRA): the frozen parameters cost 2 FLOPs each for the forward and 2 more for passing gradient through them, but not the third 2 for their own gradient. So roughly $4ND_{\text{frozen}} + 6ND_{\text{trainable}}$, and since the trainable fraction is tiny, the saving is about a third — not the 100× that "we only train 0.1% of parameters" suggests. **LoRA saves memory, not compute.** This is the single most common misconception about parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
- **Gradient checkpointing** recomputes the forward pass during the backward, adding roughly $2ND$: total $8ND$, a 33% compute increase to buy a large memory reduction.
</Aside>

## Compute and memory are different budgets

People conflate these constantly, so state it plainly.

**Compute** determines how long a job takes. It scales with parameters × tokens
and is spread across however many GPUs you have. Throw more GPUs at it and it
finishes sooner.

**Memory** determines whether the job runs at all. It scales with parameters ×
bytes-per-parameter, plus activations. Throwing more GPUs at it only helps if you
use a parallelism strategy that actually shards the state — which is exactly what
[ZeRO and FSDP](/systems/distributed-training/) are for.

## FLOPs, with the attention correction

The $6ND$ estimate counts the parameter-weighted matrix multiplications. It omits
the attention score computation, which involves no parameters but real work.

Per token, per layer, self-attention over a context of length $s$ costs about
$4sd$ forward FLOPs — $2sd$ to form the scores $QK^\top$ against $s$ previous
positions, and $2sd$ to apply them to $V$, where $d$ is the model width. Applying
the same 3× factor for the backward pass and summing over $L$ layers and $D$
tokens:

$$
C \approx 6ND + 12LsdD.
$$

To see when the second term matters, take the ratio, and use the fact that a
standard transformer has roughly $N \approx 12Ld^2$ parameters:

$$
\frac{12LsdD}{6ND} = \frac{2Ls d}{N} \approx \frac{2Lsd}{12Ld^{2}} = \frac{s}{6d}.
$$

So the attention correction is about $s / 6d$. For a 70B model with $d = 8192$ at
$s = 4096$ that is 8% — noticeable, ignorable for planning. At $s = 128{,}000$ it
is 2.6×, so attention costs more than everything else combined and $6ND$ is badly
wrong. Causal masking halves both terms of the attention count in practice, so
treat this as an upper bound.

<Aside type="caution" title="When 6ND stops being trustworthy">
Four regimes where the shortcut breaks:

- **Long context.** As above. Past roughly $s \approx 6d$, attention FLOPs rival parameter FLOPs, then exceed them.
- **Mixture of experts.** $N$ must be the *active* parameters per token, not the total. A model with 400B total and 30B active costs $6 \times 30\text{B} \times D$ for compute, but needs memory for all 400B. See [Mixture of Experts](/architectures/mixture-of-experts/).
- **Small models.** Embedding and output-projection FLOPs, and the non-matmul work, are a larger fraction. $6ND$ can underestimate by 20% or more below ~1B parameters.
- **Gradient checkpointing.** Add roughly a third, as derived above.
</Aside>

## Memory accounting, item by item

Four categories, and you must budget all four.

**1. Parameters.** $N \times b_w$ where $b_w$ is bytes per weight. BF16: 2.

**2. Gradients.** Same shape as parameters. Usually BF16 (2 bytes), sometimes
accumulated in FP32 (4).

**3. Optimizer state.** The recipe that dominates practice is Adam with an FP32
master copy: 4 bytes for the master weights, 4 for the first moment, 4 for the
second. **12 bytes per parameter.**

Adding it up for standard mixed-precision Adam: $2 + 2 + 12 = 16$ bytes per
parameter. This is the ZeRO paper's accounting and it is the number to carry
around.

**4. Activations.** Everything saved during the forward pass for use in the
backward. Unlike the first three, this scales with batch size and sequence
length, not just with the model. Korthikanti et al. give, for a standard
transformer layer without any recomputation:

$$
M_{\text{act}} = s\,b\,h\,L\left(34 + 5\,\frac{a\,s}{h}\right) \text{ bytes},
$$

with $s$ sequence length, $b$ microbatch, $h$ hidden size, $L$ layers, $a$
attention heads. The $34$ term is linear in sequence length; the $5as/h$ term is
quadratic, and comes from materialising attention scores.

<Aside type="note" title="Why the quadratic term is mostly gone now">
That $5as/h$ term assumed attention scores are written to memory.
[FlashAttention](/architectures/transformers/) does not write them, so in any
modern stack the quadratic term largely disappears and activation memory is
approximately linear in $s$. Keep the formula for intuition about *why*
long-context training used to be impossible, but do not use it to size a 2026
training run — measure instead.
</Aside>

## MFU: the number that makes estimates honest

Model FLOPs Utilisation, introduced with PaLM, is

$$
\text{MFU} = \frac{\text{model FLOPs per second actually achieved}}{\text{peak FLOP/s of the hardware}},
$$

where "model FLOPs" means the $6ND$ work the model logically requires — *not*
counting recomputation. (The variant that does count recomputation is **hardware**
FLOPs utilisation, HFU, and it is always the larger number. Papers are not always
clear about which they report; if a figure looks surprisingly good, check.)

| Setting | Typical MFU |
|---|---|
| Large-scale pretraining, well-tuned, dense transformer | 40–55% |
| Same, first attempt without tuning | 15–30% |
| Long-context training | lower — attention is less efficient |
| MoE training | typically lower — routing and expert imbalance |
| Fine-tuning small models on few GPUs | often 20–35% |
| Inference, batch-1 decode | ~1% and this is expected, not a failure |

<Aside type="tip" title="Plan with 40%, report what you measure">
The single most common estimation error is dividing by peak FLOPs. It makes every
timeline optimistic by a factor of two to three and it is the reason
"we'll train it over the weekend" becomes a fortnight. Use 40% for planning a
dense pretraining run on good hardware, 25% if you have not tuned anything, and
then measure your actual MFU in the first hour and re-plan.
</Aside>

## The wall-clock and cost formulas

Put it together. With $n$ accelerators of peak throughput $X$ FLOP/s at
utilisation $u$:

$$
T = \frac{6ND}{n \, X \, u}, \qquad \text{Cost} = T \cdot n \cdot p,
$$

with $p$ the price per accelerator-hour (and $T$ converted to hours).

Note that cost is independent of $n$: $T \cdot n = 6ND/(Xu)$. **Doubling the
cluster halves the time at the same price** — in the ideal case. In practice $u$
falls as $n$ grows, because communication grows and stragglers appear, so scaling
out costs more in total. That degradation is the real subject of [Distributed
Training](/systems/distributed-training/).

## Chinchilla, stated properly

Hoffmann et al. fit the loss as a function of parameters and tokens:

$$
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},
$$

with fitted values around $A \approx 406.4$, $B \approx 410.7$,
$E \approx 1.69$, $\alpha \approx 0.34$, $\beta \approx 0.28$.

Minimise $L$ subject to the compute constraint $C = 6ND$. Forming the Lagrangian
and setting derivatives to zero gives

$$
N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{b},
$$

with $a = \beta/(\alpha+\beta) \approx 0.46$ and $b = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)
\approx 0.54$. Because $a \approx b \approx 0.5$, the practical rule is that
**parameters and tokens should scale roughly together**, giving the famous
$D \approx 20N$.

<Aside type="caution" title="Three things Chinchilla is not">
1. **Not a deployment optimum.** It minimises the loss achievable for a fixed *training* budget. It says nothing about serving cost, and serving cost scales with $N$ forever.

2. **Not exactly 20.** The ratio depends on the fitted exponents, which depend on the architecture, the data and the tokenizer. It has been re-estimated several times with somewhat different results, and a re-analysis found the original fit's confidence intervals were understated. Treat 20 as an order-of-magnitude anchor.

3. **Not applicable to a fixed target quality.** If you need a specific capability rather than the best loss for a budget, the constrained-optimisation framing does not apply.
</Aside>

## Inference-aware sizing

Sardana et al. make the correction that matters commercially. If you will serve
$D_{\text{inf}}$ tokens over the model's lifetime, total cost is

$$
C_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{6ND_{\text{train}}}_{\text{training}} + \underbrace{2ND_{\text{inf}}}_{\text{inference}},
$$

and minimising *this* subject to a fixed target loss gives a smaller $N$ trained
on more tokens than Chinchilla prescribes.

The intuition is simple: training compute is paid once and scales as $6ND$;
inference compute is paid per served token and scales as $2N$ per token, forever.
Once $D_{\text{inf}}$ is large, the $2ND_{\text{inf}}$ term dominates everything
and shrinking $N$ is worth almost any amount of extra training.

This is why every released open-weights model is "over-trained" relative to
Chinchilla — Llama-3-8B saw roughly 15 trillion tokens against a Chinchilla
prescription of about 160 billion, nearly 100× more. That was not a mistake. It
was the correct answer to a different, better-posed question.

## KV cache, the inference memory formula

For autoregressive decoding you cache keys and values for every past position:

$$
M_{\text{KV}} = 2 \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot s \cdot b \cdot b_{\text{bytes}},
$$

with the leading 2 for keys and values, $n_{\text{kv}}$ the number of key-value
heads (equal to the number of query heads under plain MHA, far fewer under
[GQA](/systems/inference/)), $s$ the sequence length and $b$ the batch.

**Worked:** Llama-3-70B has $L = 80$, 8 KV heads, $d_{\text{head}} = 128$. In
FP16 at $s = 8192$ and $b = 32$:

$$
2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 8192 \times 32 \times 2 = 85.9 \text{ GB}.
$$

The weights are 140 GB. The cache is 86 GB and grows linearly in both $b$ and
$s$ — double the batch or double the context and it exceeds the model. This is
the constraint that PagedAttention and GQA exist to manage, and it is why context
length is priced.

Without GQA — 64 KV heads instead of 8 — the same configuration would need 687 GB.
GQA is not a minor efficiency tweak; it is what makes long-context serving
economically possible at all.

## Worked examples

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="LoRA on a 7B">

**Goal:** adapt a 7B model on 50 million tokens of domain data.

**Compute.** Frozen base costs ~4 FLOPs per parameter per token, adapters
negligible: $\approx 4 \times 7\times10^9 \times 5\times10^7 = 1.4\times10^{18}$
FLOPs.

**Hardware.** One H100 at ~30% MFU: $990\times10^{12} \times 0.3 = 2.97\times10^{14}$
FLOP/s.

**Time.** $1.4\times10^{18} / 2.97\times10^{14} \approx 4{,}700$ s ≈ **1.3 hours**.

**Memory.** 4-bit base ≈ 3.5 GB, adapters and their optimizer state ≈ 0.2 GB,
activations at modest batch and 2k context a few GB. Comfortable on a 24 GB
consumer card; trivial on an 80 GB H100.

**Cost.** Under $10. This is the regime where you should stop optimising and just
run it — and where renting is obviously correct.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Full fine-tune of a 7B">

**Goal:** full-parameter fine-tune, 7B, 50M tokens.

**Compute.** $6 \times 7\times10^9 \times 5\times10^7 = 2.1\times10^{18}$ FLOPs —
only 1.5× the LoRA run.

**Memory.** This is the actual problem. Mixed-precision Adam: $16 \times
7\times10^9 = 112$ GB of persistent state. Plus activations. **Does not fit on one
80 GB GPU.**

Options, in increasing order of disruption: 8-bit Adam (~70 GB, fits but leaves
almost nothing for activations); gradient checkpointing to cut activations at 33%
more compute; ZeRO-2 or FSDP across 2–4 GPUs to shard optimizer state and
gradients; CPU offload of optimizer state, which works but is slow.

**Realistic plan.** 4× H100 with FSDP. Compute time on 4 GPUs at 35% MFU:
$2.1\times10^{18} / (4 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.35) \approx 1{,}500$ s.
Call it an hour with data loading and checkpointing. About **$15**.

**The lesson.** Full fine-tuning is barely more compute than LoRA and vastly more
memory. If you have the memory, the compute argument for LoRA is weak; if you do
not, LoRA is not an optimisation but an enabler.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Pretrain a 7B, Chinchilla-style">

**Goal:** pretrain 7B from scratch.

**Tokens.** Chinchilla-optimal: $20 \times 7\times10^9 = 140$ billion. But per the
inference-aware argument, if you intend to serve it, train longer — 1–2 trillion
is the modern practice.

**Compute at 1T tokens.** $6 \times 7\times10^9 \times 10^{12} = 4.2\times10^{22}$
FLOPs.

**Time on 64 H100s at 40% MFU.** $4.2\times10^{22} / (64 \times 990\times10^{12}
\times 0.4) \approx 1.66\times10^{6}$ s ≈ **19.2 days**.

**Cost.** 64 GPUs × 461 hours × $3.50/GPU-hour ≈ **$103,000** at neocloud rates;
roughly $200k–300k on a hyperscaler.

**Sanity check the other way.** At 140B tokens (strict Chinchilla) it is 2.7 days
and ~$14,000 — and you get a noticeably weaker model. That gap is the price of
the inference-aware correction, and for anything you plan to deploy it is worth
paying.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Pretrain a 70B">

**Goal:** 70B parameters, 15 trillion tokens — roughly the Llama-3-70B recipe.

**Compute.** $6 \times 7\times10^{10} \times 1.5\times10^{13} = 6.3\times10^{24}$
FLOPs.

**Time on 1,024 H100s at 45% MFU.** $6.3\times10^{24} / (1024 \times
990\times10^{12} \times 0.45) \approx 1.38\times10^{7}$ s ≈ **160 days**.

**Cost.** 1,024 GPUs × 3,836 hours × $3.00/GPU-hour ≈ **$11.8 million** in compute
alone. Add data acquisition and cleaning, failed runs — plan for two to
three times the ideal figure — staff, and storage.

**Memory.** 70B × 16 bytes = 1.12 TB of optimizer state. Requires sharding across
at least 16–32 GPUs before considering activations, so tensor + pipeline + data
parallelism together, not a single strategy.

**The honest framing.** This is the scale where the compute estimate becomes the
easy part. Node failures at 1,024 GPUs are a weekly event, checkpoint-and-restart
overhead is material, and the difference between a good run and a bad one is
measured in months of wall clock.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Scaling-law fits are less settled than the citation count suggests.** The
Chinchilla coefficients have been re-derived by independent groups with
meaningfully different exponents, and a reproduction found the original paper's
uncertainty intervals were too narrow. The qualitative conclusion — parameters and
data should scale together, and GPT-3 was badly under-trained — is robust. The
specific number 20 is not.

**Compute-optimal has been largely abandoned in favour of inference-optimal.**
Every major open-weights release trains far past Chinchilla, for the reason given
above. The interesting open question is where the returns actually stop: models
continue improving at 100× the Chinchilla token count, and no one has published a
convincing account of the asymptote.

**MFU has stopped improving.** Reported figures for large dense transformer
pretraining have sat in the 40–55% band across several hardware generations.
Faster chips have not translated into better utilisation, because communication
and memory have not kept pace with arithmetic. This is the same memory-wall story
from the [accelerators page](/hardware/accelerators/) viewed from the software
side.

**MoE complicates every formula here.** With sparse activation, compute scales
with active parameters and memory with total parameters, which decouples two
quantities that all the classical scaling work treats as one. Scaling laws for
MoE exist but are less mature, and the effective-parameter-count question does not
have a settled answer.

**Reasoning models moved the budget to inference.** When a model spends thousands
of tokens of chain-of-thought per query, the $2ND_{\text{inf}}$ term grows by
whatever the average trace length is. The whole training/inference balance shifts,
and the inference-aware scaling analyses have not caught up.

## Open problems

<Steps>

1. **Predicting downstream capability from loss.** Scaling laws predict validation loss well. They predict whether a model can do a task poorly. Emergence claims are contested — much of the apparent discontinuity comes from discontinuous metrics — but the mapping from loss to usefulness remains largely empirical.

2. **Data-constrained scaling.** High-quality text is finite. What the right substitution rate is between repeated data, synthetic data and more parameters is an active and unresolved question.

3. **Scaling laws for post-training.** RLHF, distillation and reasoning training have no compute-optimal theory comparable to Chinchilla's, despite consuming a large and growing share of budgets.

4. **The utilisation gap.** Roughly half of purchased FLOPs are lost. No one has a principled account of how much of that is irreducible.

5. **Honest total cost of ownership.** Published costs are almost always the successful run only. Failed runs, data pipeline compute, evaluation and the human time to babysit a 160-day job routinely double or triple the figure, and nobody reports them.

</Steps>

## Practical guidance

- **Compute the estimate before you provision.** Five minutes of arithmetic routinely reveals that a plan is off by an order of magnitude, in either direction.
- **Check memory first, compute second.** Compute makes a job slow. Memory makes it impossible.
- **Measure MFU in the first hour of any long run.** If it is below 20%, stop and fix it — you will save more than any subsequent optimisation.
- **Budget 2–3× the ideal figure.** Failed runs, restarts, hyperparameter searches and data pipeline compute are not optional extras; they are most of the difference between the estimate and the invoice.
- **For anything under a few thousand GPU-hours, rent.** The break-even against owning hardware sits far above where most projects live — see [Choosing Hardware](/hardware/choosing-hardware/).
- **Do not size a serving deployment on weights alone.** Add the KV cache at your actual target batch and context, and it will often be the larger number.
