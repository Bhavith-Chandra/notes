<Abstract>
Almost everyone shopping for AI hardware compares the wrong number. Peak FLOPs is
the figure on the box, but the overwhelming majority of deep-learning kernels
finish long before the arithmetic units are saturated, because they are waiting
on memory. This page builds the mental model that makes hardware decisions
predictable: a small number of bytes-per-second and FLOPs-per-second figures, one
ratio that combines them, and a set of honest caveats about how vendors report
all three.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Bandwidth, not FLOPs, is the scarce resource.** An H100 can do about 990 dense BF16 TFLOP/s and read about 3.35 TB/s. That is roughly 300 FLOPs per byte. Any kernel doing less arithmetic per byte than that is memory-bound, and most are.
- **Arithmetic intensity is the one number to compute.** FLOPs divided by bytes moved. Compare it to the machine's ratio and you know immediately whether more compute or more bandwidth would help.
- **Capacity decides what you can run; bandwidth decides how fast.** These are different constraints and people routinely conflate them. 141 GB of HBM3e does not make an H200 faster at training — it makes larger batches and longer contexts possible, which then makes it faster.
- **Vendor spec sheets are not comparable as printed.** NVIDIA's H100 table prints only 2:4-sparse numbers; AMD's platform brochures print 8-GPU sparse aggregates; GeForce cards halve their tensor throughput under the FP32 accumulation that PyTorch actually uses. Halve, divide, and check the footnote before comparing anything.
- **Lower precision is a bandwidth optimisation that happens to also be a compute optimisation.** FP8 weights move half the bytes of BF16 and, on hardware with an FP8 path, run twice as fast. The second effect gets the attention; the first is usually the one that matters.

</KeyPoints>

## Start with a kitchen

A GPU is a kitchen with an enormous number of cooks and one narrow doorway to the
pantry.

The cooks are the arithmetic units. There are tens of thousands of them and they
are extremely fast. The pantry is high-bandwidth memory (HBM), which holds your
model weights and activations. The doorway is the memory bus.

Now the question that decides everything: **for each trip to the pantry, how much
cooking gets done?**

If a cook fetches one ingredient and performs one operation with it, the kitchen
is limited by the doorway. Adding cooks changes nothing. If a cook fetches one
ingredient and performs three hundred operations with it, the kitchen is limited
by the cooks, and a wider doorway changes nothing.

Deep learning contains both kinds of work, and knowing which kind you are doing
is most of what hardware intuition is.

## The one ratio

Take any piece of work. Count the floating-point operations it performs. Count the
bytes it must move from HBM. Divide.

That is **arithmetic intensity**, and it has units of FLOPs per byte.

Every machine has its own ratio: peak FLOP/s divided by peak bytes/s. For an H100
in BF16 that is roughly 989 × 10¹² ÷ 3.35 × 10¹² ≈ **295 FLOPs per byte**.

Now compare:

- Your kernel's intensity is **above** the machine's ratio → you are compute-bound. Buy more FLOPs, or use lower precision to get more FLOPs.
- Your kernel's intensity is **below** it → you are memory-bound. Buy more bandwidth, or restructure the computation to move fewer bytes. Adding FLOPs does nothing at all.

<Aside type="caution" title="The number that surprises people">
Generating one token from a 70B model at batch size 1 has an arithmetic intensity
of about **2 FLOPs per byte**. The machine wants 295. You are using roughly 0.7%
of the GPU's arithmetic capability, and no amount of extra compute will help.

This is not a bug in your code. It is the structure of the problem, and it is why
[inference](/systems/inference/) is a separate engineering discipline from
training.
</Aside>

## Why matrix multiplication is special

Deep learning runs on GPUs for one structural reason: matrix multiplication has
arithmetic intensity that grows with size.

Multiply two $n \times n$ matrices. You move $3n^2$ elements and perform $2n^3$
operations. Intensity is $2n^3 / (3n^2 \cdot \text{bytes}) \propto n$. Make the
matrices bigger and each byte does more work.

Contrast an elementwise operation — adding a bias, applying GELU. You read an
element, do one or two operations, write it back. Intensity is a small constant,
independent of size, and always far below the machine's ratio. Those kernels are
*always* memory-bound, which is why compilers work so hard to fuse them into the
matmul that produced their input.

<Aside type="tip" title="What this predicts, correctly">
Three things follow immediately, and all three are true in practice:

1. **Bigger batches are more efficient** — until you run out of memory, because they raise intensity.
2. **Small models on big GPUs waste the GPU** — the matrices are too small to reach the machine's ratio.
3. **Attention was slow for reasons that had nothing to do with FLOPs** — the $n^2$ score matrix was being written to and read from HBM. FlashAttention did not reduce the arithmetic; it kept the tiles on chip. Same FLOPs, several times faster.
</Aside>

## What is physically on the die

An NVIDIA datacenter GPU is a grid of **streaming multiprocessors** (SMs) — 132 on
an H100 SXM. Each SM contains:

- **CUDA cores** — general-purpose FP32/INT32 lanes. These do the elementwise work, the reductions, the indexing.
- **Tensor cores** — fixed-function units that consume small matrix tiles and emit a matrix product. This is where essentially all the advertised FLOPs come from. On an H100, tensor cores provide roughly 15× the BF16 throughput of the CUDA cores.
- **Shared memory / L1** — 228 KB per SM, software-managed. The staging area for tiling.
- **Register file** — 256 KB per SM, the fastest storage on the chip.

Threads are grouped into **warps** of 32 that execute in lockstep. Warps are
grouped into **thread blocks** which are resident on one SM and can share its
shared memory. This is why tile sizes in high-performance kernels are always
multiples of awkward numbers: they are chosen so that a tile fits in shared
memory and the warps map cleanly onto the tensor-core instruction shape.

<Aside type="note" title="The practical consequence for model design">
Tensor cores operate on fixed tile shapes, so dimensions that are not multiples of
those shapes leave lanes idle. Anthony et al. quantify this: hidden sizes and
vocabulary sizes divisible by 64 (better, 128) can be substantially faster than
nearby values that are not. Choosing a hidden size of 4096 rather than 4000 is not
superstition — it is alignment with the hardware's tile geometry.
</Aside>

The roofline model, from Williams, Waterman and Patterson, plots achievable
performance against arithmetic intensity. It is two straight lines on a log-log
plot, and it is the single most useful diagram in performance engineering.

## Precision, and what each format buys

Numeric format changes three things at once: bytes moved, arithmetic throughput,
and numerical behaviour. Conflating them causes most of the confusion.

| Format | Bits | Exponent / mantissa | Dynamic range | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP32 | 32 | 8 / 23 | ~10³⁸ | Master weights, optimizer state, loss scaling |
| TF32 | 19 stored as 32 | 8 / 10 | same as FP32 | Drop-in matmul acceleration on Ampere+ |
| FP16 | 16 | 5 / 10 | ~10⁵ | Older mixed precision; needs loss scaling |
| BF16 | 16 | 8 / 7 | same as FP32 | The default for training since Ampere |
| FP8 (E4M3) | 8 | 4 / 3 | ~10² | Forward activations and weights |
| FP8 (E5M2) | 8 | 5 / 2 | ~10⁴ | Gradients, which need range more than precision |
| FP4 / NVFP4 | 4 | block-scaled | tiny | Inference, and increasingly pretraining |

<Aside type="tip" title="Why BF16 beat FP16 despite being less precise">
BF16 has *fewer* mantissa bits than FP16 — 7 against 10 — so it represents each
number less precisely. It won anyway, because it keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits and
therefore FP32's dynamic range.

Training failures at low precision are overwhelmingly *range* failures: a gradient
underflows to zero or an activation overflows to infinity. FP16 training needs
loss scaling — multiplying the loss by a large constant to drag gradients into
representable range — and that machinery is fiddly and can diverge. BF16 needs
none of it, because anything that fits in FP32's range fits in BF16's.

Precision you can average away over a batch. Range you cannot. That is the whole
argument, and it generalises: the FP8 pair splits along exactly the same line,
with E5M2 (more range) reserved for gradients and E4M3 (more precision) for
forward values.
</Aside>

## Arithmetic intensity, precisely

For a kernel that performs $W$ floating-point operations and moves $Q$ bytes
between HBM and the chip, the arithmetic intensity is

$$
I = \frac{W}{Q}.
$$

Let $\pi$ be the machine's peak throughput in FLOP/s and $\beta$ its peak memory
bandwidth in bytes/s. Attainable performance is

$$
P = \min(\pi,\; \beta \cdot I).
$$

The **machine balance** is $I^\star = \pi / \beta$, the intensity at which the two
terms are equal. Below it you are bandwidth-limited; above it, compute-limited.

<Aside type="caution" title="What the roofline deliberately ignores">
This model assumes bandwidth and compute are each independently saturable and
perfectly overlapped. Reality intrudes in at least four ways:

- **Latency, not just bandwidth.** With too few concurrent memory requests in flight you cannot reach $\beta$ at all, regardless of intensity. Occupancy exists to hide latency.
- **Caches.** $Q$ is HBM traffic, so a kernel that hits in L2 has a much better effective intensity than a naive count suggests. This is exactly the lever tiling pulls.
- **Power and clocks.** Sustained heavy tensor-core work throttles clocks below the boost frequency the peak number assumes. Achieving 100% of a printed peak is not merely difficult; it is often thermally impossible.
- **Kernel launch and synchronisation.** Small operations spend more time being scheduled than executing.

The roofline is a bound and a diagnostic, not a prediction. Its value is that when
you measure far below the roof, it tells you *which* roof you are under.
</Aside>

## A worked intensity calculation

Take a linear layer $Y = XW$ with $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d}$ and
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, in BF16 (2 bytes).

**FLOPs:** $2Bd^2$ — one multiply and one add per element of the inner product.

**Bytes:** read $X$ ($2Bd$), read $W$ ($2d^2$), write $Y$ ($2Bd$), so
$Q = 4Bd + 2d^2$.

$$
I = \frac{2Bd^2}{4Bd + 2d^2} = \frac{Bd}{2B + d}.
$$

Two regimes fall straight out.

**Large batch, $B \gg d$:** the $d$ term in the denominator vanishes and
$I \to d/2$. With $d = 4096$ that is 2048 FLOPs/byte, comfortably compute-bound.
The weight read is amortised across the whole batch.

**Batch of one, $B = 1$:** $I = d/(2 + d) \to 1$. You read the entire weight
matrix to perform a single vector-matrix product. One FLOP per byte, against a
machine that wants 295.

That ratio — $d/2$ against $1$ — is the entire quantitative case for batching, and
the reason a serving system's throughput and its latency pull in opposite
directions.

## Where the FLOPs come from

A tensor core computes a small matrix product per instruction. On Hopper the
relevant instruction family operates on tiles of shape $64 \times N \times 16$ per
warpgroup. Peak throughput is

$$
\pi = (\text{SMs}) \times (\text{tensor cores per SM}) \times (\text{FLOPs per core-cycle}) \times f_{\text{clock}}.
$$

For H100 SXM: 132 SMs × 4 tensor cores × 1024 BF16 FLOPs per cycle × 1.755 GHz
≈ 949 TFLOP/s, which lands within a couple of percent of the published 989 —
the gap is clock-boost accounting.

The point of doing this arithmetic once is that it makes the structure visible:
peak FLOPs is a product of four numbers, and vendors improve it by increasing any
of them. A generational "2× FLOPs" claim may be twice the tensor cores, or the
same cores at half the precision, and those two have very different implications
for your workload.

<Aside type="caution" title="Reading a spec sheet without being fooled">
Five traps, each of which has produced widely repeated wrong numbers:

1. **Sparse vs dense.** NVIDIA's datacenter tables often print 2:4-structured-sparse figures; dense is exactly half. NVIDIA's H100 page prints only sparse, while the A100 page prints both — so a naive H100/A100 comparison overstates the gap by 2×.
2. **Per-package vs per-GPU.** A GB200 superchip contains two B200 GPUs. Superchip tables must be halved before comparing to a single GPU.
3. **Platform vs chip.** AMD's MI355X *platform* brochure headlines an 8-GPU sparse aggregate. Divide by 8, then by 2.
4. **Accumulation precision on consumer cards.** GeForce parts run FP16 tensor ops with FP32 accumulation at exactly half rate. PyTorch and cuBLAS default to FP32 accumulation, so an RTX 4090 delivers roughly 165 dense TFLOP/s in practice, not the 330 implied by the FP16-accumulate figure.
5. **SKU divergence under one name.** "B200" denotes both the HGX part (180 GB, 7.7 TB/s, 1000 W) and the GB200-rack part (186 GB, 8 TB/s, 1200 W). The widely quoted "192 GB" appears in no current NVIDIA datasheet.
</Aside>

Read the columns as answering different questions. **Memory capacity** determines
what fits. **Bandwidth** determines decode speed and how badly memory-bound
kernels hurt. **Dense FLOPs** determines training throughput once you are large
enough to be compute-bound. **Scale-up bandwidth** determines how far you can
push tensor parallelism before communication dominates.

Note what the table shows about generational progress: from H100 to B200,
bandwidth rose 2.3× while dense BF16 rose 2.3×. The ratio barely moved. Hardware
is not escaping the memory wall; it is holding position on it.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**HBM is the binding constraint on the whole industry.** Capacity and bandwidth
per package have grown far more slowly than transistor budgets, and HBM supply —
not fab capacity for the logic die — has been the practical limiter on
accelerator volume. HBM4 arrives with Rubin and MI455X at roughly 22–23 TB/s per
package, a real step, but memory has been losing ground to compute for two
decades and one generation does not reverse that.

**The unit of purchase has shifted from chip to rack.** GB200 NVL72 puts 72 GPUs
in a single NVLink domain at 130 TB/s aggregate. The product is not a faster GPU;
it is a larger region within which the node boundary does not exist. That changes
which parallelism strategies are viable — see [Distributed
Training](/systems/distributed-training/) — and it is the main reason
rack-scale systems command the premium they do.

**Precision is still descending.** FP8 training is now routine; FP4 pretraining is
being reported and the hardware paths exist on Blackwell and MI355X. Each step
halves bytes moved and doubles nominal throughput, which is the only lever that
has been reliably available. How far it goes is unresolved: the error introduced
by block-scaled 4-bit formats is not uniformly benign across architectures, and
the reported successes involve careful mixed-precision recipes rather than a flat
format switch.

**Alternatives are real but narrow.** TPUs are genuinely competitive on
throughput-per-dollar for workloads that fit the XLA compilation model, and
Google's pod-scale optical interconnect is a distinct architectural bet.
Trainium's cost position is strong. But CUDA's software depth remains the
dominant practical fact, and "it benchmarks well" and "your existing code runs on
it" are different claims.

## Open problems

<Steps>

1. **The memory wall.** Compute per package has grown far faster than bandwidth per package for twenty years. Processing-in-memory and 3D-stacked logic are proposed answers; neither has shipped at scale for training.

2. **Numerical stability at 4 bits and below.** Block scaling, stochastic rounding and mixed-precision recipes make it work, but there is no theory that predicts which architectures and which optimisers tolerate a given format. Practice is empirical.

3. **Utilisation.** Well-tuned large-scale pretraining reaches roughly 35–55% model FLOPs utilisation. The remaining half is lost to communication, bubbles, memory stalls and imperfect kernels. Recovering it is worth as much as a hardware generation and is far less well funded.

4. **Power and siting.** A GB200 NVL72 rack draws well over 100 kW and requires liquid cooling. Grid interconnect timelines, not chip supply, increasingly gate deployment — which makes performance per watt a business constraint, not an environmental footnote.

5. **Benchmarks that mean anything.** MLPerf is the serious attempt, but peak-FLOPs marketing continues to drive purchasing. There is no widely accepted way to compare accelerators on a workload you actually care about without running it.

</Steps>

## Practical guidance

- **Compute arithmetic intensity before buying anything.** It takes five minutes and tells you whether bandwidth or FLOPs is your constraint. Buying the wrong one is expensive and common.
- **Size on memory first.** Ask what fits, then ask how fast. A workload that does not fit runs at zero.
- **Profile before optimising.** Nsight Compute or PyTorch's profiler will tell you your achieved bandwidth and occupancy. Guessing which kernel is slow has a poor hit rate.
- **Halve every sparse number you see, and check the footnote.** If a comparison looks too favourable, it usually involves two different footnote conventions.
- **Treat rental pricing as the real currency.** As of August 2026, per-GPU-hour on-demand rates cluster around $3.3–4.0 for H100, $4.5–6.0 for H200 and $6.7–8.6 for B200 across the neoclouds, with hyperscalers roughly 1.5–3× higher. Divide by throughput to compare properly — see [Compute Budgeting](/hardware/compute-budgeting/).
