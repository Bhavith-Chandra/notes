<Abstract>
Static word embeddings are largely obsolete as a component and indispensable as an
education. They are the setting in which the geometry of meaning was first
understood, where the equivalence between neural training and matrix
factorisation was proved, and where the field learned — slowly, and partly by
being embarrassed — how easily a compelling demonstration can overstate what a
model knows. Everything here recurs in modern embeddings, usually with the same
failure modes and less clarity about them.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The distributional hypothesis is the whole foundation.** A word's meaning is approximated by the company it keeps. Every embedding method, including modern ones, is a way of turning that slogan into an optimisation problem.
- **Skip-gram with negative sampling is implicitly factorising a matrix.** Levy and Goldberg proved that SGNS has an optimum where the dot product equals pointwise mutual information minus $\log k$. The neural framing was a very effective optimisation trick, not a different kind of model.
- **The famous analogy result is real and substantially oversold.** The standard evaluation excludes the three input words from the answer candidates. Without that exclusion, the nearest neighbour of `king − man + woman` is usually *king*.
- **Hyperparameters mattered more than algorithms.** Levy, Goldberg and Dagan showed that once you transfer word2vec's design choices to count-based methods, most of the reported gaps close. Much of the 2013–2015 "neural beats count-based" literature was comparing tuned to untuned.
- **Bias in embeddings is measurable, real, and not removed by the popular debiasing methods.** Gonen and Goldberg showed projection-based debiasing hides the bias from the specific probe while leaving the cluster structure intact.

</KeyPoints>

## One idea, stated in 1954

> You shall know a word by the company it keeps.

That is Firth, and Harris made a similar argument about distributional structure
in the same decade. The claim is that if two words appear in the same kinds of
contexts, they mean similar things.

It is obviously incomplete — it cannot distinguish *good* from *bad*, which occur
in nearly identical contexts — and it is astonishingly productive anyway.

<Aside type="tip" title="Turning the slogan into a procedure">
Take a large corpus. For every word, count what appears near it.

- *dog*: leash 340, bark 210, walk 180, veterinarian 95, …
- *cat*: leash 45, purr 200, litter 190, veterinarian 88, …
- *democracy*: election 410, vote 380, parliament 220, …

Each word is now a vector of counts over a vocabulary. *dog* and *cat* have
similar vectors; *democracy* does not resemble either. You have an embedding.

Everything after this is fixing three problems with the naive version: the vectors
are enormous (one dimension per vocabulary word), they are dominated by frequency
rather than informativeness, and they are almost entirely zeros.
</Aside>

## Three fixes, in order

<Steps>

1. **Frequency is not information.** *the* appears near everything. Replace raw counts with **pointwise mutual information** — how much more often two words co-occur than chance would predict. Negative values are unreliable in a finite corpus, so clip them: positive PMI, or PPMI.

2. **The matrix is too big.** 100,000 words gives a 100,000² matrix. Factorise it — truncated SVD keeps the top few hundred directions. This is latent semantic analysis, and it predates word2vec by two decades.

3. **Factorising a huge matrix is expensive.** word2vec's contribution: never build the matrix. Stream the corpus and do stochastic gradient updates on a prediction task instead. Same target, vastly cheaper computation.

</Steps>

The third step is what made embeddings ubiquitous, and step 3's relationship to
step 2 is the theoretical result that ties the field together — proved after
word2vec was already famous.

## word2vec, informally

Two architectures, described in the 2013 paper.

**Skip-gram**: given a word, predict the words around it. **CBOW**: given the
surrounding words, predict the middle one.

Skip-gram works better on rare words and became the default. The training signal
is a prediction task, but **nobody wants the predictions** — the point is the
weight matrix, whose rows are the embeddings. The task is a pretext, in exactly
the sense used in [self-supervised learning](/training/self-supervision/).

## The three algorithms, compared

| | word2vec (SGNS) | GloVe | fastText |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | Local windows, streamed | Global co-occurrence counts | Local windows + character n-grams |
| **Objective** | Binary classification vs sampled negatives | Weighted least squares on log co-occurrence | Same as SGNS, over subword sums |
| **Sees the corpus** | Once per epoch, online | Once to build the matrix, then iterates on non-zeros | Once per epoch, online |
| **Out-of-vocabulary** | Fails — no vector exists | Fails | **Works** — composes from n-grams |
| **Morphology** | Ignored; *run* and *running* are unrelated tokens | Ignored | Captured by shared n-grams |
| **Best at** | The default; strong on semantic tasks | Slightly better on some analogy sets | Morphologically rich languages, noisy text, OOV |

<Aside type="note" title="GloVe's actual argument">
Pennington et al. framed GloVe as fixing a conceptual gap: word2vec looks at local
windows and never uses global co-occurrence statistics directly, whereas
count-based methods use global statistics but do poorly on analogy tasks. GloVe
was proposed as taking both.

The empirical case is weaker than the framing suggests. Levy, Goldberg and Dagan's
controlled comparison found the differences between GloVe and SGNS were mostly
attributable to hyperparameters and preprocessing rather than to the global-versus-local
distinction. GloVe remains a perfectly good method; the theoretical story for why
it should be better did not survive controlled testing.
</Aside>

## fastText and the OOV problem

word2vec has a hard failure: a word not in the training vocabulary has no vector.
Not a bad vector — none. For English news text this is an annoyance. For Finnish,
Turkish, Hungarian or any agglutinative language, or for user-generated text with
typos, it is disqualifying.

fastText represents each word as the sum of vectors for its character n-grams,
typically lengths 3 to 6. *running* becomes `<ru`, `run`, `unn`, `nni`, `nin`,
`ing`, `ng>` plus the whole-word token. An unseen word still decomposes into
n-grams that were seen.

Two consequences, one good and one worth watching. Morphology comes for free —
*run*, *running* and *runner* share most of their n-grams and therefore land near
each other. And orthographically similar but semantically unrelated words get
pulled together, which is a genuine cost. This is the same trade-off that
[subword tokenizers](/foundations/tokenization/) make, and fastText is
historically the bridge between the two ideas.

## The analogy result, and what it actually shows

The demonstration that made embeddings famous:

$$
v(\text{king}) - v(\text{man}) + v(\text{woman}) \approx v(\text{queen}).
$$

It works. It also does considerably less than it is usually taken to do, and the
gap is instructive.

<Aside type="caution" title="The exclusion that is never mentioned">
The standard evaluation, in the original code and in nearly every reimplementation,
**excludes the three input words from the candidate answers**.

Without that exclusion, the nearest neighbour of `king − man + woman` is very often
*king* itself. The arithmetic moves the vector a little way from *king*, and *king*
remains the closest point. The result is not "the model computes the analogy" but
"among all words other than the three you gave it, the closest is *queen*" — a
much weaker and much less magical claim.

Linzen showed that simple baselines exploiting this — for example, just returning
the nearest neighbour of the third input word — do surprisingly well on standard
analogy sets. Nissim, van Noord and van der Goot documented how the same exclusion
inflates the sensational bias analogies: `man : doctor :: woman : ?` returns
*nurse* only because *doctor* has been removed from consideration.

None of this means the geometry is fake. Linear structure in embedding spaces is
real and reappears in modern models as the [linear representation
hypothesis](/foundations/latent-spaces/). It means the standard demonstration is
a poor measurement of it, and that a striking demo should prompt the question
"what was excluded?"
</Aside>

## Skip-gram with negative sampling, formally

For a centre word $w$ and context word $c$, with separate embedding tables giving
$v_w$ and $u_c$, SGNS maximises

$$
\ell = \sum_{(w,c) \in D} \left[ \log \sigma(v_w^\top u_c) + k\, \mathbb{E}_{c_N \sim P_n} \log \sigma(-v_w^\top u_{c_N}) \right],
$$

where $D$ is the multiset of observed word-context pairs, $\sigma$ is the logistic
function, $k$ is the number of negatives per positive, and $P_n(c) \propto
f(c)^{0.75}$ is the noise distribution.

The objective is a binary classification: distinguish real co-occurrences from
sampled fakes. This is [noise-contrastive
estimation](/architectures/energy-based-models/) in a specific guise, and it is
why the same mathematics reappears in InfoNCE and in every modern contrastive
embedding model.

## The result that reframed the field

Levy and Goldberg analysed what SGNS optimises at its global optimum, treating
$v_w^\top u_c$ as a free parameter for each pair.

Differentiating the per-pair objective and setting it to zero gives, for the
quantity $x = v_w^\top u_c$:

$$
v_w^\top u_c = \log\left(\frac{\#(w,c) \cdot |D|}{\#(w)\cdot \#(c)}\right) - \log k = \mathrm{PMI}(w,c) - \log k.
$$

So **SGNS is implicitly factorising the word-context PMI matrix shifted by
$\log k$**. The neural network, the sampling, the SGD — all of it is an efficient
way to approximate a factorisation of a matrix that was already the standard
object in distributional semantics.

<Aside type="tip" title="Why this mattered, and still does">
Three things follow.

**Historically:** it dissolved the "neural versus count-based" debate. They were
the same method with different optimisation. Levy, Goldberg and Dagan then showed
empirically that transferring word2vec's design choices — dynamic context windows,
subsampling frequent words, context distribution smoothing — to explicit PPMI-SVD
closed most of the remaining gap. The lesson was that the reported advantage came
from hyperparameters, not from the neural framing.

**Methodologically:** it is the clearest example in the field of a result that
looked like a new kind of model and was a new kind of optimiser. That pattern
repeats, and it is worth being suspicious in the same way.

**Practically:** the equivalence is exact only at the unconstrained optimum. Real
SGNS uses a low-dimensional bottleneck, so the factorisation is rank-constrained
and the correspondence is approximate — which is also where the useful
generalisation comes from. Levy and Goldberg found that explicit SVD of the shifted
PPMI matrix matched or beat SGNS on similarity tasks but lost on analogy tasks,
and the reason for that split is still not fully explained.
</Aside>

## GloVe's objective

GloVe fits log co-occurrence counts directly:

$$
J = \sum_{i,j} f(X_{ij}) \left( w_i^\top \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log X_{ij} \right)^2,
$$

with $X_{ij}$ the co-occurrence count, $b$ and $\tilde{b}$ bias terms, and the
weighting function

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} (x/x_{\max})^{\alpha} & x < x_{\max} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

with $x_{\max} = 100$ and $\alpha = 3/4$ in the paper. The weighting does two
jobs: it prevents very frequent pairs from dominating, and $f(0) = 0$ lets the
sum skip zero entries entirely, which is what makes the sparse matrix tractable.

Note that $\alpha = 3/4$ is the same exponent as word2vec's noise distribution,
arrived at independently, and neither paper derives it. Both report that it
works better than the alternatives they tried.

## Geometry, and the correction that fixes it

Trained word embeddings are not isotropic. Mu, Bhat and Viswanath found two
robust properties: the vectors have a large non-zero common mean, and the
variance is dominated by a small number of top principal directions. Both
inflate the similarity between arbitrary word pairs.

Their post-processing, **all-but-the-top**:

<Steps>

1. Subtract the mean vector from every embedding.
2. Compute the top $d$ principal components of the centred set, with $d \approx D/100$ for dimension $D$.
3. Project each embedding off those components.

</Steps>

This consistently improves performance on word similarity and downstream tasks,
at no training cost. The same anisotropy reappears in contextual embeddings, where
it is stronger, and the same family of fixes applies — see [Latent
Spaces](/foundations/latent-spaces/).

## Bias: measurement, and the failure of the popular fix

Caliskan, Bryson and Narayanan adapted the Implicit Association Test to
embeddings. The **Word Embedding Association Test** measures whether two sets of
target words ($X$, $Y$) associate differentially with two sets of attribute words
($A$, $B$):

$$
s(w, A, B) = \operatorname{mean}_{a \in A}\cos(w,a) - \operatorname{mean}_{b \in B}\cos(w,b),
$$

with the test statistic $\sum_{x \in X} s(x,A,B) - \sum_{y \in Y} s(y,A,B)$ and a
permutation test for significance.

Their finding: embeddings trained on ordinary web text reproduce human IAT results
across the board, including the benign ones (flowers versus insects) and the
harmful ones (European-American versus African-American names with pleasantness;
gender with career versus family). The correlation with real-world occupational
gender statistics was strong. Embeddings are an accurate mirror of the corpus,
which is precisely the problem.

<Aside type="caution" title="Why projection-based debiasing does not work">
Bolukbasi et al. proposed identifying a "gender direction" from pairs like
*he*/*she*, then projecting gender-neutral words off it. Measured by the original
probe, it works.

Gonen and Goldberg showed the bias is not removed. After debiasing, words that
were originally gender-biased **still cluster together** — you can still classify a
word's original bias from its debiased vector with high accuracy, and the nearest
neighbours of stereotypically gendered professions remain stereotypically gendered.
The projection removed the specific direction the probe was looking at while
leaving the underlying structure intact.

The general lesson is worth more than the specific finding: **a debiasing method
evaluated with the metric used to define the bias will look successful by
construction.** Any mitigation must be evaluated with a probe that was not used to
design it. This mistake recurs constantly in current alignment and safety work —
see [AI Safety](/foundations/ai-safety/).
</Aside>

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**As a component, static embeddings are mostly retired.** For any task where a
contextual or sentence-level model is affordable, it wins — the same-vector-for-every-sense
limitation is fatal for anything requiring disambiguation.

**They remain in production for specific reasons**, and the reasons are good ones:
they are essentially free at inference (a table lookup), they need no accelerator,
they are interpretable enough to debug, and they are a strong baseline that a
neural retrieval system should be required to beat. A large fraction of
recommender and search systems still use them in the candidate-generation stage,
where latency budgets are measured in microseconds.

**The analysis is still the field's clearest.** We understand what word2vec
optimises, in closed form. Nothing comparable exists for a modern text embedding
model — we know the objective but not what the optimum looks like. That gap is
worth noticing when reading claims about what modern embeddings "understand".

**The bias findings generalised, and so did the flawed evaluation.** WEAT-style
analyses transferred straight to contextual and multimodal models. So did the
methodological error: mitigations evaluated with the metric that defined the
target. Gonen and Goldberg's critique is one of the most transferable results on
this site.

## Open problems

<Steps>

1. **Why linear structure emerges at all.** There are partial accounts — PMI factorisation with a low-rank constraint, latent-variable models, a paraphrase-based argument — but no consensus derivation, and the same question is now open for [modern latent spaces](/foundations/latent-spaces/).

2. **The SVD/SGNS split.** Explicit factorisation of the shifted PPMI matrix matches SGNS on similarity but loses on analogies. Something about the SGD trajectory, or the weighting implied by sampling, is doing work that the closed-form factorisation does not, and it has not been isolated.

3. **Polysemy without context.** Multi-sense embeddings were tried extensively and never worked well enough to displace single-vector models before contextual models made the question moot. Whether a fixed-size representation *can* carry multiple senses usefully is unresolved rather than answered.

4. **Bias mitigation that survives an independent probe.** Still open. Every method that reports success has, so far, been evaluated with a probe related to its own construction.

5. **Cross-lingual alignment without supervision.** Unsupervised alignment of monolingual spaces works well for related languages and degrades sharply for distant ones. Why the isomorphism assumption fails is understood qualitatively and not quantitatively.

</Steps>

## Practical guidance

- **Use fastText over word2vec if you use static embeddings at all.** OOV handling is free and morphology helps in nearly every language.
- **Apply all-but-the-top.** It costs one PCA and reliably improves similarity tasks.
- **Do not report analogy results without saying whether inputs were excluded.** They are not comparable across the two conventions, and most published numbers use exclusion.
- **Run WEAT before deploying.** It takes minutes, and knowing the associations your corpus encoded is better than discovering them from a user.
- **Distrust any debiasing method evaluated with its own defining metric.** Ask for an independent probe; if there is not one, assume the bias is still there.
- **Keep static embeddings as your retrieval baseline.** If a large neural retriever cannot beat fastText plus BM25 on your data, the problem is your data or your evaluation, not your model.
