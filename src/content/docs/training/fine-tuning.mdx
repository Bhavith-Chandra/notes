<Abstract>
Full fine-tuning of a 70B model needs roughly a terabyte of GPU memory, almost
none of it for the weights themselves — the optimizer states dominate. LoRA
sidesteps this by freezing the model and training a low-rank update, cutting
trainable parameters by three to four orders of magnitude with little quality
loss. The reason it works is a hypothesis about adaptation being intrinsically
low-rank, and that hypothesis also explains what fine-tuning cannot do.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Optimizer state, not weights, is what makes full fine-tuning expensive.** Adam keeps two fp32 moments per parameter. For a 70B model that is roughly 560 GB before you have stored a single activation.
- **LoRA trains $BA$ instead of $\Delta W$.** With rank 16 on a 4096×4096 matrix you train 131k parameters instead of 16.8M — a 128× reduction — and merge them back at inference for zero added latency.
- **Fine-tuning changes behaviour far more readily than knowledge.** It is excellent at format, tone, and task framing; it is an unreliable and expensive way to install facts. For facts, retrieval usually wins.
- **Catastrophic forgetting is proportional to how much you move the weights.** LoRA's constrained update is partly why it forgets less than full fine-tuning — a side benefit that is often the deciding factor.

</KeyPoints>

## Where the memory actually goes

The intuition that a 70B model needs 140 GB (at fp16) to fine-tune is wrong by
roughly a factor of eight. The accounting:

| Component | Bytes per parameter | 70B model |
|---|---|---|
| Weights (fp16) | 2 | 140 GB |
| Gradients (fp16) | 2 | 140 GB |
| Adam first moment (fp32) | 4 | 280 GB |
| Adam second moment (fp32) | 4 | 280 GB |
| **Total** | **12** | **~840 GB** |

Plus activations. The optimizer alone is four times the size of the model, and
this is the number that decides whether fine-tuning is possible on your hardware.

**So the useful question is not "how do we shrink the model?" but "how do we shrink
the set of parameters we optimise?"** Freeze the weights and only a small trainable
set carries gradients and optimizer state.

## The low-rank bet

Here is the hypothesis LoRA rests on.

A pretrained model already knows an enormous amount. Adapting it to summarise legal
documents does not require rebuilding that knowledge — it requires a comparatively
small adjustment. So the weight *change* $\Delta W$, though it has the same shape
as $W$, should be much simpler in structure than $W$ itself.

Concretely: express $\Delta W$ as the product of two thin matrices, $B$ ($d \times
r$) and $A$ ($r \times d$), with $r$ tiny — 8, 16, 64. Their product has the full
shape but rank at most $r$. You train $B$ and $A$; $W$ never moves.

For $d = 4096$ and $r = 16$ that is 131,072 trainable parameters where full
fine-tuning would have 16,777,216. And since only those carry optimizer state, the
memory problem largely evaporates.

<Aside type="tip" title="Two properties that make LoRA practical rather than merely clever">
**No inference cost.** $BA$ can be added into $W$ after training, giving a single
merged weight matrix. Unlike adapter layers, there is nothing extra to run.

**Swappable.** A rank-16 LoRA is a few megabytes. You can keep dozens for different
tasks and hot-swap them against one base model in memory — which is why the entire
open-source fine-tuning ecosystem is built on it.
</Aside>

## What fine-tuning is good and bad at

Worth stating early, because it is the most common misconception in applied work.

**Good at:** output format, tone and persona, task framing, following a specific
schema, domain-specific style, refusing or not refusing particular categories.

**Bad at:** installing new facts. It can be done with enough data and epochs, and
it is unreliable — the model may reproduce the fact in the training format and fail
to use it in conversation, or overwrite adjacent knowledge in the process.

**If you want the model to know your documents, retrieve them.** If you want the
model to *behave* differently, fine-tune. Reaching for fine-tuning to inject
knowledge is the standard expensive mistake.

## Choices that matter

<Steps>

1. **Which matrices.** The original paper adapted only attention $W_Q$ and $W_V$.
   Later practice found adapting **all** linear layers — including the MLP — works
   better, and the MLP layers matter more than the attention ones for most tasks.
   Default to all.

2. **Rank $r$.** 8–16 handles style and format. 64–128 for harder domain shifts.
   Beyond that the returns are thin and you should ask whether full fine-tuning is
   the honest answer.

3. **Alpha.** The update is scaled by $\alpha/r$. Setting $\alpha = 2r$ is a common
   default. The scaling exists so that changing $r$ does not change the effective
   learning rate — which means you should *not* re-tune the learning rate every
   time you change rank.

4. **Learning rate.** LoRA tolerates and wants much higher rates than full
   fine-tuning — typically 1e-4 to 3e-4 versus 1e-5. Using a full-fine-tuning
   learning rate is the most common reason a LoRA run underperforms.

5. **Initialisation.** $A$ random, $B$ **zero**. So $BA = 0$ at step zero and the
   model is exactly the pretrained one. Initialising both randomly injects noise
   into a well-trained model and degrades the start.

</Steps>

## QLoRA

Three ideas stacked, which together put 65B fine-tuning on a single 48 GB GPU.

**4-bit NormalFloat (NF4).** A quantisation format whose levels are the quantiles
of a normal distribution. Since pretrained weights are approximately normally
distributed, this is information-theoretically closer to optimal than uniform
4-bit levels for the same bit budget.

**Double quantisation.** The quantisation constants themselves are quantised,
saving roughly another 0.37 bits per parameter. Small, and it adds up at 65B.

**Paged optimizers.** Optimizer state pages to CPU memory on GPU pressure, using
unified memory, which prevents the out-of-memory crashes that otherwise occur at
long sequence lengths.

The base model is stored in 4-bit and dequantised to bf16 *on the fly* for each
matmul. LoRA adapters remain in bf16. Gradients flow through the dequantised
weights to the adapters; the 4-bit weights never update.

<Aside type="caution" title="QLoRA's real cost is speed, not quality">
Dequantising on every forward pass costs compute. QLoRA is typically noticeably
slower per step than LoRA on a model that already fits. It is a *memory* solution.
If your model fits in bf16, use plain LoRA.
</Aside>

## The PEFT family

| Method | What it trains | Merges at inference | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full FT | everything | n/a | best quality, worst memory, most forgetting |
| LoRA | low-rank $\Delta W$ | **yes** | the default |
| QLoRA | LoRA over 4-bit base | yes | memory win, speed cost |
| DoRA | magnitude + direction separately | yes | small consistent gain over LoRA |
| Prefix / prompt tuning | virtual tokens | no | very few params, consumes context |
| Adapters (Houlsby) | inserted bottleneck layers | no | adds inference latency |
| BitFit | biases only | n/a | tiny; surprisingly effective for simple tasks |

The "merges at inference" column is why LoRA displaced adapters. Any method adding
layers adds latency to every request forever; LoRA's cost is zero once merged.

## The parameterisation

For a pretrained weight $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, constrain the update to
rank $r \ll \min(d,k)$:

$$
W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k}.
$$

Forward pass:

$$
h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r}B(Ax).
$$

Note the association: computing $Ax$ first ($r \times k$ times $k$) then $B(\cdot)$
costs $O(r(d+k))$, against $O(dk)$ for the frozen path. For $r=16$, $d=k=4096$
that is 131k multiply-adds against 16.8M — about 0.8% overhead during training,
and zero after merging.

**Parameter count.** $|\Delta W|_{\text{trainable}} = r(d+k)$ versus $dk$ for full
fine-tuning. The compression ratio is

$$
\frac{dk}{r(d+k)} \;\xrightarrow{d=k}\; \frac{d}{2r},
$$

so 128× at $d=4096$, $r=16$.

## Why $\alpha/r$

Without the scaling, changing $r$ changes the magnitude of a typical update, and
you would have to re-tune the learning rate. With $B=0$ initially, early updates
give $\Delta W \approx \frac{\alpha}{r}\,\Delta B\, A$, and since $A$ has $r$ rows
contributing to each output, the sum over the rank index grows like $r$ under
standard initialisation. Dividing by $r$ cancels that growth, so the effective step
size on $\Delta W$ is approximately $r$-independent.

Practical consequence: **$\alpha$ and the learning rate are redundant knobs.** Fix
$\alpha = 2r$ and tune the learning rate only. Tuning both is a common waste of a
sweep.

## The intrinsic dimension argument

LoRA's justification is the *intrinsic dimensionality* hypothesis: the minimum
number of parameters needed to reach within $\epsilon$ of full fine-tuning
performance is far smaller than the parameter count. Empirically, for many tasks a
few thousand well-chosen directions suffice.

If adaptation lives in a low-dimensional manifold, then restricting $\Delta W$ to
rank $r$ loses little — provided the manifold is *approximately linear* in weight
space, which is the assumption that can fail.

**Where it fails.** Tasks requiring genuinely new capability — a new language, a
new modality, substantially new knowledge — need higher rank, sometimes ranks where
LoRA's advantage disappears. The rank at which LoRA stops matching full fine-tuning
is a rough measure of how far the task sits from the pretraining distribution, and
it is a useful diagnostic: if you need $r = 512$, the low-rank assumption is telling
you it does not hold for your task.

## NF4 quantisation

For weights assumed $\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, NF4 places its $2^4$ levels at
the quantiles of the standard normal, so that each bin holds equal probability
mass:

$$
q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{i}{17}\right) + \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{i+1}{17}\right)\right), \quad i = 1,\dots,16,
$$

normalised to $[-1,1]$. This is an information-theoretically optimal quantisation
for normally-distributed data under a fixed level count — as opposed to uniform
levels, which waste resolution in the tails where few weights live.

Quantisation is applied **block-wise** (typically 64 weights per block) with a
per-block scale, so local variation in weight magnitude is handled. Double
quantisation then quantises those per-block scales, saving roughly

$$
\frac{32}{64} - \frac{8}{64} - \frac{32}{256} \approx 0.37 \text{ bits/parameter}.
$$

## Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tuning on a narrow distribution degrades performance elsewhere. A useful
frame: the loss on the original distribution grows roughly with the distance moved
through weight space, to second order,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{orig}}(W_0 + \Delta W) \approx \mathcal{L}_{\text{orig}}(W_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta W^{\top} H\, \Delta W,
$$

since $\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{orig}}(W_0) \approx 0$ at convergence. Damage is
therefore quadratic in $\|\Delta W\|$ weighted by the Hessian.

LoRA constrains $\Delta W$ to a rank-$r$ subspace, bounding how far you can travel
and — more importantly — restricting *which* directions you can travel in. This is
a large part of why LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning, and it is a benefit
that rarely appears in the headline comparison but often decides the choice in
practice.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**LoRA is the default and the "adapt all layers" correction is settled.** The
original attention-only recommendation is outdated; adapting MLP layers matters
more for most tasks. Any implementation still defaulting to $W_Q, W_V$ only is
leaving quality on the table.

**The LoRA-versus-full-fine-tuning question depends on task distance.** For
instruction tuning, style, and format — near the pretraining distribution — LoRA
matches full fine-tuning at a fraction of the cost. For new languages or genuinely
new capabilities, full fine-tuning still wins, and the required rank tells you
which regime you are in.

**Serving many adapters concurrently is solved.** Batched multi-LoRA serving lets a
single base model handle requests for different adapters in one batch, which is
what makes per-customer fine-tuning economically viable.

**Fine-tuning is losing ground to prompting and retrieval for knowledge tasks**,
and gaining ground for behaviour. Long context and good retrieval removed most of
the "fine-tune it on our documents" use case; what remains is genuinely about
changing how the model acts.

## Open problems

- **No principled way to pick the rank.** It is swept. The relationship between
  task difficulty, dataset size, and required rank is not characterised.

- **LoRA on MoE models is unreliable.** Adapting a routed model can shift routing
  in ways that destabilise it, and the interaction is poorly understood.

- **Composing adapters does not work well.** Adding two LoRAs trained separately
  rarely gives you both behaviours; they interfere. Methods to merge them exist and
  none is robust.

- **Knowledge injection remains unreliable.** The mechanism by which fine-tuning
  installs a fact — and why it often fails to generalise beyond the training
  phrasing — is not understood. Retrieval is the pragmatic answer and it is a
  workaround, not an explanation.

- **The alignment tax is real and under-measured.** Fine-tuning on a narrow task
  measurably degrades safety behaviours, including when the fine-tuning data is
  benign. This has direct deployment consequences and is not routinely checked.
