<Abstract>
Show a language model three examples of a task in its prompt and it does the
task — with no gradient step, no weight change, nothing persisted. That should
not obviously be possible, and explaining it has produced four competing
accounts that are all partially right. The most useful finding for practitioners
is also the most deflating: **the input–label mappings in your examples matter far
less than the format**, and models often perform nearly as well when the labels
are wrong.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **No weights change. Ever.** The "learning" happens entirely in the forward pass, in activations that vanish when the context does. Whatever this is, it is not learning in the sense the rest of machine learning uses the word.
- **Correct labels contribute surprisingly little.** Min et al. replaced demonstration labels with *random* ones and performance dropped only slightly. What the examples supply is the label space, the input distribution, and the format — not the mapping.
- **There are four explanations and they are not mutually exclusive.** Induction heads (mechanistic), implicit Bayesian inference (statistical), implicit gradient descent (algorithmic), and task vectors (representational). Each is well-supported in its own regime.
- **A prompt compresses to a single vector.** Task and function vector work shows the demonstrations produce an activation that can be extracted, transplanted into an unrelated context, and made to induce the task on its own.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The phenomenon

Put this in a prompt:

```text
sea otter -> loutre de mer
peppermint -> menthe poivrée
plush giraffe -> girafe peluche
cheese ->
```

The model outputs `fromage`. It was not trained on this task, no gradient was
computed, and the model's parameters are bit-for-bit identical before and after.

**Nothing was learned in the usual sense.** Ask the same model the same question in
a fresh conversation and it has no memory of having done it.

## Why this is strange

Every other form of learning in machine learning changes something persistent.
Training updates weights. Fine-tuning updates weights. Even
[retrieval](/systems/retrieval/) changes an index.

In-context learning changes **nothing**. The adaptation lives entirely in the
activations of a single forward pass and is discarded when the pass ends.

So either the model already knew how to do the task and the examples merely
*selected* it, or the forward pass is running something that deserves to be called
a learning algorithm. Both turn out to be partly true, and the argument over the
proportions is the interesting part.

## The result that reframes it

The obvious hypothesis: the model reads the examples, infers the input→output rule,
and applies it. Min et al. tested this directly and the finding is deflating.

**They replaced every label with a random one** — `sea otter -> menthe poivrée` —
and measured performance. It dropped only a little.

If the model were inferring a mapping, corrupting the mapping should destroy
performance. It did not. So the demonstrations are mostly supplying something else.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the examples actually provide">
Min et al. isolated four things demonstrations carry, and found the mapping is the
least important:

1. **The label space** — which outputs are even on the table.
2. **The input distribution** — what kind of thing you are asking about.
3. **The format** — that the answer follows an arrow, is one word, is lowercase.
4. **The input–label mapping** — which turns out to contribute least.

The practical reading: **your examples are configuring the output channel, not
teaching the task.** Getting format and label space right matters more than
hand-curating perfect examples — which is the opposite of most prompt-engineering
advice.
</Aside>

## Four explanations

<Steps>

1. **Induction heads.** A concrete circuit — find an earlier occurrence of the
   current token, copy what followed it. Mechanistically verified, and it appears in
   training at exactly the moment ICL ability appears.

2. **Implicit Bayesian inference.** The model has learned a mixture over latent
   "topics" or tasks from pretraining. The prompt is evidence, and the model is
   doing posterior inference over which task it is in.

3. **Implicit gradient descent.** The forward pass through attention layers
   computes something equivalent to gradient steps on a small regression problem
   defined by the examples.

4. **Task vectors.** The demonstrations compress into a single activation vector
   that encodes the task; the query is then processed under that vector's influence.

</Steps>

**These are compatible.** A mechanism (1) can implement an algorithm (3) that
performs an inference (2) via a representation (4). The disagreement is about which
level explains the observed behaviour in real models on real tasks.

<Tier level="mechanics" />

## Induction heads

The mechanistic account, and the best-verified one. Covered in full on
[interpretability](/foundations/interpretability/).

A two-head circuit across two layers implements **match-and-copy**: a previous-token
head writes, at each position, information about what preceded it; an induction head
then attends to positions following an earlier occurrence of the current token and
copies what came next.

**Why this counts as an explanation of ICL.** Generalise "copy the literal token"
to "copy the *behaviour* that followed a similar context", and you have few-shot
prompting. The prompt establishes a pattern; the induction mechanism continues it.

**The evidence linking it to ICL** is the strongest in this area:

- Induction heads form during a narrow window of training.
- That window coincides with a visible bump in the loss curve.
- ICL ability appears at the same moment.
- Ablating induction heads degrades ICL disproportionately.

Correlational plus causal, which is more than the other accounts have.

## Implicit Bayesian inference

Xie et al.'s account. Pretraining data contains many latent "concepts" — a document
is about some topic, in some style, following some implicit task. The model learns
$p(\text{next token} \mid \text{context})$ marginalised over these concepts.

At inference, the prompt is **evidence about which concept you are in**. The model
implicitly computes a posterior over concepts and predicts under it.

$$
p(y \mid x, \text{prompt}) = \int p(y\mid x, \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \text{prompt})\,d\theta
$$

**Why this predicts the Min et al. result.** If the prompt is evidence for *which
task*, then the format and label space are highly informative — they identify the
task — while individual label correctness is weak evidence that can be outweighed.
The model is not learning the mapping; it is **locating** a mapping it already has.

**Where it is weak.** It explains selection among known tasks, and not learning
genuinely novel ones. Which brings us to the case where models demonstrably do
learn something new.

## Implicit gradient descent

The most surprising line, and it starts with a clean experiment.

Garg et al. asked whether transformers can learn function classes in-context that
have nothing to do with language. Train a transformer on sequences of
$(x_1, f(x_1), x_2, f(x_2), \dots)$ where $f$ is drawn from some class — linear
functions, sparse linear, decision trees, small MLPs — and test on unseen $f$.

**They can, and they match the appropriate classical algorithm.** For linear
functions the transformer's in-context predictions match ordinary least squares. For
sparse linear it matches Lasso. It is not retrieving a memorised task; it is fitting
a new function from examples in the forward pass.

**Then two papers explained how.** Akyürek et al. and von Oswald et al. showed —
independently, with different methods — that a linear self-attention layer can
**implement one step of gradient descent** on a least-squares objective, and that
transformers trained on these tasks converge to weights that do approximately this.

So a $k$-layer transformer can implement $k$ steps of gradient descent on a
regression problem defined by the in-context examples. **The forward pass is running
an optimisation.**

<Aside type="caution" title="How far this transfers to real models">
These results are on *linear* self-attention and *synthetic* function classes.
Whether a production language model doing sentiment classification is running
implicit gradient descent is a much stronger claim, and the evidence is
circumstantial.

The honest summary: **transformers are capable of implementing learning algorithms
in their forward pass, and this is demonstrated constructively.** Whether that is
what they do on natural tasks is open, and probably varies by task — retrieval-like
tasks look Bayesian, novel-function tasks look algorithmic.
</Aside>

## Task vectors

The representational account, and the most practically striking.

Hendel et al. and Todd et al. showed — concurrently — that the demonstrations
compress into a **single activation vector**. The procedure:

<Steps>

1. Run the model on a prompt containing demonstrations.
2. Extract the activation at a particular layer and position — typically the last
   token of the demonstrations.
3. Run the model on a *fresh* prompt with **no demonstrations at all**, and patch
   that activation in.
4. The model performs the task.

</Steps>

**The demonstrations are not needed at inference** once you have the vector. The
task has been reduced to a point in activation space.

**Why this is more than a curiosity.** It says ICL factorises: the prompt's job is
to produce a task representation, and the query's job is to be processed under it.
Those are separable stages, which is why the vector transplants. It also connects
directly to the [linear representation
hypothesis](/foundations/latent-spaces/) — tasks, like concepts, are directions.

## What the accounts explain

| | Induction heads | Bayesian | Implicit GD | Task vectors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Level | mechanism | statistics | algorithm | representation |
| Explains pattern continuation | **yes** | partly | no | partly |
| Explains novel function learning | no | no | **yes** | no |
| Explains random-label robustness | partly | **yes** | no | **yes** |
| Explains the training-time phase change | **yes** | no | no | no |
| Verified causally | **yes** | no | in toy settings | **yes** |
| Evidence from real LLMs | **yes** | indirect | limited | **yes** |

**Nothing explains everything**, and the row structure suggests why: they are
answering different questions. Treating them as rivals is the mistake.

<Tier level="formal" />

## The setup

Given a prompt of $k$ demonstrations and a query:

$$
P = \big(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2, \dots, x_k, y_k, x_{\text{query}}\big),
$$

the model produces $p_\theta(y \mid P)$ with $\theta$ **fixed**. Define in-context
learning as the property that accuracy improves with $k$:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{A}(k)\big] > \mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{A}(0)\big],
$$

typically with a roughly log-linear improvement over a range of $k$ before
saturating.

## The Bayesian account

Model pretraining as generating documents from a mixture over latent concepts
$\theta$:

$$
p(\text{doc}) = \int p(\text{doc}\mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta .
$$

A model trained to convergence on this distribution computes the posterior
predictive:

$$
p\big(y \mid x_{\text{query}}, P\big) = \int p\big(y\mid x_{\text{query}},\theta\big)\,p\big(\theta\mid P\big)\,d\theta .
$$

As $k$ grows, if the demonstrations are informative about $\theta^{\star}$, the
posterior concentrates:

$$
p\big(\theta \mid P\big) \longrightarrow \delta_{\theta^{\star}},
$$

recovering the task-specific predictor. **Improvement with $k$ is posterior
concentration.**

**Why the prompt works despite low likelihood.** Xie et al.'s key technical point:
the prompt is *not* a typical sample from the pretraining distribution — the
transitions between independent examples are unlikely under any single concept. The
argument is that this "distribution mismatch" contributes an $O(k)$ penalty that is
**dominated** by the $O(k)$ evidence for $\theta^\star$, provided the signal exceeds
the mismatch. So the posterior still concentrates correctly, and the analysis
predicts ICL should fail when the mismatch term dominates — which matches the
empirical brittleness to prompt format.

## Attention as gradient descent

The constructive result. Consider linear self-attention with query, key, value
matrices $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ acting on tokens $e_j = (x_j, y_j)$.

Take a linear regression task $y = w^\top x$ and a current estimate $w$. One step of
gradient descent on the squared loss over the in-context examples is

$$
\Delta w = -\eta\,\nabla_w \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(w^{\top}x_j - y_j\big)^2 = -\eta \sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(w^{\top}x_j - y_j\big)x_j .
$$

**The construction.** Choose the attention parameters as

$$
W_K = W_Q = \begin{pmatrix} I_x & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
W_V = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ w_0^{\top} & -1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

with an identity read-out. Then the update a linear attention layer applies to the
query token's $y$-slot is

$$
\Delta y_{\text{query}} = \sum_{j} \big(x_{\text{query}}^{\top}x_j\big)\big(w_0^{\top}x_j - y_j\big),
$$

which is exactly $x_{\text{query}}^\top \Delta w$ for the gradient step above, with
$\eta$ absorbed into the scaling.

**So one linear attention layer = one gradient step**, and $L$ layers implement $L$
steps. von Oswald et al. then showed empirically that transformers *trained* on
these tasks converge to weights close to this construction, and that layer-wise
in-context predictions track the corresponding GD iterates.

**Akyürek et al.'s complementary result** is a capacity statement: transformers of a
given depth can implement ridge regression and exact least squares, and the
algorithm the trained model implements shifts with problem dimension and depth —
behaving like ridge in some regimes and like exact OLS in others.

**Read this correctly.** These establish that the function class of a transformer
*contains* these algorithms and that training finds them on synthetic tasks. Neither
establishes that a production LLM classifying sentiment is doing this.

## Induction heads, formally

From the [interpretability](/foundations/interpretability/) treatment: the circuit
uses **K-composition**, where layer 2's keys are built from layer 1's output rather
than from the embedding.

For a sequence containing `[A][B] … [A]`, the induction head's attention score at
the current position is

$$
\big(W_Q^{(2)} e(A)\big)^{\top}\big(W_K^{(2)} W_{OV}^{(1)} e(A)\big),
$$

which is large exactly at positions following an earlier $A$, since layer 1 wrote
$A$-information into those positions. The head then copies via $W_{OV}^{(2)}$.

**The phase-change evidence.** Olsson et al. define an ICL score as the difference
in loss between predicting the 500th token and the 50th token of a context — how
much the model benefits from more context. Plotting it against training step shows
a sharp improvement in a narrow window, coinciding with induction head formation and
with a visible bump in the training loss.

That is a mechanistic account of an emergent capability, and it remains the field's
strongest such result.

## Task vectors

Formally, let $h^{(\ell)}_{-1}(P)$ be the activation at layer $\ell$, final
demonstration position, for prompt $P$. The **task vector** is

$$
v_{\mathcal{T}} = \mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mathcal{T}}\Big[h^{(\ell)}_{-1}(P)\Big],
$$

averaged over prompts for task $\mathcal{T}$. Then run the model on a
demonstration-free query and **patch**:

$$
h^{(\ell)}_{-1}\big(x_{\text{query}}\big) \leftarrow v_{\mathcal{T}}.
$$

The model performs $\mathcal{T}$.

**Two properties worth noting.** The vectors are **causal** — patching changes
behaviour, so this is not a correlational probe. And they are **compositional** in
limited ways: Todd et al. found vector arithmetic over function vectors can
compose tasks, echoing the [linear representation
hypothesis](/foundations/latent-spaces/).

**The layer matters.** Effectiveness peaks at intermediate layers. Too early and the
task has not been formed; too late and the computation conditioned on it has already
happened.

## Scaling with the number of examples

Empirically, accuracy grows roughly logarithmically over a range:

$$
\mathcal{A}(k) \approx a + b\log k,
$$

before saturating. The saturation point moved substantially with long-context
models.

**Many-shot ICL** (Agarwal et al., 2024) revisited this with hundreds or thousands
of examples and found continued gains well past the few-shot regime, plus two
things that are practically useful:

- **Reinforced ICL** — replace human-written rationales with model-generated ones
  filtered for correctness, which removes the human bottleneck on example count.
- **Unsupervised ICL** — supply only *inputs*, no outputs, and still see gains,
  which is further evidence that the input distribution rather than the mapping is
  doing much of the work.

Many-shot also reduces sensitivity to example ordering and can override pretraining
priors that few-shot cannot — suggesting the regimes are qualitatively different,
not just quantitatively.

<Tier level="frontier" />

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**The explanations have stratified rather than converged.** Induction heads explain
the mechanism and the training-time phase change. The Bayesian account explains
task *selection* and the robustness to wrong labels. Implicit gradient descent
explains genuine in-context *function fitting* in controlled settings. Task vectors
explain the representational format. The field has largely stopped treating these
as competitors.

**Many-shot changed the practical picture.** With long contexts, hundreds of
examples are affordable, and the resulting behaviour is closer to lightweight
fine-tuning than to the classic few-shot phenomenon — including overriding
pretraining priors, which few-shot could not do.

**Task vectors are a genuine capability, not just an analysis tool.** Extracting a
task into a vector means you can apply it without spending context on
demonstrations, which is directly useful when context is the scarce resource.

**Reasoning models complicate the story.** Models trained with [RL on verifiable
rewards](/training/post-training/) do much of the adaptation through generated
reasoning tokens rather than through the prompt's demonstrations. Whether that is
still in-context learning, or a different capability wearing the same clothes, is
not settled.

## Open problems

- **We cannot predict when ICL will work.** Performance is highly sensitive to
  format, ordering, and example selection, and no theory predicts the sensitivity in
  advance.

- **The gap between toy and real remains.** The implicit-gradient-descent results
  are on linear attention and synthetic functions. Nothing establishes the same
  mechanism operates on natural language tasks.

- **Ordering effects are unexplained.** Permuting the same demonstrations can swing
  accuracy substantially. Recency and primacy effects are documented, and no account
  predicts their magnitude.

- **The relationship to fine-tuning is unclear.** ICL and fine-tuning sometimes
  reach similar performance by apparently different routes. Whether they induce
  similar internal computations is open — task vectors suggest partly yes.

- **Nothing persists.** Every conversation starts from zero. Whether that is a
  limitation to fix or a property to preserve is a design question the field has not
  settled.

## Practical guidance

- **Fix the format first.** It contributes more than the labels. Consistent
  separators, consistent casing, consistent answer shape.
- **Cover the label space.** Every possible output should appear at least once among
  the demonstrations.
- **Match the input distribution** to your real queries. This matters more than
  choosing "good" examples.
- **Test ordering.** It is a real variable; if performance is borderline, permuting
  is a cheap thing to try.
- **If context is scarce**, consider extracting a task vector rather than spending
  tokens on demonstrations.
- **If context is plentiful**, use many more examples than the classic 3–5. The
  gains continue much further than the few-shot literature suggested.

## Reading path

1. **Min et al. (2022)** — the random-label experiment. Read it first; it corrects
   the intuition everything else builds on.
2. **Olsson et al. (2022), Induction Heads** — the mechanism, plus the
   training-dynamics evidence.
3. **Garg et al. (2022)** — the clean experimental setup that made "is this a
   learning algorithm?" answerable.
4. **von Oswald et al. (2022)** — the construction showing attention can implement
   gradient descent. Work through the parameter choice.
5. **Hendel et al. (2023)** and **Todd et al. (2023)** — task and function vectors,
   read together.
6. **Agarwal et al. (2024)** — many-shot, for how the picture changes with long
   context.
