<Abstract>
A pretrained language model predicts what text is likely, not what text is
useful. Post-training closes that gap. The classical recipe learns a reward model
from human preference comparisons and optimises against it with RL; DPO showed the
reward model can be skipped entirely; and the 2025 shift is toward rewards that
are *verifiable* — where a program, not a human or a learned proxy, decides
whether the answer is right.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Pretraining and helpfulness are different objectives.** A base model asked a question may continue with more questions, because that is what the internet does. Post-training is what makes "answer it" the likely continuation.
- **Preferences are used because absolute ratings are unreliable.** Humans agree poorly on "rate this 1–10" and much better on "which of these two is better". The Bradley–Terry model turns those comparisons into a scalar reward.
- **DPO's result is an identity, not an approximation.** The optimal policy under a KL-constrained reward objective has a closed form, which can be inverted to express reward in terms of the policy — so you can optimise the preference likelihood directly and never build a reward model.
- **Every learned reward model gets gamed.** Optimise hard enough against a proxy and you find its errors rather than the intent. The KL penalty is a leash on how far you may stray, and it is a mitigation rather than a fix.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The base model problem

Pretrain on the internet and you get a system that continues text plausibly. Ask
it *"What is the capital of France?"* and a perfectly good continuation is:

> What is the population of Germany? What is the largest city in Italy?

That is not a failure. Lists of quiz questions exist on the internet, so this is a
*likely* continuation. It is simply not a *useful* one, and the model was never
told the difference.

**Post-training installs that difference.** It shifts the model from "what usually
follows?" to "what would a helpful assistant say?".

## Why comparisons rather than scores

The first instinct is to have humans rate responses out of ten. This works badly.
Different annotators anchor differently; the same annotator drifts over a session;
nobody can articulate why a 7 is not a 6.

Ask instead: *"Which of these two responses is better?"* Agreement rates jump
dramatically. People are poor at absolute judgement and good at relative
judgement — a robust finding well outside machine learning.

So the pipeline collects pairwise comparisons and fits a model that assigns scores
*consistent* with them. This is the same statistical problem as ranking chess
players from match outcomes, and it uses the same century-old model.

## The three stages, and why the third is hard

<Steps>

1. **Supervised fine-tuning.** Show the model thousands of high-quality
   demonstrations of assistant behaviour. Ordinary next-token training on curated
   data. This does most of the format-learning work.

2. **Reward modelling.** Collect comparisons, train a model that scores responses
   in agreement with human preference.

3. **Reinforcement learning.** Optimise the language model to score highly under
   that reward model.

</Steps>

Stage 3 is where it gets difficult, for a reason worth stating plainly: **the
reward model is a proxy, and optimisers exploit proxies.** Push hard enough and the
policy stops producing better answers and starts producing answers that trip the
reward model's blind spots — excessive length, sycophancy, confident formatting
over correct content. This is Goodhart's law with a gradient.

<Aside type="caution" title="What the KL penalty is really doing">
Every RLHF objective includes a term penalising divergence from the pre-RL model.
It is not there for regularisation in the usual sense — it is a **leash**. It
bounds how far the policy may travel into regions where the reward model has never
seen data and its predictions are fiction. Remove it and the model reliably
degenerates into reward-hacking gibberish.
</Aside>

## What PPO actually costs

The classical RLHF setup holds **four** models in memory: the policy being trained,
a frozen reference for the KL term, the reward model, and a value model for
advantage estimation. Plus generation during training, which is sequential and
slow.

This is the practical reason DPO landed so hard. It needs two models (policy and
reference), no generation, and no RL machinery — it is a classification loss on
pre-collected pairs.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="PPO">

Policy-gradient with a clipped surrogate objective. The clipping prevents any
single update from moving the policy too far, which is what makes on-policy RL
stable enough to use here.

**Where it still wins:** PPO is *on-policy* — it generates fresh responses and
learns from the reward assigned to them. DPO only ever sees the fixed preference
dataset. When the policy drifts away from the distribution that dataset covers,
DPO has nothing to say and PPO does. Careful head-to-head comparisons have
generally found well-tuned PPO ahead on hard tasks, at substantially more
engineering cost.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="DPO">

The insight: for the KL-constrained reward-maximisation objective, the optimal
policy has a closed form in terms of the reward. Invert it, and the reward has a
closed form in terms of the policy. Substitute that into the Bradley–Terry
preference likelihood, and the reward model cancels out.

What remains is a binary classification loss on the *log-probability ratios* of
chosen versus rejected responses. No reward model, no sampling, no value network.

**The known failure mode:** DPO increases the *margin* between chosen and rejected
responses, and it can do that by pushing the rejected response's likelihood down
rather than the chosen one's up. Both likelihoods can decrease, with the model
drifting to some third distribution neither annotator endorsed. Variants (IPO,
KTO, and the SFT-regularised forms) exist to address this.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="GRPO">

Introduced in DeepSeekMath, and now widely used for reasoning models.

The observation: PPO's value network is expensive and hard to train. But if you
sample a *group* of $G$ responses to the same prompt, the group's mean reward is a
perfectly serviceable baseline — no learned critic needed. Advantage becomes the
response's reward normalised within its group.

This removes one of the four models and much of the tuning difficulty, which is
why GRPO became the default for RL on verifiable tasks where you can afford to
sample many completions per prompt.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="RLVR">

Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards. The reward is not learned at all —
it is computed. Run the generated code against unit tests. Check the maths answer
against ground truth. Parse the output format.

**This eliminates reward hacking against a learned proxy**, because there is no
proxy. The reward is the actual thing you wanted.

DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated that RL on verifiable rewards alone — no reward model, no
human preference data — induces extended chain-of-thought reasoning. The model
learns to think longer because thinking longer produces correct answers, and
correct answers are what is rewarded.

**The limitation is scope.** Verifiable rewards exist for maths, code, and formal
logic. They do not exist for "is this essay good", "is this response kind", or most
of what people actually use assistants for.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Reward modelling

Under the Bradley–Terry model, the probability that response $y_w$ is preferred to
$y_l$ given prompt $x$ is

$$
p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp r(x,y_w)}{\exp r(x,y_w) + \exp r(x,y_l)} = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\big).
$$

Fit by maximum likelihood:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big].
$$

**The reward is only identified up to an additive constant per prompt.** Adding
$c(x)$ to every response's reward leaves all differences — and hence the
likelihood — unchanged. This is why reward values are not comparable across
prompts and why reward-model "scores" reported in isolation are close to
meaningless.

## The RLHF objective and its solution

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\ y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] \;-\; \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big).
$$

This has a closed-form maximiser. Writing the objective per prompt and applying
calculus of variations with the normalisation constraint:

$$
\pi^{*}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
$$

The optimal policy is the reference **reweighted exponentially by reward**. $Z(x)$
is intractable — it sums over all sequences — which is why you cannot simply sample
from $\pi^*$ and must run RL instead.

## DPO's derivation

Take logarithms of the solution above and solve for $r$:

$$
r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
$$

Now substitute into the Bradley–Terry likelihood. The reward appears only as a
*difference* between two responses to the **same** prompt, so $\beta\log Z(x)$ —
which depends on $x$ alone — cancels exactly:

$$
r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) = \beta\left[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right].
$$

Replacing $\pi^*$ with the trainable $\pi_\theta$ gives the DPO loss:

$$
\boxed{\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]\ }
$$

**The cancellation of $Z(x)$ is the entire trick**, and it depends on both
responses sharing a prompt. This is exact — DPO is not approximating RLHF, it is
optimising the same objective in different coordinates, under the assumption that
the preference data is generated by a Bradley–Terry model with the optimal policy's
implicit reward.

**Where the equivalence leaks.** The derivation assumes the preference dataset is
on-policy for $\pi_\theta$. In practice it is collected once, from some other
model. As $\pi_\theta$ moves away from that distribution, the implicit reward is
being evaluated where no data exists — which is exactly the extrapolation problem
RLHF's KL term guards against, reappearing in a different place.

The gradient makes the failure mode visible:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_l - \hat r_w\big)\Big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big],
$$

with $\hat r$ the implicit rewards. The update raises $\log\pi_\theta(y_w)$ and
lowers $\log\pi_\theta(y_l)$, weighted by how wrong the current implicit reward
ordering is. Nothing constrains the *absolute* level of either — hence the
observed drift where both fall.

## PPO's clipped objective

$$
\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}} = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t \hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\hat A_t\big)\Big], \qquad \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.
$$

The $\min$ with the clipped term makes the objective pessimistic: when the
advantage is positive, gain is capped at $1+\varepsilon$; when negative, the
penalty is not capped below $1-\varepsilon$. So the update cannot be driven by a
large importance ratio in either direction, which is what keeps it stable without
an explicit trust region.

## GRPO

Drop the value network. For a group of $G$ sampled responses to prompt $x$:

$$
\hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)},
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\big(\rho_i \hat A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_i, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\big)\right] + \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big).
$$

The group mean is an unbiased baseline for the prompt's expected reward, which is
all the value network was providing. Standardising by the group's standard
deviation additionally normalises the advantage scale across prompts of differing
difficulty — a prompt where all $G$ responses score similarly contributes little
gradient, which is sensible.

**Trade-off:** you need $G$ generations per prompt (typically 8–64) instead of one.
Cheaper in models and tuning, more expensive in sampling. For verifiable tasks
where reward evaluation is a cheap function call, this is clearly the right trade.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Verifiable rewards are the most significant recent shift.** DeepSeek-R1 showed
that RL against automatically-checkable rewards induces long chain-of-thought
reasoning without any human preference data or reward model — the model discovers
that thinking longer improves accuracy, and lengthens its reasoning accordingly.
This sidesteps reward hacking entirely for the domains where verification is
possible.

**DPO won on adoption; PPO retains a quality edge on hard tasks.** DPO's
simplicity made it the default for open-source post-training. Careful comparisons
at scale still favour well-tuned on-policy RL, and the gap is largest where the
preference dataset poorly covers the policy's output distribution. Iterative and
online DPO variants — regenerate preferences from the current policy, retrain —
close much of it, at the cost of reintroducing the sampling loop DPO removed.

**The scope problem is the field's real constraint.** Verifiable rewards cover
maths, code, and formal reasoning. They do not cover helpfulness, tone, honesty, or
judgement — which is most of what assistants do. Those still need learned reward
models, with all the hacking risk that entails.

**Reward hacking is documented and persistent.** Length bias is the canonical
example: reward models systematically prefer longer responses, so RLHF makes models
verbose. Explicit length normalisation helps and does not eliminate it. Sycophancy
is the same phenomenon on a different axis — agreeing with the user is rated
higher than being right.

## Open problems

- **Preference data does not capture what people want.** Annotators rate what looks
  good, which correlates imperfectly with what *is* good. A confidently-worded wrong
  answer often beats a hedged correct one, and the model learns exactly that.

- **No principled way to set $\beta$.** Too small and the policy hacks the reward;
  too large and it barely changes. It is tuned by inspection.

- **Multi-objective alignment is unsolved.** Helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty
  conflict. Collapsing them to one scalar reward forces a trade-off nobody
  explicitly chose. Constitutional AI addresses part of this by generating critiques
  from written principles, which at least makes the trade-offs legible.

- **Evaluation is circular.** Post-training is increasingly evaluated by LLM judges,
  which have their own biases (including a preference for length and for their own
  outputs). Optimising against a judge is reward hacking one level up.

- **Distribution shift in DPO.** The off-policy assumption breaks as training
  proceeds, and there is no clean diagnostic for when it has broken.
