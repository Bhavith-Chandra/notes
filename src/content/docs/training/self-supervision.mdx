<Abstract>
Self-supervised learning manufactures a supervised problem out of unlabelled
data by hiding part of the input and asking the model to recover it. The reason
it took over is not elegance — it is a supply curve. Labelled data is bounded by
human effort; unlabelled data is bounded by storage. Once a self-supervised
objective works at all, it inherits a scaling trajectory that supervised learning
cannot match.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The pretext task is a means, not an end.** Nobody wants a model that predicts masked patches. You want the representation it was forced to build in order to do so, and the task is chosen for what it forces, not what it produces.
- **There are three families, distinguished by what they compare.** Generative compares to the input, contrastive compares to other samples, predictive compares to the model's own output. Everything else is a variation.
- **The augmentation set is the specification.** In contrastive learning you are declaring what should be ignored. SimCLR's ablations showed this choice dominates architecture, and it means a wrong choice silently destroys information you needed.
- **Evaluation is the field's weakest link.** Linear probing is standard and known to be a poor proxy — it measures what a linear head can extract, not what the representation contains. Every reported comparison rests on it anyway.

</KeyPoints>

## The bargain

Supervised learning needs someone to write down the answer. A million labelled
images means a million human judgements — expensive, slow, and capped by how many
people you can pay.

Self-supervised learning gets the answer for free by *hiding part of the data and
using it as its own label*. Cover a word, ask what it was — the sentence already
contains the answer. Cover a patch of an image, ask what was there — the image
already contains the answer. No annotator required, so the data supply becomes
"everything on the internet" rather than "everything we could afford to label".

## Why hiding things teaches anything

This is the part that deserves a moment, because it isn't obvious.

Consider: *"She poured the coffee into the ____ and carried it to her desk."*

To fill the blank you need to know that coffee is liquid, that liquids go in
containers, that the container is portable, that desks are where people work. The
blank is trivial to *check* and hard to *answer*, and answering it requires
assembling a great deal of knowledge about how the world works.

**That asymmetry is the entire mechanism.** The task is cheap to construct and
expensive to solve, and everything the model builds in order to solve it is the
thing you actually wanted.

<Aside type="tip" title="Choose the task for what it forces">
A pretext task is good if solving it *requires* the structure you want. Predicting
image rotation was an early attempt and it turned out to be solvable by spotting
sky-at-the-top — a shortcut that teaches almost nothing. The design question is
always: can this be solved by a cheap trick?
</Aside>

## Generative: predict the input

The oldest and conceptually simplest. Corrupt, then restore.

**Masked language modelling (BERT).** Hide 15% of tokens, predict them from both
directions. Of the masked positions, 80% become `[MASK]`, 10% a random token, 10%
are left unchanged — a detail that exists because `[MASK]` never appears at
fine-tuning time, so training exclusively on it creates a train/test mismatch.

**Masked image modelling (MAE).** Hide 75% of image patches, reconstruct pixels.
The high ratio is the key design choice and the reason it works: images are
spatially redundant, so hiding 15% leaves the task solvable by local interpolation.
At 75% the model must actually understand content. A secondary benefit is speed —
the encoder only processes the visible 25%.

**Next-token prediction (GPT).** Predict token $i$ from tokens $< i$. Every
position is a training example, which makes it the most sample-efficient objective
per pass, and it is the reason decoder-only models scaled so well.

## Contrastive: predict which sample

Take two augmented views of the same image. Pull their embeddings together. Push
embeddings of different images apart.

<Steps>

1. **Augment.** Random crop, colour jitter, grayscale, blur. Two independent draws
   from the same source image.
2. **Encode.** Both views through the same encoder.
3. **Project.** A small MLP head. Loss is computed on the projection; the
   representation you keep is the layer *before* it — this detail is worth several
   points of accuracy and is easy to get wrong.
4. **Contrast.** InfoNCE over the batch: the matching view is the positive, the
   other $2(N-1)$ views are negatives.

</Steps>

<Aside type="danger" title="Augmentations are a specification, and a dangerous one">
SimCLR's ablations found that the *composition* of augmentations matters more than
anything architectural, and that random crop plus colour distortion is the
critical pair. The reason is instructive: crops of the same image share a colour
histogram, so without colour jitter the model can match views by colour alone and
learn nothing else.

The flip side is a real hazard. Colour-invariance is exactly wrong if you are
classifying ripeness, diagnosing rashes, or reading resistor bands. **You are
declaring what the model should be blind to**, and if you declare wrong, the
information is gone and no amount of downstream fine-tuning brings it back.
</Aside>

**The negatives problem.** InfoNCE's quality depends on having many negatives.
SimCLR used batches up to 8192, which requires substantial hardware. MoCo's answer
was a queue of embeddings from recent batches plus a momentum-updated encoder to
keep those stale embeddings consistent — decoupling the number of negatives from
the batch size. This is the practical route if you don't have a TPU pod.

## Predictive: predict your own representation

Covered in depth on the [JEPA page](/architectures/jepa/); the summary is that a
student network predicts the output of an EMA copy of itself, with a stop-gradient
on the target. No negatives, no decoder — and the trivial constant solution has to
be blocked structurally rather than by the loss.

## Choosing between them

| You want | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval, search, matching | contrastive | cosine geometry is directly meaningful |
| Cross-modal (image↔text) | contrastive (CLIP-style) | pairs supply the positives for free |
| General features, many unknown tasks | predictive (DINOv2, I-JEPA) | best transfer; no invariance assumptions |
| Generation or reconstruction | generative | only family with an invertible map |
| Small compute budget | MoCo or SimSiam | neither needs enormous batches |
| Text understanding | generative (MLM) | still the strongest for encoder-only text |

## InfoNCE

For anchor $x$ with positive $x^+$ and $N-1$ negatives:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z^+)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z_j)/\tau\big)}\right],
$$

with $\operatorname{sim}(u,v) = u^\top v / (\|u\|\|v\|)$ and temperature $\tau$.

**The mutual information bound.** van den Oord et al. showed

$$
I(z; z^+) \;\geq\; \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} .
$$

Two consequences, one encouraging and one sobering:

- Minimising the loss maximises a lower bound on mutual information between views.
  This is the theoretical justification for the whole approach.
- **The bound is capped at $\log N$.** With $N = 8192$, $\log N \approx 9$ nats.
  Any mutual information above that is invisible to the objective. So the bound is
  *loose* at realistic batch sizes, and you should be sceptical of arguments that
  lean heavily on it. It explains why large batches help; it does not establish
  that the method is maximising anything tight.

**Temperature.** Writing the gradient with respect to a negative's similarity
$s_j$:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s_j} = \frac{1}{\tau}\cdot \frac{\exp(s_j/\tau)}{\sum_k \exp(s_k/\tau)} .
$$

Small $\tau$ concentrates gradient on the *hardest* negatives — those most similar
to the anchor. Large $\tau$ spreads it evenly. This makes $\tau$ a hard-negative
mining knob, not a normalisation detail, and it is genuinely sensitive: typical
values sit in $[0.07, 0.2]$ and performance degrades noticeably outside.

## Alignment and uniformity

Wang & Isola's decomposition is the most useful diagnostic tool in the area.
Define, on the unit hypersphere:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}(f;\alpha) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^+)}\big[\|f(x) - f(x^+)\|_2^{\alpha}\big],
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{unif}}(f;t) = \log\, \mathbb{E}_{x,y \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} p_{\text{data}}}\big[e^{-t\|f(x)-f(y)\|_2^2}\big].
$$

They prove that as $N \to \infty$, InfoNCE converges to a weighted sum of these
two, and — more usefully — that optimising them *directly* gives representations
comparable to or better than contrastive learning.

The practical payoff: **measure them separately**. They diagnose different
failures.

| Alignment | Uniformity | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| good | good | healthy |
| good | poor | collapsing — embeddings clumping |
| poor | good | positives too weak, or augmentations too aggressive |
| poor | poor | not training |

The loss value alone cannot distinguish these, which is why tracking the pair is
worth the small extra cost.

## Masked language modelling

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}\left[\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta\big(x_i \mid x_{\setminus \mathcal{M}}\big)\right],
$$

where $\mathcal{M}$ is a random subset, $|\mathcal{M}| \approx 0.15\,n$.

**Why 15%, and what the trade-off is.** Let $\rho$ be the mask ratio. The
conditioning context has $(1-\rho)n$ tokens and the supervised signal covers $\rho
n$ positions. Small $\rho$ gives rich context but few gradients per sequence; large
$\rho$ gives many gradients but impoverished context. The optimum depends on the
redundancy of the modality — and this is exactly why images (highly redundant) use
$\rho = 0.75$ while text (much less redundant) uses $\rho = 0.15$. The numbers
differ by modality for a principled reason, not by convention.

**Compared with causal LM.** MLM conditions on both sides and supervises $\rho n$
positions; CLM conditions on the left only and supervises all $n$. MLM gets better
representations per parameter; CLM gets more gradient per FLOP. Neither dominates,
which is why the encoder-versus-decoder question stayed open.

## The evaluation problem, stated precisely

The standard protocol: freeze the encoder, train a linear classifier on top,
report accuracy.

$$
\text{acc}_{\text{linear}} = \max_{W,b}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[\mathbb{1}\{\arg\max(W f(x) + b) = y\}\big] .
$$

The problem is that this measures **linear accessibility**, not information
content. A representation could encode a feature perfectly in a form a linear
probe cannot read — via an XOR of two dimensions, say — and score zero. Alain &
Bengio introduced probes as a diagnostic and were explicit that probe accuracy is
a property of the probe-and-representation pair, not of the representation.

The alternatives are each unsatisfying in their own way:

- **Full fine-tuning** — measures the fine-tuning recipe as much as the encoder,
  and washes out differences between pretraining methods.
- **k-NN classification** — no learned head, so cleaner, but assumes Euclidean or
  cosine geometry is the right metric, which is only true for some families.
- **Alignment/uniformity** — better, but defined relative to an augmentation
  family, which is circular for methods that claim to need no augmentations.

**Every headline comparison between SSL methods rests on one of these.** That is
worth remembering when a paper reports a 0.4-point improvement.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**In vision, self-distillation is the practical default.** DINOv2 features are
what most practitioners reach for when they need general-purpose visual
representations, and they transfer across a wide range of tasks without
fine-tuning. The emergent segmentation behaviour in the DINO line — attention maps
that outline objects with no segmentation supervision — remains the most striking
qualitative evidence that these objectives capture something structural.

**In language, SSL is simply how models are trained**, to the point that the term
has fallen out of use. Next-token prediction is self-supervised; nobody calls it
that any more.

**Masked reconstruction refuses to die.** MAE remains competitive despite the
theoretical argument that pixel reconstruction wastes capacity. The proposed
explanation — aggressive masking makes the task semantic — is plausible and
unproven, and this is a genuine open disagreement rather than a settled question.

**Scaling behaviour is less characterised than for generative models.** We know
more data helps. We do not have clean compute-optimal curves comparable to
Chinchilla, partly because there is no agreed loss to plot against.

## Open problems

- **Evaluation is unsolved and everything depends on it.** No task-agnostic
  measure of representation quality exists. This is not a minor methodological
  gripe — it means the field's ranking of its own methods is built on a proxy
  known to be biased.

- **Shortcut learning is hard to detect in advance.** A pretext task solvable by a
  spurious cue teaches nothing, and you generally find out only after training.
  There is no principled way to audit a proposed task.

- **Augmentation design remains hand-crafted.** Learning the augmentation
  distribution is an obvious idea that has not produced a robust method.

- **Modality transfer of recipes is poor.** MAE's 75% mask ratio does not transfer
  to text; BERT's 15% does not transfer to images. Each modality needed its own
  tuning, and there is no theory predicting the right ratio from properties of the
  data.

- **We cannot say what a representation "contains".** Superposition means features
  share dimensions non-orthogonally, so neither individual neurons nor linear
  probes are the right unit of analysis. This connects directly to the
  interpretability work on sparse autoencoders.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Chen et al. (2020), SimCLR** — read Section 3 and Figure 5, the augmentation
   ablations. The most transferable finding in the area.
2. **Wang & Isola (2020)** — for the alignment/uniformity decomposition. It will
   change how you debug an encoder.
3. **He et al. (2021), MAE** — read the mask-ratio ablation, which is the paper's
   real contribution.
4. **Alain & Bengio (2016)** — short, and it inoculates you against over-reading
   linear probe results.
