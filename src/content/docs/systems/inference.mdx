<Abstract>
Generating one token requires reading every weight in the model from memory and
doing almost no arithmetic with each. On an H100 that means the GPU is idle
roughly 99% of the time, waiting on memory. Nearly every inference optimisation
that matters is a response to this single fact — either move fewer bytes, or get
more useful work out of the bytes you already moved.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Decoding is memory-bound; prefill is compute-bound.** They are different workloads with different bottlenecks, and treating them as one problem is why naive serving is slow.
- **Arithmetic intensity is the number to compute.** Batch size 1 decoding does ~2 FLOPs per byte read; an H100 wants ~300. That ratio is the whole story, and it is why batching is the single highest-leverage optimisation.
- **Quantization is a bandwidth optimisation first.** 4-bit weights are 4× fewer bytes to move, so ~4× faster decoding. The memory saving is real; the speedup is the actual reason people do it.
- **Speculative decoding buys parallelism, not fewer FLOPs.** A small model guesses several tokens, the large model verifies them in one pass. You spend more compute to use the bandwidth you were wasting — and the output distribution is provably unchanged.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The GPU is not the bottleneck

Here is the fact that reorganises everything.

To generate one token, the model reads every weight from GPU memory and multiplies
each by a number. For a 70B model in fp16 that is 140 GB read. An H100 has about
3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth, so the read alone takes roughly 42 milliseconds —
about 24 tokens per second, and that is a hard ceiling.

Meanwhile the arithmetic — 140 billion multiply-adds — takes under a millisecond on
hardware capable of ~1000 TFLOP/s.

**The GPU spends 98% of its time waiting for memory.** Buying a faster GPU with
the same bandwidth changes nothing.

## Two workloads wearing one name

<Steps>

1. **Prefill.** Process the prompt. All tokens go through at once, so you read each
   weight once and use it for hundreds of tokens. Compute-bound, efficient, fast.

2. **Decode.** Generate token by token. Each token requires reading all the weights
   again, and uses each weight exactly once. Memory-bound, inefficient, slow.

</Steps>

A 1000-token prompt might prefill in 100ms and then generate 1000 tokens over 40
seconds. **Almost all the time is in the slow phase**, which is why "time to first
token" and "tokens per second" are reported separately — they measure different
bottlenecks and different optimisations fix them.

## Why batching is the biggest win

If you read 140 GB of weights to serve one user, and a second request arrives, you
can serve both from the *same* read. The weights are already in registers; using
them twice costs almost nothing extra.

Batch 32 requests and you read the weights once for 32 tokens instead of 32 times.
Throughput goes up nearly 32×, with only a small increase in latency per request.

**This is why hosted inference is cheap and local inference is not.** A server
batching hundreds of concurrent requests operates in a fundamentally more efficient
regime than your laptop generating one stream.

<Aside type="tip" title="What every technique on this page is doing">
Either **move fewer bytes** (quantization, GQA, KV-cache compression) or **get more
work per byte moved** (batching, speculative decoding). There is no third category.
Once you have that frame, each technique's benefit and limits are predictable.
</Aside>

For a matrix-vector product with an $n \times n$ weight matrix at batch size $B$:

$$
\text{FLOPs} = 2n^2 B, \qquad \text{bytes read} \approx 2n^2 \ (\text{fp16 weights}),
$$

$$
\text{arithmetic intensity} = \frac{2n^2 B}{2n^2} = B .
$$

**Arithmetic intensity is just the batch size.** That is the cleanest statement of
why batching matters: at $B=1$ you get 1 FLOP per byte on hardware wanting ~300.

## The KV cache

Attention needs keys and values for all previous tokens. Recomputing them each step
would be quadratic waste, so they are cached — and the cache becomes the dominant
memory consumer at long context.

$$
\text{KV bytes} = 2 \times L \times n_{\text{layers}} \times n_{\text{kv-heads}} \times d_{\text{head}} \times \text{bytes/elem}.
$$

For a 70B-class model (80 layers, 8 KV heads after GQA, $d_{\text{head}}=128$) in
fp16 at 100k tokens:

$$
2 \times 100{,}000 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 \approx 32\ \text{GB}
$$

per sequence. Without GQA (64 heads) that would be 256 GB — larger than the model.
**GQA is primarily a serving optimisation**, and this calculation is why.

## The four levers

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Quantization">

Store weights in fewer bits. 4-bit instead of 16-bit means 4× fewer bytes to read,
so roughly 4× faster decoding — the speedup comes from bandwidth, not from cheaper
arithmetic.

**GPTQ** quantises layer by layer, minimising the error in that layer's *output*
rather than in its weights, using second-order (Hessian) information to decide
rounding. Weights whose perturbation matters more get rounded more carefully.

**AWQ** starts from a different observation: a small fraction of weights —
roughly 1% — matter disproportionately, and which ones can be identified from
*activation* magnitudes rather than weight magnitudes. Scale those channels up
before quantising so they survive, then scale back. No backprop required, so it is
fast and it generalises across calibration sets better.

**The rule of thumb:** 8-bit is essentially lossless. 4-bit costs a small,
measurable amount of quality. Below 4-bit, degradation becomes serious except with
specialised methods.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Speculative decoding">

Use a small fast model to draft $\gamma$ tokens, then have the large model verify
all $\gamma$ **in a single forward pass** — the same pass that would have produced
one token.

Why this is free-ish: verifying $\gamma$ tokens costs the same *weight read* as
producing one, because you read the weights once regardless. You are converting
wasted bandwidth into speculative compute.

Accepted tokens are kept; at the first rejection you resample from a corrected
distribution and discard the rest. Typical speedups are 2–3×.

**The output distribution is provably identical** to standard sampling. This is the
property that makes it usable in production — it is not an approximation, and
enabling it cannot change your model's behaviour.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="PagedAttention">

The insight behind vLLM, borrowed directly from operating systems.

Naive serving allocates a contiguous KV buffer sized to the *maximum* sequence
length for every request. A request that generates 100 tokens in a 4096-token
buffer wastes 97.5% of it. Measured waste in real systems was 60–80%.

PagedAttention stores the KV cache in fixed-size **blocks** that need not be
contiguous, with a block table mapping logical positions to physical blocks —
virtual memory, applied to attention. Waste drops to under 4%.

The second benefit is sharing. Two requests with the same prompt prefix can point
at the *same* physical blocks, with copy-on-write on divergence. For chat with a
long shared system prompt, or parallel sampling from one prompt, this is a large
saving.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Batching">

**Static batching** waits for a full batch, runs it to completion, returns. The
whole batch is held hostage by its longest sequence — finished slots sit idle.

**Continuous batching** operates at the iteration level: when a sequence finishes,
a queued request takes its slot immediately. GPU utilisation stays high and
reported throughput improvements are large — often several-fold over static
batching at the same latency target.

This, combined with PagedAttention, is most of what modern serving engines do.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## The roofline

Achievable performance is

$$
P = \min\big(P_{\max},\ \beta \cdot I\big),
$$

with $P_{\max}$ peak FLOP/s, $\beta$ memory bandwidth in bytes/s, and $I$
arithmetic intensity in FLOPs/byte. The **ridge point** is $I^{*} =
P_{\max}/\beta$.

For an H100 SXM: $P_{\max} \approx 990$ TFLOP/s (bf16 with sparsity off, dense
~990), $\beta \approx 3.35$ TB/s, giving

$$
I^{*} \approx \frac{990 \times 10^{12}}{3.35 \times 10^{12}} \approx 295\ \text{FLOPs/byte}.
$$

Batch-1 decode has $I \approx 1$–2. You are operating at well under 1% of peak
compute, and this is not a software inefficiency — it is the workload.

## Decode latency, from first principles

Ignoring attention (which is small relative to weight reads at moderate context),
time per token is bounded below by the weight read:

$$
t_{\text{token}} \geq \frac{N_{\text{params}} \cdot b}{\beta},
$$

with $b$ bytes per parameter. For 70B at fp16 ($b=2$) on an H100:

$$
t \geq \frac{70\times10^{9} \times 2}{3.35\times10^{12}} \approx 41.8\ \text{ms} \implies \approx 24\ \text{tok/s}.
$$

At 4-bit ($b = 0.5$):

$$
t \geq \frac{70\times10^{9} \times 0.5}{3.35\times10^{12}} \approx 10.4\ \text{ms} \implies \approx 96\ \text{tok/s}.
$$

**The 4× speedup from quantization falls straight out of the bandwidth ratio.** It
has nothing to do with faster integer arithmetic — which is a common
misunderstanding, and it explains why quantization helps decode and barely helps
prefill.

## Speculative decoding, and its correctness proof

Draft model $q$, target model $p$. Draft $\gamma$ tokens autoregressively from $q$,
then evaluate $p$ on all of them in one pass. For each draft token $x_i$ in order,
accept with probability

$$
\min\left(1,\ \frac{p(x_i \mid x_{<i})}{q(x_i \mid x_{<i})}\right).
$$

On rejection, resample from the residual distribution

$$
p'(x) = \frac{\max\big(0,\ p(x) - q(x)\big)}{\sum_{x'}\max\big(0,\ p(x') - q(x')\big)}
$$

and discard the remaining drafts.

**Why the output is exactly $p$.** Consider the probability of emitting token $x$
at a given position. It is emitted either by accepting the draft or by resampling
after rejection:

$$
\Pr[\text{emit } x] = \underbrace{q(x)\min\left(1, \tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)}_{\text{accepted}} + \underbrace{\left(\sum_{x'}q(x')\left(1-\min\left(1,\tfrac{p(x')}{q(x')}\right)\right)\right)p'(x)}_{\text{resampled}} .
$$

The first term is $\min(q(x), p(x))$. The rejection mass is $\sum_{x'}
\max(0, q(x')-p(x'))$, which equals $\sum_{x'}\max(0, p(x')-q(x'))$ since both $p$
and $q$ sum to 1 — so it exactly cancels $p'$'s normaliser, leaving
$\max(0, p(x)-q(x))$. Summing:

$$
\min(q(x),p(x)) + \max(0, p(x)-q(x)) = p(x).
$$

**This is a modified-rejection-sampling identity, and it is exact.** The draft model
affects *speed only* — a poor draft model wastes work, it cannot corrupt output.

**Expected speedup.** With per-token acceptance rate $\alpha$ and draft length
$\gamma$, the expected number of tokens per target-model pass is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}] = \frac{1 - \alpha^{\gamma+1}}{1-\alpha}.
$$

Accounting for draft cost $c$ (draft time relative to target time), the speedup is

$$
S = \frac{1-\alpha^{\gamma+1}}{(1-\alpha)(1 + c\gamma)}.
$$

Two readings: there is an optimal $\gamma$ (too long and you waste drafts past the
first rejection), and $\alpha$ matters far more than draft-model size — which is
why draft models are usually small models from the *same family*, trained on
similar data.

## KV cache memory, with GQA

$$
M_{\text{KV}} = 2 \cdot B \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{layer}} \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot b .
$$

Multi-head sets $n_{\text{kv}} = n_{\text{head}}$; multi-query sets $n_{\text{kv}}
= 1$; grouped-query sets $n_{\text{kv}} = n_{\text{head}}/g$.

The reduction factor is exactly $g$. GQA with 8 groups on a 64-head model gives an
8× smaller cache, which translates directly into 8× more concurrent sequences at
fixed memory — and concurrency is throughput.

## PagedAttention's fragmentation accounting

With contiguous allocation of $L_{\max}$ per request and actual length $L_i$:

$$
\text{waste} = \sum_{i} (L_{\max} - L_i),
$$

which for a heavy-tailed length distribution is most of the allocation.

With block size $S$, the only waste is internal fragmentation in the last block:

$$
\text{waste} = \sum_{i}\big(S - (L_i \bmod S)\big) \bmod S \;\leq\; N \cdot (S-1).
$$

For $S=16$ and mean length 500, that is ~1.5% rather than 60–80%. Since memory
freed is memory available for more concurrent sequences, and concurrency is
throughput, this is a throughput result dressed as a memory result.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Continuous batching plus PagedAttention is table stakes.** Every serious serving
stack implements both. The differentiation has moved to scheduling policy,
prefix-cache management, and disaggregation.

**Prefill/decode disaggregation is the current architectural shift.** Since the two
phases have opposite bottlenecks, running them on separate hardware pools —
compute-dense for prefill, bandwidth-dense with more memory for decode — lets each
be provisioned appropriately and prevents long prefills from stalling decode
batches.

**4-bit is the practical floor for weights.** GPTQ and AWQ at 4-bit are near-
lossless for most uses. Below that, quality degrades sharply without specialised
methods, and the marginal bandwidth saving shrinks anyway.

**KV cache quantization is the newer frontier.** Weights are well-handled;
the cache is now often the larger consumer at long context. 8-bit KV is
near-lossless; 4-bit is usable with per-channel scaling. Attention sinks — the
observation that the first few tokens absorb disproportionate attention mass and
must be preserved at higher precision — matter here.

**Speculative decoding has broadened.** Beyond a separate draft model: self-
speculation using early layers, Medusa-style multiple prediction heads, and n-gram
lookup from the prompt (very effective for code, where verbatim repetition is
common). All share the same correctness guarantee.

## Open problems

- **Long-context serving remains hard.** The KV cache grows linearly and attention
  cost grows with context. Eviction, compression, and offloading all trade quality
  or latency, and none is clearly right.

- **Latency/throughput scheduling is unsolved.** Larger batches raise throughput and
  raise per-request latency. Optimal scheduling under mixed SLAs is an open systems
  problem, currently handled with heuristics.

- **Quantization interacts badly with fine-tuning and MoE.** Quantised MoE models
  can shift routing; quantised fine-tuned models sometimes degrade
  disproportionately. The interactions are not well characterised.

- **Speculative decoding is worthless at large batch.** Its benefit comes from
  spare bandwidth, and at large batch there is none. The techniques that help
  single-stream latency and those that help server throughput are largely disjoint,
  which is under-appreciated.

- **Evaluating quantized models is done badly.** Perplexity is insensitive to
  degradations that show up on reasoning and long-context tasks. Reported
  "lossless" claims frequently rest on perplexity alone.
