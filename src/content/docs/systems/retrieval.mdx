<Abstract>
Retrieval-augmented generation looks like a solved problem — embed documents,
search by cosine similarity, paste the results into the prompt. In practice most
RAG systems fail quietly: they retrieve plausible-looking passages that do not
contain the answer, and the model produces a fluent response anyway. Almost every
real improvement comes from fixing retrieval, not from a better generator.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Chunking is the highest-leverage decision and gets the least attention.** If the answer spans a chunk boundary, no retriever can find it, and no amount of reranking or prompting recovers it.
- **Dense and sparse retrieval fail on different queries.** Embeddings handle paraphrase and miss exact identifiers; BM25 does the reverse. Hybrid search is not a hedge — it covers two genuinely disjoint failure sets.
- **Retrieve broadly, then rerank.** A bi-encoder scores query and document independently, which is fast and lossy. A cross-encoder reads them together, which is accurate and slow. Use the first for recall, the second for precision.
- **Position in the prompt changes whether the model uses a passage.** Models attend most to the beginning and end of their context and reliably under-use the middle — so retrieval order is part of the system, not a cosmetic detail.

</KeyPoints>

## Why not just fine-tune it in

The instinct when a model does not know your documents is to train it on them.
This is usually the wrong tool, for three reasons worth stating plainly.

**Knowledge does not stick reliably.** Fine-tuning is excellent at changing
behaviour and unreliable at installing facts — the model may reproduce a fact in
the training format and fail to use it in conversation.

**Updates are expensive.** A changed document means retraining. Retrieval means
replacing a row.

**There is no citation.** A fine-tuned model cannot tell you where an answer came
from. A retrieval system hands you the passage, which for most real applications
is the entire point.

So: **if you want the model to know something, retrieve it. If you want the model
to behave differently, fine-tune it.**

## The pipeline in one paragraph

Split your documents into chunks. Convert each chunk into a vector that captures
its meaning. When a question arrives, convert it into a vector too, find the
chunks whose vectors are closest, paste them into the prompt, and ask the model to
answer using them.

Every step in that sentence is a place where the system can silently fail, and
the rest of this page is about which failures matter.

## The failure that defines the field

A user asks a question. The system retrieves three passages that are *topically
related* but do not contain the answer. The model, given three relevant-looking
passages and a question, writes a confident answer anyway — synthesising something
plausible from adjacent facts.

The output looks good. It has citations. It is wrong.

<Aside type="danger" title="Why this is worse than a plain hallucination">
An unaided model that does not know something often hedges. A RAG system supplies
context that *looks* authoritative, which both suppresses the hedging and gives
the user documents to point at. **Retrieval failures produce more confident wrong
answers than no retrieval at all**, which is why measuring retrieval quality
separately from answer quality is not optional.
</Aside>

## Chunking

The decision that determines the ceiling on everything downstream.

<Steps>

1. **Fixed-size** — split every N tokens with overlap. Simple, and it cuts
   sentences and tables in half.

2. **Recursive structural** — split on paragraph, then sentence, then word, taking
   the largest unit that fits. The sane default.

3. **Semantic** — embed sentences and split where consecutive similarity drops.
   Better boundaries, more expensive, and it needs tuning.

4. **Document-aware** — split on the document's own structure: markdown headings,
   HTML sections, function definitions in code. **Usually the best option when the
   structure exists**, and routinely skipped because it needs per-format work.

</Steps>

Two adjustments that pay for themselves:

- **Overlap** (10–20%) so a boundary does not orphan a sentence.
- **Contextual headers** — prepend the document title and section path to each
  chunk before embedding. A chunk reading "The limit is 40 hours" is useless; the
  same chunk prefixed with "Employment Policy › Overtime" is retrievable.

## Dense versus sparse

| | Dense (embeddings) | Sparse (BM25) |
|---|---|---|
| Matches | meaning, paraphrase | exact terms |
| "car" finds "automobile" | yes | no |
| Product code `XR-4471` | **often fails** | reliable |
| Rare domain jargon | weak unless in-domain | strong |
| Needs training | yes | no |
| Explains itself | no | yes — you see the matched terms |

**They fail on disjoint sets of queries**, which is why hybrid retrieval is
standard rather than a compromise. Dense embeddings map a rare identifier to
whatever region of the space its subword pieces suggest — which is nowhere useful.
BM25 finds it because it is a literal string match.

Fusion is usually **Reciprocal Rank Fusion**, which combines rankings rather than
scores and so needs no score normalisation between two systems whose scores are not
comparable.

## Reranking

Retrieval and reranking use fundamentally different architectures, and the
difference is the whole reason for the two-stage design.

**Bi-encoder (retrieval).** Query and document are embedded *separately*. Document
vectors can be computed offline and indexed, so search is an approximate
nearest-neighbour lookup over millions of items in milliseconds. The cost is that
the model never sees query and document together, so it cannot reason about their
interaction.

**Cross-encoder (reranking).** Query and document are concatenated and run through
a transformer together, with full attention between them. Far more accurate, and
it requires one forward pass *per pair* — impossible over a whole corpus, fine
over 50 candidates.

**ColBERT** sits between them: encode separately into per-token vectors, then score
by summing each query token's maximum similarity over document tokens. Late
interaction retains some cross-attention benefit while keeping documents
pre-computable. More accurate than a bi-encoder, more storage-hungry.

<Aside type="tip" title="The retrieve-wide-then-rerank rule">
Retrieve 50–100 candidates cheaply, rerank to 3–5 accurately. This is almost
always better than retrieving 5 directly, and the reason is asymmetric: the
reranker can only fix ordering *within* what retrieval returned. Recall at stage
one is a hard ceiling on the whole system.
</Aside>

## Lost in the middle

Models do not attend uniformly across their context. Liu et al. found a pronounced
U-shape: information at the *start* or *end* of the context is used reliably;
information in the *middle* is often ignored, even when it is exactly the answer
and the context window is nowhere near full.

Two direct consequences for system design:

- **Order retrieved passages so the highest-scoring ones sit at the edges** — best
  first, second-best last, weakest buried in the middle.
- **More context is not better.** Stuffing 20 passages in because the window allows
  it measurably degrades accuracy relative to 5 well-chosen ones.

## Dense retrieval

A bi-encoder learns $E_q$ and $E_d$ scoring by inner product:

$$
s(q, d) = E_q(q)^{\top} E_d(d),
$$

trained contrastively with in-batch negatives:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp\big(s(q, d^{+})/\tau\big)}{\exp\big(s(q,d^{+})/\tau\big) + \sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\big(s(q, d_j^{-})/\tau\big)}.
$$

This is [InfoNCE](/training/self-supervision/) with documents as the contrastive
set. Two practical points that determine whether a retriever is good:

**Hard negatives dominate quality.** Random negatives are trivially separable and
teach almost nothing. DPR's central finding was that mining negatives from BM25's
top results — documents that *look* relevant and are not — improves retrieval far
more than architectural changes.

**In-batch negatives make batch size a quality parameter.** Each batch supplies
$n-1$ free negatives, so larger batches mean a harder and more informative
contrastive task.

## Sparse retrieval: BM25

$$
\text{BM25}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \operatorname{IDF}(t)\cdot \frac{f(t,d)\,(k_1+1)}{f(t,d) + k_1\left(1 - b + b\,\dfrac{|d|}{\operatorname{avgdl}}\right)},
$$

$$
\operatorname{IDF}(t) = \ln\left(\frac{N - n(t) + 0.5}{n(t) + 0.5} + 1\right).
$$

Three ideas are encoded here and each is worth reading off:

- **Term saturation** ($k_1 \approx 1.2$–2.0). Term frequency enters through
  $f/(f+k_1)$, which saturates — the tenth occurrence of a word adds far less than
  the second. Raw counts would let keyword-stuffed documents win.
- **Length normalisation** ($b \approx 0.75$). Long documents contain more of
  everything; dividing by $|d|/\operatorname{avgdl}$ prevents length alone from
  driving the score.
- **Rarity weighting.** IDF makes a match on a rare term worth far more than a
  match on a common one.

BM25 has no learned parameters, needs no training data, and remains a
hard-to-beat baseline. Any dense retriever that does not beat it on your data is
not earning its complexity.

## Reciprocal Rank Fusion

$$
\text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in R} \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}, \qquad k \approx 60.
$$

The key property is that RRF uses only **ranks**, never scores. BM25 scores are
unbounded sums; cosine similarities live in $[-1,1]$. Combining them numerically
requires a normalisation nobody can justify. Ranks are directly comparable.

The constant $k$ damps the influence of the very top positions — without it, a
single system's first result would dominate any consensus among the others.

## Approximate nearest neighbours

Exact search over $N$ vectors is $O(Nd)$ per query — unusable at scale. HNSW
builds a multi-layer proximity graph: upper layers are sparse, giving long-range
hops; lower layers are dense, giving local refinement. Search descends greedily
from the top.

Expected complexity is $O(\log N)$, with a recall/latency knob (`efSearch`).

**The trade you are making is silent.** ANN returns *approximate* neighbours —
recall of 95–99% is typical and considered fine. That means 1–5% of the time the
true best passage is simply not in your candidate set, and nothing downstream can
tell. When debugging a retrieval failure, check exact search on the failing query
before blaming the embedding model.

## Why cross-encoders win

A bi-encoder computes $s(q,d) = \langle E_q(q), E_d(d)\rangle$. The document
representation is fixed **before the query is known**, so it must compress the
document into a single vector adequate for *every* possible query.

A cross-encoder computes $s(q,d) = f([q; d])$ with full attention across the pair,
so it can condition the document's representation on the query. That is strictly
more expressive: the bi-encoder's scoring function is a bilinear form in fixed
embeddings, and the cross-encoder's is an arbitrary function of the pair.

The cost is combinatorial. Bi-encoder: $N$ document encodings offline, one query
encoding online. Cross-encoder: $N$ forward passes online. Hence the split.

ColBERT's late interaction:

$$
s(q,d) = \sum_{i \in q} \max_{j \in d}\ E_q(q)_i^{\top} E_d(d)_j,
$$

with per-token embeddings. The $\max$ over document tokens is a soft term-matching
operation, and because document tokens are still encoded independently they can be
indexed offline. Storage grows by roughly the token count per document, which is
the price.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Long context did not kill RAG, and the reason is cost, not capability.** Models
with million-token windows can in principle hold your corpus. In practice
attention cost, KV-cache memory, and the lost-in-the-middle effect all mean that
retrieving 5 relevant chunks beats stuffing 500 — and it is orders of magnitude
cheaper per query. The two are complements: better retrieval feeds better
long-context reasoning.

**Hybrid retrieval with reranking is the settled default.** Dense-only systems
underperform on exact identifiers and rare terminology often enough that pure
vector search is now considered a beginner's configuration.

**Embedding model choice matters less than people think, above a threshold.** MTEB
leaderboard differences of a point or two rarely survive contact with a specific
domain. Chunking strategy and reranking produce larger gains than swapping a
top-20 embedding model for a top-5 one.

**Agentic retrieval is the active direction.** Rather than one retrieval pass, the
model decides *whether* to retrieve, issues multiple reformulated queries, reads
results, and retrieves again. Self-RAG formalised the "should I retrieve, and is
this passage actually relevant" decision as learned reflection tokens. It costs
more per query and handles multi-hop questions that single-pass RAG cannot.

## Open problems

- **Evaluation is genuinely hard.** End-to-end answer quality conflates retrieval
  and generation failures. Measuring retrieval alone needs relevance labels, which
  are expensive and subjective. Most deployed systems are not measured properly.

- **Multi-hop questions defeat single-pass retrieval.** "Which of our vendors in
  the EU has a contract expiring before the new regulation takes effect" requires
  chaining. One embedding lookup cannot do it.

- **Negation and temporal qualifiers are poorly handled.** Embeddings of "contracts
  that include an indemnity clause" and "contracts that exclude an indemnity
  clause" are close together. This is a known and unsolved weakness of the
  bi-encoder formulation.

- **No principled chunking method.** It remains hand-tuned per corpus, and it is
  the highest-leverage parameter in the system.

- **Freshness and invalidation.** When a document changes, its chunks, embeddings,
  and any cached answers all go stale. Most systems handle this by re-indexing
  everything on a schedule and hoping.
