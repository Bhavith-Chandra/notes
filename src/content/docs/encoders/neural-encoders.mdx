<Abstract>
A neural encoder is a learned function from raw data to a vector. Everything
interesting about one is determined not by its architecture but by the objective
that trained it — which is why a ResNet trained on ImageNet and the same ResNet
trained with SimCLR share every weight shape and almost no useful behaviour. This
page walks the six families in the order they were invented, because each one
exists to fix a specific, nameable failure of the one before it.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The objective is the encoder.** Architecture determines what *can* be represented; the training signal determines what *is*. When choosing an encoder, read the loss function first and the architecture second.
- **Each family fixes the previous family's failure.** AE overfits to identity → DAE adds noise. DAE's space has holes → VAE adds a prior. VAE blurs → VQ-VAE discretises. All of them waste capacity on pixels → contrastive and JEPA move the loss into latent space.
- **Reconstruction prevents collapse for free, and that is its real value.** Not fidelity — collapse-resistance. Every non-reconstructive method has to buy that resistance some other way, and that purchase is the hard part of modern SSL.
- **Normalise before you use cosine distance.** Contrastive encoders produce spaces where cosine similarity is meaningful; reconstructive ones generally do not. Using the wrong metric on the wrong space is a silent, extremely common error.

</KeyPoints>

## The compression game

Here is a game. I show you a photograph. You have to describe it to a friend in
exactly 32 numbers, and your friend — who has never seen the photo — has to draw
it back. You both practise on a million photographs until you get good.

To win, you are forced to discover structure. Wasting numbers on individual pixel
values is hopeless; there are millions of pixels and you have 32 slots. So you
start encoding *concepts*: how bright overall, is there a face, roughly where, is
it outdoors. Nobody told you to find those concepts. The bottleneck did.

**That bottleneck-forces-abstraction effect is the entire foundation of neural
encoders.** Everything that follows is a refinement of it.

## The six families, and the complaint that produced each

You can learn this whole page as a chain of complaints.

<Steps>

1. **Autoencoder.** Squeeze data through a narrow layer, reconstruct it.
   *Complaint: with enough capacity it just memorises — it learns to copy, not to
   understand.*

2. **Denoising autoencoder.** Corrupt the input, reconstruct the clean original.
   Copying no longer works, because the thing to output is not the thing you were
   given. *Complaint: the latent space is full of holes. Points between two valid
   encodings decode to garbage.*

3. **Variational autoencoder.** Encode to a *distribution* rather than a point,
   and pull all those distributions toward a common shape. Now the space is
   smooth and you can sample from it. *Complaint: averaging over a distribution
   makes outputs blurry.*

4. **VQ-VAE.** Snap each encoding to the nearest entry in a learned codebook —
   discrete tokens instead of a continuous blur. *Complaint: still reconstructing
   pixels, so still spending capacity on texture nobody cares about.*

5. **Contrastive encoders.** Stop reconstructing. Just require that two views of
   the same thing land close together and two different things land far apart.
   *Complaint: "far apart from what?" requires enormous batches of negative
   examples.*

6. **Joint-embedding predictive (JEPA).** Drop the negatives. Predict one part's
   representation from another part's, and prevent the trivial constant solution
   architecturally. *Complaint: preventing collapse is now the whole difficulty,
   and it is not well understood.*

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="If you only remember one thing">
Reconstruction-based encoders answer *"can you rebuild it?"*. Embedding-based
encoders answer *"can you tell these two apart?"*. The second question is closer
to what almost every downstream task actually needs, and that single observation
explains the last eight years of the field.
</Aside>

## Family by family

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="AE / DAE">

**What it does.** Encoder squeezes to a bottleneck, decoder rebuilds. Loss is
pixel-wise reconstruction error. The denoising variant corrupts the input first
(masking, Gaussian noise, dropout) and asks for the *clean* original back.

**Why the denoising trick matters more than it looks.** A plain autoencoder with
a wide enough bottleneck can learn the identity function and score perfectly
while learning nothing. Corruption breaks that shortcut: to fill in a masked
region you must know what usually goes there, which is a statement about
structure, not about copying. This single idea — corrupt, then restore — is the
direct ancestor of BERT's masked language modelling and of MAE.

**Use it when.** Anomaly detection (train on normal data; high reconstruction
error signals an outlier), denoising, and as a pretraining step when you have
lots of unlabelled data and no better idea.

**Don't use it when.** You need a smooth or samplable latent space. AE latents
are an arbitrary point cloud with gaps.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="VAE">

**What it does.** The encoder outputs a mean and a variance rather than a point.
You sample from that Gaussian, decode, and add a KL penalty pulling every
per-input Gaussian toward a standard normal.

**Why.** The KL term is what fills the holes. It forces the aggregate of all
encodings to cover the prior, so any point you sample decodes to something
plausible. That is what makes a VAE *generative* where an AE is not.

**The knob that matters.** $\beta$ scales the KL term. Raise it and you get more
disentangled, more sample-able latents but blurrier reconstructions; lower it and
you approach a plain autoencoder. There is no setting that gives you both, and
this trade-off is intrinsic rather than a tuning failure.

**Use it when.** You need to generate, interpolate, or sample. Also when you want
an explicit density-ish model for detecting distribution shift.

**Don't use it when.** You want sharp outputs, or you only want features. For
pure representation quality, contrastive and predictive methods beat VAEs
decisively.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="VQ-VAE">

**What it does.** Between encoder and decoder, replace each continuous latent
vector with its nearest neighbour from a learned codebook of $K$ entries. The
latent becomes a grid of discrete integer tokens.

**Why this fixes blur.** A VAE's decoder averages over a continuous distribution,
and averaging images produces blur. Snapping to a codebook entry removes the
averaging — each token has a definite decoded meaning.

**Why it mattered historically.** Discrete tokens let you model images and audio
with the same autoregressive machinery used for text. Almost every image-token
generative system descends from this idea.

**The characteristic failure.** *Codebook collapse* — most entries go unused and
the effective vocabulary is a fraction of $K$. Mitigations include EMA codebook
updates, restarting dead codes from encoder outputs, and $\ell_2$-normalising the
codebook.

**Use it when.** You want discrete tokens for a downstream sequence model, or
you need sharp reconstructions with a compact latent.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="CNN / ViT">

These are *backbones* — the architectural substrate — rather than objectives, and
you can train either with any of the objectives on this page.

**CNN (e.g. ResNet).** Builds in translation equivariance and locality. A cat is
a cat wherever it appears, and nearby pixels matter more than distant ones. Those
priors are correct for natural images, which is why CNNs are far more
sample-efficient on small datasets.

**ViT.** Splits the image into patches and runs self-attention. Assumes almost
nothing about images — which is a liability at small scale and an asset at large
scale, because the model can learn priors from data rather than inheriting ones
that may be wrong.

**The crossover.** ViTs underperform CNNs on ImageNet-scale data and overtake
them given roughly 100M+ images. If your dataset is small and you are not
starting from pretrained weights, a CNN is very likely the better choice, and
this is still true in 2026.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Contrastive">

**What it does.** Take two augmented views of the same image (crop, colour jitter,
blur). Require their embeddings to be close. Require embeddings of *different*
images to be far. That is the whole method.

**What the augmentations really are.** They are your specification of what should
be ignored. Crop-invariance says position doesn't matter; colour-jitter says hue
doesn't matter. **Choosing augmentations is choosing what the encoder will be
blind to** — which is a feature for object recognition and a catastrophe for a
task where colour is the label. SimCLR's ablations showed augmentation choice
dominates architecture choice, and that finding has held up.

**The cost.** Negatives. You need many, and they must be in the same batch (SimCLR
used batches up to 8192) or in a maintained queue (MoCo). This is the practical
barrier to using contrastive methods without a lot of hardware.

**Use it when.** Retrieval, search, deduplication, matching, or anything where you
will compare embeddings with cosine similarity. CLIP-style cross-modal training is
the same recipe with image/text pairs as the positive.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="JEPA / self-distill">

**What it does.** Two encoders — a student and an EMA copy of it called the
teacher. Show the student a partial or augmented view, ask it to predict the
teacher's embedding of another view. Gradients flow only through the student
(`stop-gradient` on the teacher).

**Why no negatives are needed.** The asymmetry does the work. Because the teacher
updates slowly and receives no gradient, the student cannot reach the trivial
constant solution by dragging the target to meet it. The target is, in effect, a
moving but non-cooperative goalpost.

**Why this is unsatisfying.** It empirically works and there is no theory that
predicts in advance whether a given asymmetry suffices. This is stated plainly in
the literature and remains true.

**I-JEPA specifically.** Rather than augmenting, it masks: predict the
representations of several target blocks from a single context block, in latent
space. No hand-designed augmentations at all, which removes the "you chose the
invariances" objection that dogs contrastive methods.

**Use it when.** You want general-purpose features for unknown downstream tasks
and you have a lot of unlabelled data. This is the current best answer for that
situation.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Straight comparison

| Family | Loss computed in | Needs negatives | Latent geometry | Sharp reconstructions | Collapse risk | Best at |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AE / DAE | data space | no | arbitrary, gappy | moderate | none | anomaly detection, denoising |
| VAE | data space | no | smooth, samplable | no (blurry) | posterior collapse | generation, interpolation |
| VQ-VAE | data space | no | discrete grid | yes | codebook collapse | tokenising for sequence models |
| Supervised CNN/ViT | label space | no | class-clustered | n/a | none | one known task, many labels |
| Contrastive | latent space | **yes** | hypersphere, cosine-valid | n/a | prevented by negatives | retrieval, matching, cross-modal |
| JEPA / self-distill | latent space | no | cosine-valid | n/a | **central difficulty** | general transfer features |

<Aside type="danger" title="The metric mismatch">
Cosine similarity is meaningful in contrastive and JEPA spaces because the
objective explicitly shapes angular geometry, usually on a normalised sphere. It
is **not** automatically meaningful in AE or VAE latent spaces, where nothing
constrained the norms or angles. Running a cosine-similarity nearest-neighbour
search over VAE latents is a real and common mistake — it returns something, it
just doesn't mean what you think.
</Aside>

## Autoencoder and the PCA connection

For an encoder $E_\theta$ and decoder $D_\psi$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AE}} = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\|x - D_\psi(E_\theta(x))\|_2^2\big].
$$

A result worth knowing because it calibrates expectations: **a linear
autoencoder with squared loss recovers the principal subspace of the data.** With
$E(x)=Wx$, $D(z)=W^{\top}z$ and $W \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$, the optimum spans
the same subspace as the top-$k$ eigenvectors of the covariance — though not
necessarily the eigenvectors themselves, since any rotation within the subspace
is equally optimal.

The practical reading: **the non-linearity is doing all the work.** If your
autoencoder isn't beating PCA on your problem, it has not learned anything a
linear method couldn't, and you should check your training rather than your
architecture.

The denoising variant replaces $x$ at the input with a corrupted $\tilde{x} \sim
q(\tilde{x} \mid x)$ while keeping the clean $x$ as the target:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DAE}} = \mathbb{E}_{x}\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}\sim q(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\|x - D_\psi(E_\theta(\tilde{x}))\|_2^2\big].
$$

Vincent et al. showed this is equivalent to learning the score of the data
distribution in the small-noise limit — the same object that modern diffusion
models estimate. The lineage from DAE to diffusion is direct.

## VAE: the ELBO, derived

We want $\log p_\psi(x)$ but cannot compute it, because it requires marginalising
the latent. Introduce a variational posterior $q_\theta(z \mid x)$ — this is the
encoder — and apply Jensen:

$$
\log p_\psi(x) = \log \int p_\psi(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz
= \log \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\psi(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\right]
\;\geq\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}},
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}}(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\psi(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularisation}}.
$$

The gap between $\log p_\psi(x)$ and the bound is exactly
$\mathrm{KL}(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\psi(z\mid x))$ — the bound is tight
precisely when the encoder matches the true posterior.

**The reparameterisation trick.** Sampling $z \sim q_\theta(z\mid x)$ is not
differentiable in $\theta$. Rewrite it as a deterministic function of a
parameter-free noise variable:

$$
z = \mu_\theta(x) + \sigma_\theta(x) \odot \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
$$

Now the randomness sits in $\epsilon$, which carries no parameters, and gradients
pass straight through $\mu_\theta$ and $\sigma_\theta$. Without this the variance
of the gradient estimator is so high that training is impractical — this trick,
not the ELBO, is the reason VAEs work.

For diagonal Gaussians against a standard normal prior the KL is closed-form:

$$
\mathrm{KL} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\Big).
$$

**Posterior collapse.** If the decoder is powerful enough to model $x$ without
using $z$, the optimiser drives $q_\theta(z\mid x) \to p(z)$, sending the KL to
zero and making the latent carry no information. Look at the expression above: an
encoder that outputs $\mu_j = 0, \sigma_j = 1$ for every input scores perfectly on
the KL term. Collapse is not a bug; it is a local optimum the objective actively
rewards. Mitigations — KL annealing, free bits, weakening the decoder — all amount
to preventing the optimiser from taking that shortcut early.

## Beta-VAE and the rate–distortion reading

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\beta} = \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p_\psi(x\mid z)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).
$$

The KL term is the *rate* — bits per sample the latent carries. The
reconstruction term is negative *distortion*. So $\beta$ is a position on a
rate–distortion curve, and you cannot be at two positions at once. This reframing
is why "$\beta$-VAE gives disentanglement" is better stated as "$\beta$-VAE
restricts the rate, and under some conditions the surviving bits align with
generative factors" — the disentanglement claim has been contested, and the
rate–distortion claim has not.

## VQ-VAE: quantisation and the straight-through estimator

Given a codebook $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times D}$, quantise:

$$
z_q(x) = \mathbf{e}_k, \qquad k = \arg\min_j \big\|z_e(x) - \mathbf{e}_j\big\|_2.
$$

The $\arg\min$ has zero gradient almost everywhere, so the decoder's gradient
cannot reach the encoder. VQ-VAE simply **copies the gradient across the
quantisation step** — the straight-through estimator — and adds two terms to keep
the two sides from drifting apart:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\log p(x \mid z_q(x))}_{\text{reconstruction}}
+ \underbrace{\big\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e(x)] - \mathbf{e}\big\|_2^2}_{\text{codebook loss}}
+ \beta \underbrace{\big\|z_e(x) - \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{e}]\big\|_2^2}_{\text{commitment loss}}.
$$

Read the two $\operatorname{sg}$ placements carefully — they are the whole design.
The codebook loss moves *codebook entries* toward encoder outputs; the commitment
loss moves *encoder outputs* toward codebook entries. Splitting them lets the two
move at different rates, with $\beta$ (typically 0.25) controlling how strongly
the encoder is held to its assigned code.

## Contrastive: InfoNCE

For an anchor $x$, a positive $x^+$, and $N-1$ negatives $x_i^-$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z^+)/\tau\big)}{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z^+)/\tau\big) + \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z_i^-)/\tau\big)},
$$

with $\operatorname{sim}$ the cosine similarity and $\tau$ a temperature.

Two facts about this loss that are worth carrying around:

**It bounds mutual information.** $I(z; z^+) \geq \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}}$.
Note the $\log N$: the bound is capped by batch size, which is the formal reason
large batches help and small ones plateau. It also means the bound is loose at
practical $N$, so don't over-read it.

**Temperature controls how hard the hard negatives hit.** Small $\tau$ sharpens
the softmax so gradient concentrates on the most similar negatives; large $\tau$
spreads it. $\tau$ is a genuinely sensitive hyperparameter, not a formality —
typical values sit around 0.07–0.2.

And the decomposition from Wang & Isola, which is the most useful practical
handle on this loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^+)}\big[\|z - z^+\|_2^{\alpha}\big], \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{unif}} = \log \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[e^{-t\|z_x - z_y\|_2^2}\big].
$$

Asymptotically InfoNCE optimises exactly these two, and measuring them separately
diagnoses a failing encoder far better than the loss value does — good alignment
with bad uniformity means you are collapsing; the reverse means your positives
are too weak.

## JEPA: prediction with an asymmetric target

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{JEPA}} = \sum_{i} \Big\| P_\phi\big(E_\theta(x_{\text{ctx}}),\, m_i\big) - \operatorname{sg}\big[E_{\bar\theta}(x_{\text{tgt}}^{(i)})\big] \Big\|_1,
\qquad \bar\theta \leftarrow \lambda\bar\theta + (1-\lambda)\theta .
$$

Here $m_i$ positions the $i$-th target block and $\bar\theta$ is the EMA teacher,
typically with $\lambda \approx 0.996 \to 1.0$ on a schedule.

**Why this does not collapse.** Consider the degenerate solution $E_\theta \equiv
c$. For the loss to be zero the *teacher* must also output $c$ — but the teacher
is a lagged average, so it arrives at $c$ only after the student has already been
constant for many steps, during which the loss is non-zero and gradients push
away. The stop-gradient means the student can never shortcut this by pulling the
target down. That is the mechanism, and it is an argument about dynamics, not a
proof: there is no guarantee, and collapse does occur when $\lambda$ is set too
low.

<Aside type="note" title="Read this as three anti-collapse strategies">
Reconstruction forces $I(Z;X)$ above a floor by requiring invertibility.
Contrastive learning adds an explicit repulsive term. Self-distillation uses
temporal asymmetry. Every method on this page uses exactly one of these three,
and knowing which tells you immediately what its failure mode is.
</Aside>

## Where things stand, as of 2026

**Masked reconstruction did not die, and that is genuinely awkward for the
latent-prediction thesis.** MAE reconstructs raw pixels with a 75% mask ratio and
remains a strong pretraining method. The theoretical argument says it should waste
capacity on irrelevant detail; the benchmarks say it is competitive. The
reconciliation people generally offer — that an aggressive mask ratio makes the
task semantic enough that pixel-level detail stops dominating the gradient — is
plausible but not established. Anyone presenting this as settled is overstating.

**Encoder-only text models were prematurely written off.** After 2020 the field
moved to decoder-only LLMs for nearly everything. ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024)
showed encoder-only architectures had simply not received the same modernisation
— rotary embeddings, longer context, better data — rather than being fundamentally
inferior. Controlled 2025 comparisons at matched parameter counts found encoders
still ahead on classification and retrieval. For embeddings specifically,
encoder-only remains the better default.

**Vision encoders have consolidated around self-distillation.** DINO-family
features are the practical default for general-purpose visual representation, and
they exhibit properties nobody trained for — attention maps that segment objects
without any segmentation supervision. That emergent behaviour is the strongest
qualitative evidence that the objective is capturing something structural.

## Open problems

- **No good evaluation.** Linear probing rewards linearly accessible information
  and is blind to structure a non-linear head could exploit; full fine-tuning
  measures the fine-tuning recipe as much as the encoder. Alignment/uniformity is
  better but is defined relative to a chosen augmentation family, which begs the
  question.

- **Anti-collapse is empirical.** We have three strategies that work and no theory
  that says in advance whether a proposed asymmetry is sufficient. Every new
  method's collapse-resistance is established by running it.

- **Augmentation choice is unprincipled.** Contrastive methods require you to
  specify invariances up front, and getting that wrong silently destroys
  information you needed. I-JEPA's masking sidesteps it; whether masking
  introduces its own implicit invariances is not well characterised.

- **Encoder spaces are not interoperable.** Same data, same objective, different
  seed produces incomparable spaces. This blocks modular reuse and forces
  hierarchical systems to train levels jointly in a shared space rather than
  composing independently trained parts.

- **Scaling laws for encoders are poorly mapped** compared to generative models.
  We know roughly that more data helps; we do not have the clean
  compute-optimal curves that exist for language modelling.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Kingma & Welling (2013)** — for the reparameterisation trick specifically.
   Section 2.4 is the part that matters.
2. **van den Oord et al. (2017)** — read it for the straight-through estimator and
   the two-term loss split; it is short and the design is unusually legible.
3. **Chen et al. (2020), SimCLR** — read the augmentation ablations, not the
   method. They are the most transferable finding in the paper.
4. **Assran et al. (2023), I-JEPA** — the current reference point for
   augmentation-free representation learning.
