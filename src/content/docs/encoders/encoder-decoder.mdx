<Abstract>
Here "encoder" names a *position*, not a technique: the half of a model that
reads input and produces a representation, as opposed to the half that emits
output one token at a time. The whole history of this design is one problem and
its fixes — a fixed-size summary cannot carry a long input, attention removed that
limit, and the field then spent a decade working out whether you need the encoder
half at all. As of 2026 the answer is: for generation, no; for understanding and
retrieval, yes.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The encoder–decoder split exists because input and output are asymmetric.** The input is fully available and can be read in any order; the output must be produced left to right. Bidirectional attention on one side and causal masking on the other is the natural consequence.
- **The bottleneck was the original sin, and attention was the fix.** Compressing an arbitrarily long sentence into one fixed vector degrades with length. Bahdanau et al. let the decoder look back at every input position — the single most consequential change in the lineage.
- **Encoder-only and decoder-only differ by one thing: the attention mask.** Not by size, not by training data, not by architecture family. Bidirectional versus causal is the entire distinction, and everything else follows from it.
- **Decoder-only won generation; encoder-only did not lose understanding.** The 2020–2023 consensus that decoders subsume encoders has partly reversed on the strength of matched-parameter comparisons published in 2024–2025.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## Reading versus writing

Think about translating a sentence. You do two different things.

First you **read** the whole source sentence. You can look at any word in any
order, jump back to check whether *bank* meant a river or a vault, re-read the
ending before deciding the tense. Nothing stops you, because the whole sentence is
sitting there.

Then you **write** the translation. Now you are constrained: you produce words in
order, and when you choose word five you cannot consult word seven, because word
seven doesn't exist yet.

**Encoder = the reading half. Decoder = the writing half.** That's it. And the
asymmetry in how you're allowed to look around is exactly the asymmetry that
shows up in the architecture, as a difference in attention masking.

## The original design and its flaw

The 2014 systems worked like this: the encoder read the source sentence word by
word, ending with a single fixed-size vector — the *thought vector* — meant to
capture the whole meaning. The decoder then generated the translation from that
vector alone.

It worked, which was remarkable. But there was an obvious problem, and it showed
up exactly where you'd expect: **long sentences**. One vector of 512 numbers has
to hold a three-word sentence and a sixty-word sentence equally well. It cannot.
Translation quality visibly degraded as sentences got longer, and the degradation
was sharp rather than gradual.

<Aside type="tip" title="Why this history is worth knowing">
Attention was not invented as a general-purpose mechanism. It was invented as a
patch for one specific failure — long-sentence translation quality — and only
later turned out to be the thing worth keeping. *Attention Is All You Need* is
literally the observation that you could throw away the recurrent parts and keep
only the patch.
</Aside>

## Three shapes, one difference

Modern transformers come in three configurations, and people talk about them as
if they were three different architectures. They are not. **They differ in the
attention mask.**

## What each shape can and cannot do

<Steps>

1. **Encoder-only.** Every token attends to every other token, in both
   directions. To decide what *bank* means, the model can use the word three
   positions later. This is why encoder representations are better: they are
   genuinely contextual in both directions. It also means the model cannot be
   used for autoregressive generation — there is no notion of "next token" when
   every position already sees the future. Training is masked language modelling:
   hide 15% of tokens, predict them from both sides.

2. **Decoder-only.** Each token attends only to itself and earlier tokens. That
   restriction is what makes next-token prediction a well-posed objective — the
   model never sees the answer. Every position is simultaneously a training
   example, which makes the objective extremely sample-efficient, and this is a
   large part of why decoder-only scaled so well.

3. **Encoder–decoder.** The encoder processes the input bidirectionally. The
   decoder generates causally, but each decoder layer additionally runs
   *cross-attention*: queries from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder
   output. The input is read with full context; the output is produced in order.

</Steps>

## The case for and against each

| | Encoder-only | Decoder-only | Encoder–decoder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can generate text | no | yes | yes |
| Bidirectional input context | yes | no | yes (input side) |
| Training signal density | ~15% of tokens | 100% of tokens | ~100% (denoising) |
| Parameter efficiency for understanding | **best** | weakest | middling |
| Inference cost for seq2seq | n/a | reprocesses prompt each step | encoder runs once |
| Prompting / in-context learning | no | **yes** | limited |
| Typical use | embeddings, classification, reranking | chat, code, general assistants | translation, summarisation, ASR |

<Aside type="caution" title="The training-signal asymmetry is underrated">
Masked language modelling supervises only the ~15% of positions you masked.
Next-token prediction supervises *every* position. For the same corpus, a
decoder-only model gets roughly 6× more gradient signal per pass. This is a real
efficiency advantage of decoders — and it is separate from, and often confused
with, the question of which produces better representations per parameter.
</Aside>

## When to actually use which

- **Semantic search, RAG retrieval, reranking, deduplication** → encoder-only.
  You need one vector per document and cheap cosine comparison. A decoder-only
  LLM can produce embeddings, but at matched quality it costs far more.
- **Classification, NER, token tagging** → encoder-only, unless you need
  zero-shot behaviour, in which case an LLM's prompting ability may outweigh its
  inefficiency.
- **Anything conversational, open-ended, or requiring in-context learning** →
  decoder-only. Not close.
- **Fixed input → fixed output transformations at scale** (translation,
  summarisation, speech recognition) → encoder–decoder is still competitive and
  often cheaper at inference, because the encoder runs once rather than being
  reprocessed at every generated token.

## Attention, and what the mask does

Scaled dot-product attention:

$$
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V .
$$

Everything architectural on this page reduces to the choice of $M \in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$:

$$
M^{\text{enc}}_{ij} = 0 \;\;\forall i,j,
\qquad
M^{\text{dec}}_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & j \leq i \\ -\infty & j > i \end{cases}
$$

The $-\infty$ entries become zero after the softmax, severing those connections
exactly. **Encoder-only and decoder-only transformers are the same function with
different $M$.** Any claim that one is architecturally more expressive than the
other has to route through this fact.

**Why $\sqrt{d_k}$.** If $q$ and $k$ have i.i.d. zero-mean unit-variance
components, then $q\cdot k$ has variance $d_k$. Without rescaling, the logits grow
like $\sqrt{d_k}$, the softmax saturates, and gradients vanish. Dividing by
$\sqrt{d_k}$ restores unit variance. It is not a tuned constant.

**Cross-attention** is the same operation with the sources split:

$$
Q = H^{\text{dec}} W_Q, \qquad K = H^{\text{enc}} W_K, \qquad V = H^{\text{enc}} W_V,
$$

with no mask, since the decoder is permitted to see the entire input. This is
the only structural difference between an encoder–decoder and two stacked
transformers.

## The bottleneck, quantified

In the pre-attention design the decoder is conditioned on a single vector $c =
h_T \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$:

$$
p(y_1,\dots,y_{T'} \mid x_1,\dots,x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T'} p\big(y_t \mid y_{<t},\, c\big).
$$

The mutual information the decoder can possibly exploit is bounded by the channel
capacity of $c$, which is fixed at $O(d)$ regardless of $T$. Meanwhile the entropy
of the source grows roughly linearly in $T$. So for $T$ beyond some threshold,
information loss is not a modelling deficiency — it is forced by the architecture.

Attention replaces the single $c$ with a per-step context vector:

$$
c_t = \sum_{i=1}^{T} \alpha_{ti}\, h_i, \qquad \alpha_{ti} = \frac{\exp(e_{ti})}{\sum_{j}\exp(e_{tj})}, \qquad e_{ti} = a(s_{t-1}, h_i).
$$

Now the decoder has access to $\{h_1,\dots,h_T\}$, whose total capacity scales
with $T$. The bound disappears. Bahdanau et al.'s central empirical result is
exactly this: BLEU no longer degrades with source length.

## MLM versus CLM as objectives

Masked language modelling, with $\mathcal{M}$ the masked index set:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta\big(x_i \mid x_{\setminus \mathcal{M}}\big).
$$

Causal language modelling:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CLM}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p_\theta\big(x_i \mid x_{<i}\big).
$$

Two structural differences, both consequential:

**Conditioning set.** MLM conditions on both sides; CLM on the left only. The MLM
representation of token $i$ can encode information from token $i+5$; the CLM
representation cannot. For any task where the answer depends on later context —
which is most classification and retrieval — this is a genuine advantage, and it
is not recoverable by scale.

**Signal density.** MLM supervises $|\mathcal{M}| \approx 0.15n$ positions per
sequence; CLM supervises all $n$. Per unit of compute, CLM extracts more gradient.

These pull in opposite directions, which is precisely why the question "which is
better?" has no unconditional answer and why the 2025 controlled comparisons
matter more than the earlier informal consensus did.

## The encoder-only revival, as of 2026

Between roughly 2020 and 2023 the field's working assumption was that
decoder-only LLMs subsume encoders: if a model can do everything by prompting,
why keep a specialised understanding architecture? Encoder-only research largely
stopped, and BERT-family models stayed frozen at their 2019 recipes while
decoders absorbed a decade of improvements.

Three results have complicated that picture:

**ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024)** applied the accumulated decoder-side
engineering — rotary position embeddings, 8192-token context, modern data mixtures
and training recipes — to an encoder-only architecture. The result was
substantially better than BERT and RoBERTa on classification and retrieval at
comparable size. The finding is not that encoders are secretly superior; it is
that the comparison everyone had been making was against an un-modernised
baseline.

**Seq vs Seq (Weller et al., 2025)** is the controlled experiment the debate
needed: paired encoder and decoder models trained on identical data with identical
recipes, differing only in objective and mask. At matched parameter counts,
encoders led on classification and retrieval; decoders led on generation. Neither
subsumed the other, and cross-objective adaptation did not close the gap.

**Should We Still Pretrain Encoders with MLM? (Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2025)**
examined whether the MLM objective itself is the source of the advantage or
whether CLM pretraining followed by adaptation would do. The nuanced answer —
that a CLM-then-MLM sequence can be compute-efficient — suggests the two
objectives are complementary rather than competing.

<Aside type="note" title="How to hold this">
The honest summary is *"for retrieval and classification at a fixed parameter
budget, encoder-only is currently the better default"* — not *"encoders beat
decoders"*. The comparison is budget-dependent, and a sufficiently large
decoder-only model will outperform a small encoder on almost anything. The claim
is about efficiency, and efficiency claims have to state their budget.
</Aside>

## Where encoder–decoder still lives

Largely outside text-to-text NLP, where the input is a different modality from the
output and the asymmetry is unavoidable:

- **Speech recognition** — Whisper is encoder–decoder, because audio frames and
  text tokens are not the same kind of object.
- **Any fixed-input, repeated-decode workload** — the encoder runs once and its
  output is reused for every generated token, which is a real inference saving
  over a decoder-only model that reprocesses the prompt.
- **Diffusion conditioning** — text encoders feed cross-attention in image
  generators. This is an encoder–decoder structure even though nobody calls it
  one.

## Open problems

- **Nobody has cleanly separated objective from mask.** Encoder-only models are
  bidirectional *and* MLM-trained; decoder-only are causal *and* CLM-trained. The
  2025 work begins to disentangle these, but the four-way factorial comparison
  does not exist at scale.

- **Bidirectional generation remains unsolved.** Diffusion language models and
  masked-generative approaches attempt it, and none currently match
  autoregressive quality. If one did, the encoder/decoder distinction would
  largely dissolve.

- **Long context has not settled the bottleneck question, only moved it.**
  Attention's quadratic cost means very long inputs are once again compressed —
  now into KV caches, sparse patterns, or state-space summaries. The 2014
  bottleneck argument applies to those compressions too, and it is not obvious
  the field has internalised that.

- **Encoder-only models cannot do in-context learning**, and there is no known
  reason this must be so. It may be an artefact of the training objective rather
  than of bidirectionality.
