<Abstract>
Models consume numbers; a lot of real data is words. Feature encoding is the
translation step, and it is the least glamorous and most consequential part of
applied tabular ML. The methods have been stable for twenty years. What is *not*
stable is practitioners' handling of target encoding, which leaks the label into
the features unless computed inside a cross-validation fold — producing models
that validate at 0.95 AUC and fail in production. That single failure mode is the
reason this page exists.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The only question that matters first is cardinality.** Under ~15 categories, one-hot and stop thinking. Over a few thousand, one-hot is not an option and the interesting trade-offs begin.
- **Ordinal encoding asserts an ordering.** Encoding `{red, green, blue}` as `{0,1,2}` tells a linear model that green sits between red and blue. If that is false, you have injected a lie into your features, and tree models will happily exploit it.
- **Target encoding is the strongest method and the most dangerous.** It leaks by construction. Computed correctly — out-of-fold, inside the pipeline — it is excellent. Computed on the full dataset, it produces a model that has memorised the answer.
- **Tree models and linear models want different encodings.** Gradient-boosted trees handle ordinal integers fine and are hurt by one-hot's sparsity; linear models are the reverse. Choosing an encoding without knowing the model is choosing blind.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The problem

You have a table. One column is `city`, with values like `London`, `Mumbai`,
`São Paulo`. Your model does arithmetic. `London` is not a number.

So you need a rule for turning cities into numbers. The obvious rule — number
them 1, 2, 3 in alphabetical order — creates a problem that is easy to miss:
you have just told the model that Mumbai is *between* London and São Paulo, and
that São Paulo is *three times* London. Neither statement means anything. A
linear model will take both entirely seriously.

**Feature encoding is the study of rules that don't accidentally assert things
you didn't mean.**

## The trap, stated before you can fall into it

Target encoding replaces each category with the average outcome for that
category. `London → 0.31` means "31% of London rows were positive".

Consider a category that appears exactly once, in a row where the label is 1. Its
target encoding is 1.0. The feature now *contains the label for that row*. Your
model does not need to learn anything — it can read the answer off the feature.
Validation accuracy will look extraordinary. Production accuracy will be chance.

<Aside type="danger" title="This is the single most common serious bug in applied tabular ML">
If you compute target encoding on your full dataset and *then* split into train
and test, every fold is contaminated. The fix is not optional and not subtle:
**compute the encoding using only the training portion, inside the
cross-validation loop.** In scikit-learn terms, it must be a step in a `Pipeline`,
never a transformation applied to the dataframe beforehand.
</Aside>

## Choosing: a procedure

<Steps>

1. **Count the distinct values.** This single number drives most of the decision.

2. **Under ~15 categories** → one-hot. It is unambiguous, it makes no false
   assertions, and the dimensional cost is negligible. Do not overthink it.

3. **15 to a few thousand** → depends on your model. Gradient-boosted trees:
   native categorical handling if available (LightGBM, CatBoost), otherwise
   ordinal. Linear or neural: target encoding done properly, or learned
   embeddings.

4. **Genuinely ordered categories** (`small < medium < large`, `bronze < silver <
   gold`) → ordinal, with the codes reflecting the real order. This is the one
   case where ordinal is not a lie, and it is strictly better than one-hot
   because it hands the model a fact it would otherwise have to learn.

5. **Tens of thousands or more** (user IDs, URLs, product SKUs) → hashing if you
   need a fixed memory budget and streaming; learned embeddings if you have a
   neural model and enough data per category.

6. **Unseen categories at inference time** → hashing handles this natively;
   everything else needs an explicit `unknown` bucket, decided in advance.

</Steps>

## Model-dependence, which people skip

The right encoding depends on what consumes it, and the two dominant model
families want opposite things.

| | Linear / neural | Gradient-boosted trees |
|---|---|---|
| One-hot | good — each category gets its own weight | **poor** — sparse binary splits waste tree depth |
| Ordinal | **bad** — imposes a false linear relationship | fine — trees split on thresholds, order is largely irrelevant |
| Target | good | good — often the strongest option |
| Embeddings | **best**, learned jointly | not applicable directly |

The one-hot-hurts-trees result surprises people. The reason: a tree splitting on a
one-hot column can only ask "is it London or not?", so isolating one of 100
categories takes up to 100 sequential splits, and each split sees fewer samples
than the last. Native categorical handling asks "is it in `{London, Paris,
Berlin}`?" in a single split.

## Regularising target encoding

Raw category means are unusable for rare categories — one observation gives you a
mean of 0 or 1 with no reliability. The standard fix is smoothing toward the
global mean, with the weight determined by how much data the category has:

$$
\hat{y}_c = \frac{n_c \cdot \bar{y}_c + m \cdot \bar{y}}{n_c + m},
$$

where $n_c$ is the count for category $c$, $\bar{y}_c$ its mean, $\bar{y}$ the
global mean, and $m$ a smoothing constant. A category seen once is pulled almost
entirely to the global mean; a category seen ten thousand times keeps its own.

This is Micci-Barreca's 2001 scheme and it remains the standard. But note what it
does **not** fix: smoothing reduces variance, it does not remove leakage. You
still need out-of-fold computation.

<Tier level="formal" />

## One-hot and the dummy-variable trap

One-hot maps $c \in \{1,\dots,k\}$ to $e_c \in \{0,1\}^k$. Distances are
uninformative by construction:

$$
\|e_i - e_j\|_2 = \sqrt{2} \quad \text{for all } i \neq j.
$$

Every pair of categories is equidistant. This is exactly right when you have no
prior similarity information, and exactly wrong when you do — it is why one-hot
cannot express that London and Manchester are more alike than London and Mumbai.

**The dummy-variable trap.** The $k$ columns are linearly dependent — they sum to
the all-ones vector. With an intercept, the design matrix is rank-deficient and
$X^{\top}X$ is singular, so ordinary least squares has no unique solution. Drop
one column (`drop_first=True`) to restore full rank.

Two important caveats on that advice, both routinely mishandled:

- **With $\ell_2$ regularisation, do not drop.** Ridge already handles the
  singularity, and dropping a level makes the penalty asymmetric across
  categories — the dropped level is treated differently from the rest for no
  principled reason.
- **For tree models, never drop.** There is no matrix inversion, and dropping a
  level simply hides a category from the splitter.

## Ordinal encoding, formally

$$
\phi_{\text{ord}}(c) = \pi(c), \qquad \pi: \{1,\dots,k\} \to \{0,\dots,k-1\}.
$$

For a linear model $f(x) = w\,\phi_{\text{ord}}(c) + b$, the encoding constrains
the fitted effects to be *collinear in the assigned order*:

$$
f(c_{i+1}) - f(c_i) = w \quad \text{for all } i,
$$

i.e. the model is forced to believe every adjacent pair of categories differs by
the same amount. When the ordering is real and roughly evenly spaced, this is a
useful constraint that saves parameters. When it is arbitrary, it is a
misspecification the model cannot escape.

For trees the situation is different but not harmless. A tree can carve
$\{0,\dots,k-1\}$ into arbitrary intervals, so it can recover any grouping that is
*contiguous in $\pi$*. Groupings that are not contiguous require multiple splits.
So the arbitrary ordering does not make the model wrong, but it does make some
groupings cheaper to express than others — a real, if mild, inductive bias you did
not intend.

## Target encoding and the leakage bound

$$
\phi_{\text{tgt}}(c) = \mathbb{E}\big[Y \mid C = c\big], \quad \text{estimated as} \quad \hat{\phi}(c) = \frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i : c_i = c} y_i .
$$

Consider the contribution of row $i$ to its own encoded value. Decompose:

$$
\hat{\phi}(c_i) = \frac{y_i}{n_c} + \frac{n_c - 1}{n_c}\cdot \hat{\phi}_{-i}(c_i),
$$

where $\hat{\phi}_{-i}$ excludes row $i$. The first term is direct label leakage,
and its magnitude is $1/n_c$. So:

- $n_c = 1$: the feature *is* the label. Complete leakage.
- $n_c = 10$: 10% of the feature value is the row's own label.
- $n_c = 10\,000$: negligible.

**Leakage severity is inversely proportional to category frequency** — which is
why the problem is worst exactly where target encoding is most attractive, on
high-cardinality columns with long tails.

The correct estimator is the leave-one-out or, better, out-of-fold version:

$$
\hat{\phi}^{\text{OOF}}(c_i) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{c}^{\,\setminus k(i)}|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_{c}^{\,\setminus k(i)}} y_j,
$$

computed from folds other than the one containing row $i$. CatBoost goes further
with *ordered target statistics*: impose a random permutation and encode each row
using only rows earlier in that permutation, which mimics a time-ordered stream
and removes the leakage exactly rather than approximately.

<Aside type="caution" title="Leave-one-out is not as safe as it looks">
Plain LOO target encoding removes the direct term but introduces a subtler
problem: for a binary target within a small category, the LOO value is a
deterministic decreasing function of $y_i$. A tree can invert it. Ordered target
statistics or proper K-fold OOF are the safe options; LOO is not.
</Aside>

## Feature hashing

$$
\phi_{\text{hash}}(c)_j = \sum_{c' : h(c') = j} \xi(c'), \qquad h: \mathcal{C} \to \{1,\dots,b\}, \quad \xi: \mathcal{C} \to \{\pm 1\}.
$$

The signed variant $\xi$ is the part that makes the guarantee work. Weinberger et
al. showed the hashed inner product is unbiased:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\langle \phi_{\text{hash}}(x), \phi_{\text{hash}}(x') \rangle\big] = \langle x, x' \rangle,
$$

because collisions contribute $+1$ and $-1$ with equal probability and cancel in
expectation. Without the signs, collisions would add and the inner product would
be systematically inflated.

Collision probability for $k$ categories in $b$ buckets is approximately $1 -
e^{-k^2/2b}$ — the birthday bound. Practical consequence: to keep pairwise
collisions rare you need $b$ substantially larger than $k^2$, which is often
infeasible. In practice one accepts collisions and relies on the unbiasedness, and
the resulting model is genuinely worse than a collision-free encoding — hashing
buys memory and streaming capability at a real accuracy cost.

## Learned embeddings

$$
\phi_{\text{emb}}(c) = E_c, \qquad E \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}, \quad d \ll k,
$$

with $E$ trained by backpropagation alongside the model. Since the embedding
layer is a matrix multiply against a one-hot vector, this is *exactly* a one-hot
encoding followed by a linear layer — the difference is that the linear layer is
learned end-to-end, so it discovers a similarity structure that one-hot alone
cannot express.

A common heuristic for dimensionality, from the entity-embeddings literature and
the fast.ai defaults:

$$
d = \min\big(600,\; \lceil 1.6 \cdot k^{0.56} \rceil\big).
$$

Treat this as a starting point, not a result — it is empirical folklore, widely
used, with no theoretical backing.

The real requirement is data. Each category needs enough occurrences for its row
of $E$ to receive meaningful gradient. Categories appearing a handful of times end
up with essentially random vectors, which is worse than target encoding's
smoothed estimate for those same categories. **Embeddings are the best method
when data is plentiful per category and among the worst when it is not.**

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**The methods are settled; the practice is not.** Nothing fundamental has changed
since roughly 2017. What has changed is tooling: scikit-learn added a native
`TargetEncoder` with internal cross-fitting in 1.3, which makes the safe path the
default path for the first time. That is a bigger deal for real-world model
quality than any new method would be.

**Native categorical handling has largely won for trees.** LightGBM and CatBoost
handle categorical features internally, using approaches that are both more
accurate and less error-prone than manual preprocessing. If you are using
gradient-boosted trees on tabular data in 2026, the default advice is to use the
library's native support and not encode manually at all.

**Tabular foundation models are the live question.** Approaches that pretrain a
transformer across many tables and treat categorical values as text tokens
sidestep the encoding question entirely — the model reads `London` as a string. On
small datasets these are competitive with tuned gradient boosting. Whether they
displace the classical pipeline at scale is genuinely unsettled, and I would not
bet either way yet.

## Open problems

- **Extreme cardinality is unsolved.** A column with millions of categories and a
  heavy tail defeats every method here: hashing collides badly, target encoding's
  leakage scales as $1/n_c$ and the tail has $n_c$ of one or two, embeddings
  starve. This is the norm for user and item IDs in recommendation, and it is
  handled by domain-specific hacks rather than by any general method.

- **No principled dimensionality rule for embeddings.** The $1.6 k^{0.56}$
  heuristic is folklore. There is no derivation and no dataset-dependent theory.

- **Encoding choice is not part of model selection.** It is treated as
  preprocessing and tuned by hand, when it interacts strongly with model family,
  regularisation, and cardinality. Joint search over encoding and model is rare
  and expensive.

- **Concept drift in target encodings is under-addressed.** A target encoding is a
  statistic of the training distribution. When category-level base rates shift —
  which they do, constantly, in production — the encoding silently becomes wrong,
  and nothing in the standard pipeline detects it.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Micci-Barreca (2001)** — six pages, and the smoothing formula everyone still
   uses. Read it for the derivation, not the results.
2. **Weinberger et al. (2009)** — the unbiasedness argument for signed hashing is
   short and worth seeing in full.
3. **Prokhorenkova et al. (2017), CatBoost** — Section 3 on ordered target
   statistics is the definitive treatment of the leakage problem.
