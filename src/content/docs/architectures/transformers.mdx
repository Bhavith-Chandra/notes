<Abstract>
A transformer is a stack of one idea: let every position look at every other
position and decide, per position, what to pull in. That idea is
*differentiable dictionary lookup*, it costs $O(n^2)$, and the entire
engineering history since 2017 is a fight with that exponent. Almost nothing
about the core has changed; almost everything around it has.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Attention is a soft dictionary lookup.** Queries ask, keys advertise, values are returned — with a softmax deciding how much of each. Once you see it that way, multi-head, cross-attention, and causal masking are all trivial variations.
- **The quadratic cost is not an implementation flaw.** Every position attending to every other is what gives transformers their power; it is also why context length is expensive. Techniques that beat $O(n^2)$ do so by giving something up, and it is always worth asking what.
- **The 2017 architecture is not the 2026 architecture.** Post-norm → pre-norm, learned positions → RoPE, ReLU → SwiGLU, MHA → GQA. None of these were in the original paper and all of them are in every serious model today.
- **FlashAttention changed nothing mathematically and everything practically.** It computes the same function. It just stops moving intermediate results to slow memory — a reminder that on modern hardware, memory traffic, not FLOPs, is usually the binding constraint.

</KeyPoints>

## The problem attention solves

Read this sentence: *"The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big."*

What does *it* refer to? The trophy. Now change one word: *"...because it was too
small."* Now *it* is the suitcase.

To resolve *it*, you had to look at a word seven positions away and let its
meaning change how you interpret the pronoun. That is the whole problem. A model
processing words strictly left to right, carrying a fixed-size memory, has to
have *guessed in advance* that "trophy" would matter later and kept it around.
Most of the time it guesses wrong, and information about distant words decays.

**Attention removes the guessing.** At the moment the model processes *it*, it
reaches back and looks at every other word directly, weighting each by how
relevant it is right now. Nothing has to be predicted in advance and nothing
decays with distance — position 1 and position 500 are equally reachable.

## The lookup metaphor, which is exact rather than loose

Think of a dictionary. You have a **key** you look up and you get a **value**
back. `dictionary["cat"] → "a small furry animal"`. Exact match, one result.

Attention is the soft version:

- Every position emits a **query**: *what am I looking for?*
- Every position emits a **key**: *what do I have on offer?*
- Every position emits a **value**: *what I'll hand over if you pick me.*

Instead of an exact match, you compare your query against every key, turn those
similarities into percentages, and take a weighted blend of all the values. The
pronoun *it* emits a query meaning roughly "I need a noun that could be big"; the
key at *trophy* matches it better than the key at *suitcase*; so the returned
value is mostly trophy-flavoured.

<Aside type="tip" title="Why soft matters">
Hard lookup is not differentiable — you cannot take a gradient through "pick
entry 4". The softmax makes the choice a smooth blend, so the model can learn
*what to look for* by gradient descent. Differentiability is the reason for the
blend, not some deep claim about how meaning works.
</Aside>

## The bill

Every position compares itself against every other position. Ten words, a hundred
comparisons. A thousand words, a million. A hundred thousand words, ten billion —
per layer, per head.

This is the single fact that shapes everything downstream: why context windows
were tiny for years, why they got expensive rather than free, why there is an
entire research industry in "efficient attention", and why state-space models
keep getting a second look. Hold onto it.

<Steps>

1. **Project.** Multiply the input by three learned matrices to get $Q$, $K$, $V$.
   Same input, three different views of it.

2. **Score.** $QK^\top$ — every query dotted with every key. This is the $n \times
   n$ matrix, and it is where both the power and the cost live.

3. **Scale.** Divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$. Not a tuned hyperparameter; see the formal
   tier for why it is forced.

4. **Mask.** Add $-\infty$ to positions a query is not allowed to see. In a
   decoder that is everything in the future; in an encoder, nothing.

5. **Normalise.** Softmax each row, so the weights for one query sum to 1.

6. **Blend.** Multiply the weights by $V$. Each output position is a weighted
   average of all value vectors.

</Steps>

## Why multiple heads

One attention head produces one set of weights, so it can express one notion of
relevance. But "relevant" is not one thing — a word might need to attend to its
syntactic head, its coreferent, and the topic of the paragraph, all at once, and
one softmax cannot do three things.

Multi-head attention runs $h$ independent heads on lower-dimensional slices
($d_k = d/h$) and concatenates. Total compute is roughly unchanged; expressive
capacity is not. Interpretability work has found heads that do recognisable jobs —
tracking previous occurrences of a token, attending to the immediately preceding
word — though the tidy "one head, one function" story is much weaker than early
papers suggested, and most heads are not cleanly interpretable at all.

## What a block actually contains

A transformer block is attention plus a position-wise MLP, each wrapped in a
residual connection and a normalisation:

```text
x = x + Attention(Norm(x))     # pre-norm: normalise the branch input
x = x + MLP(Norm(x))
```

Two things worth noticing:

**The MLP holds most of the parameters.** With the standard $4\times$ expansion,
the MLP is roughly two-thirds of a block's weights. Attention gets the attention;
the MLP does much of the work, and interpretability research increasingly points
at MLP layers as where factual knowledge lives.

**The residual stream is the real object.** Every block *adds* to a running vector
rather than replacing it. The modern mental model is a shared bus that blocks read
from and write to, which is why you can often ablate a block with surprisingly
little damage.

<Aside type="caution" title="Pre-norm vs post-norm is not cosmetic">
The 2017 paper put the normalisation *after* the residual add. Training deep
post-norm transformers requires a learning-rate warmup, and without it they
diverge. Xiong et al. (2020) showed pre-norm keeps gradient magnitudes stable at
initialisation and removes the need for warmup entirely. Every large model since
is pre-norm. If you read the original paper and implement it literally, you will
hit this.
</Aside>

## What changed since 2017

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Positions">

Attention is permutation-equivariant — shuffle the input and the output shuffles
identically. It has no idea what order anything is in. Position information has
to be injected.

- **Sinusoidal / learned absolute (2017).** Add a position vector to the input
  embedding. Simple; extrapolates poorly beyond trained lengths.
- **RoPE (2021).** *Rotate* the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to
  position. Because a dot product between two rotated vectors depends only on the
  difference of the angles, attention becomes naturally relative. This is the
  current default in essentially every open LLM.
- **ALiBi (2021).** Skip embeddings entirely; add a linear penalty to attention
  scores proportional to distance. Extrapolates to longer sequences than trained
  on, which was its selling point.

RoPE won on quality; ALiBi's extrapolation argument was partly absorbed via RoPE
scaling tricks.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="KV cache & GQA">

At generation time, each new token re-attends to every previous token. Recomputing
their keys and values every step would be quadratic waste, so they are cached.

The cache is the memory problem in deployment. Its size is

$$
2 \cdot n_{\text{layers}} \cdot n_{\text{heads}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot n_{\text{tokens}} \cdot \text{bytes}
$$

which for a large model at long context runs to tens of gigabytes — often more
than the weights.

- **MQA (Shazeer, 2019).** All heads share *one* key/value head. Cache shrinks by
  a factor of $h$. Quality drops noticeably.
- **GQA (2023).** Group heads; each group shares a K/V head. A middle setting
  (say 8 groups for 64 heads) recovers most of the quality at most of the saving.
  This is the standard today.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="FlashAttention">

The naive implementation materialises the full $n \times n$ attention matrix in
GPU high-bandwidth memory, writes it, reads it back for the softmax, writes
again. For long sequences that traffic dominates runtime — the arithmetic is not
the bottleneck, the *memory movement* is.

FlashAttention tiles the computation, keeps blocks in fast on-chip SRAM, and uses
a streaming (online) softmax so it never needs the whole row at once. Memory goes
from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$ and wall-clock drops several-fold.

**It computes exactly the same function.** No approximation, no quality change.
That is what makes it unusual: nearly every other efficiency technique on this
page trades something away.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Activations">

The 2017 MLP was `Linear → ReLU → Linear`. Modern models use a gated variant,
usually SwiGLU:

$$
\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \big(\text{Swish}(xW_1) \odot xW_2\big)W_3 .
$$

The elementwise product is a multiplicative gate: one branch decides how much of
the other to let through. It reliably beats ReLU at equal parameter count, which
is why the hidden dimension is usually scaled by $\tfrac{2}{3}$ to keep the
parameter count matched when comparing.

Shazeer's paper is candid that there is no theory here — the abstract attributes
the gains to "divine benevolence". It is empirical, it replicates, and it is
universal now.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Scaled dot-product attention

$$
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M\right)V,
$$

with $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k}$, $K \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_k}$, $V \in
\mathbb{R}^{m \times d_v}$, and $M$ the additive mask.

**Why $\sqrt{d_k}$, derived.** Suppose the components of $q$ and $k$ are
independent with mean 0 and variance 1. Then

$$
\mathbb{E}[q \cdot k] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(q \cdot k) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k}\operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = d_k .
$$

So the logits have standard deviation $\sqrt{d_k}$. With $d_k = 128$ that is
$\approx 11$ — large enough that the softmax saturates, putting nearly all mass on
one entry. And a saturated softmax has vanishing gradient: for
$p = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$ the Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top$,
which goes to zero as $p$ approaches one-hot. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ restores
unit variance and keeps the softmax in its responsive regime. The constant is
forced by the algebra, not chosen.

**Multi-head.**

$$
\operatorname{MHA}(X) = \operatorname{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)W_O, \quad
\text{head}_i = \operatorname{Attention}(XW_Q^i, XW_K^i, XW_V^i).
$$

With $d_k = d_v = d/h$, total parameters are $4d^2$ regardless of $h$ — heads are
free in parameter count, and buy expressiveness by partitioning rather than
adding.

## Complexity, stated carefully

Per layer, for sequence length $n$ and model width $d$:

| | Time | Memory (naive) | Memory (Flash) | Sequential ops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Self-attention | $O(n^2 d)$ | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Recurrent | $O(n d^2)$ | $O(n)$ | — | $O(n)$ |
| MLP | $O(n d^2)$ | $O(nd)$ | — | $O(1)$ |

Two readings people often miss:

**Attention is not always the dominant cost.** Comparing $O(n^2 d)$ against $O(n
d^2)$, attention dominates only when $n > d$. For $d = 4096$ and $n = 2048$, the
MLPs cost more. The quadratic term matters at long context, not universally.

**The $O(1)$ sequential depth is the real 2017 contribution.** An RNN needs $n$
sequential steps; a transformer needs one. That is what made training
parallelisable across the sequence dimension, and parallelisability — not
accuracy — is why transformers displaced RNNs.

## RoPE, formally

Rotate $q$ and $k$ by an angle proportional to absolute position. For a
two-dimensional slice and rotation matrix $R_\theta$:

$$
\tilde{q}_m = R_{m\theta}\, q_m, \qquad \tilde{k}_n = R_{n\theta}\, k_n .
$$

Then, using $R_a^\top R_b = R_{b-a}$:

$$
\tilde{q}_m^\top \tilde{k}_n = q_m^\top R_{m\theta}^\top R_{n\theta} k_n = q_m^\top R_{(n-m)\theta}\, k_n .
$$

The score depends only on the **relative** offset $n - m$. Absolute position is
injected but cancels in the dot product — you get relative-position behaviour
without a relative-position bias term, and without changing the shape of anything.
That elegance is why it won.

The full scheme applies this to $d/2$ two-dimensional slices with geometrically
spaced frequencies $\theta_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$, giving each slice a different
wavelength — fast-rotating slices resolve nearby positions, slow ones carry
long-range order.

## Scaling laws, and the correction

Kaplan et al. (2020) found test loss follows a power law in parameters $N$,
data $D$, and compute $C$:

$$
L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \qquad \alpha_N \approx 0.076 .
$$

Their conclusion was that under a fixed compute budget you should mostly grow the
model. Hoffmann et al. (2022) re-ran the analysis with a learning-rate schedule
matched to each run's length and got a materially different answer: **parameters
and tokens should scale roughly in proportion**, about 20 tokens per parameter.
Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T tokens) beat Gopher (280B, 300B tokens) — a model four times
smaller trained on nearly five times more data.

<Aside type="note" title="The methodological lesson generalises">
The Kaplan/Chinchilla discrepancy came down to a confound in the experimental
setup, not to a disagreement about the data. Fixed-schedule sweeps
systematically penalised the longer runs. This is worth remembering whenever you
read a scaling result: the fitted exponents are only as good as the training
protocol held constant beneath them.
</Aside>

## Where things stand, as of 2026

**The core is stable; the periphery is not.** Attention itself is unchanged from
2017. What surrounds it — normalisation placement, position encoding, activation,
KV-head sharing, kernel implementation — has turned over completely. If you learn
transformers from the original paper alone, roughly half of what you learn is
obsolete in the details while the central idea is exactly right.

**State-space models are a real alternative, and have not displaced transformers.**
Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) achieves linear-time sequence modelling with a *selective*
state-space mechanism — the state update is input-dependent, which is what earlier
SSMs like S4 lacked and what let Mamba close much of the quality gap on language.
It matches or beats similarly sized transformers on several benchmarks with far
better long-sequence scaling.

The honest read as of 2026: SSMs are strong, genuinely better on very long
sequences, and *weaker at exact retrieval from context*. A transformer can attend
directly to any past token; a fixed-size recurrent state cannot store everything,
so tasks that require copying specific distant content favour attention. This is
why the most convincing results are **hybrids** that interleave a few attention
layers into an SSM stack — the attention layers handle recall, the SSM layers
handle bulk sequence processing. Anyone claiming attention is obsolete is ahead of
the evidence.

**Memory bandwidth is the binding constraint, not FLOPs.** FlashAttention's
success made this explicit and it has shaped design since: the useful question
about a new technique is usually "how much does it move between memory tiers?",
not "how many multiplications?".

## Open problems

- **Long context is unsolved, not solved.** Models advertise million-token windows
  but retrieval quality degrades in the middle of long contexts, and the KV cache
  cost is brutal. The advertised number is a capacity, not a competence.

- **Nobody knows what most attention heads do.** The tidy induction-head story
  covers a small fraction. Superposition — features sharing dimensions — means
  individual heads and neurons are usually not the right unit of analysis, which
  is what motivates sparse-autoencoder approaches to interpretability.

- **Position encoding remains unsatisfying.** RoPE works but extrapolates poorly
  without ad-hoc scaling. There is no principled account of how positional
  information *should* be represented.

- **The MLP is under-theorised.** It holds most parameters and most knowledge, and
  attracts a small fraction of the research attention. Why the $4\times$ expansion?
  Why gating helps? Both open.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Vaswani et al. (2017)** — but read it as a historical document. Section 3.2.1
   on $\sqrt{d_k}$ and Table 3's ablations are the durable parts.
2. **Xiong et al. (2020)** — the pre-norm analysis. Short, and it changes how you
   read every subsequent architecture diagram.
3. **Dao et al. (2022), FlashAttention** — read it for the IO-awareness framing,
   which generalises far beyond attention.
4. **Hoffmann et al. (2022), Chinchilla** — read the methodology section, not the

5. **Gu & Dao (2023), Mamba** — the strongest current statement of the
