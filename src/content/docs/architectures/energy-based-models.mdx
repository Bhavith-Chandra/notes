<Abstract>
An energy-based model assigns a single number to every possible configuration
and declares low numbers likely. That is the most permissive way to define a
probability distribution — *any* function of the input gives you a valid one —
and the permissiveness is paid for by a single intractable integral. Forty
years of work on energy-based models is forty years of trying to avoid
computing that integral, and the eventual escape route turned out to be so
effective that it stopped being called energy-based modelling and started being
called diffusion.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **One idea: a scalar score per configuration.** Low energy means plausible, high energy means implausible. No architectural constraint at all — no invertibility, no bottleneck, no ordering. That freedom is the family's defining advantage.
- **One problem: the partition function.** Turning energies into probabilities requires dividing by a sum over *every possible configuration*. In high dimensions that integral is intractable, and essentially every technique here is a way of not computing it.
- **The escape is to model the gradient instead.** $\nabla_x \log p(x) = -\nabla_x E(x)$ — the normaliser has no $x$ in it, so it vanishes under differentiation. This single observation converts an intractable problem into a tractable regression, and it is the whole basis of score matching and diffusion.
- **Every classifier you have trained is already an EBM.** The logits are negative energies; softmax is the Boltzmann distribution with the normaliser computed over the (small, tractable) label set. EBMs only look exotic because the configuration space is usually large.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## A landscape, not a formula

Imagine a hilly landscape stretched over the space of all possible images. Real
photographs sit in the valleys. Static, noise and nonsense sit on the peaks.

That surface is the **energy function** $E(x)$. It takes any configuration — any
image, any sentence, any protein fold — and returns one number. Low is good.

**To generate, you roll downhill.** Start somewhere random, follow the slope
toward lower energy, and you arrive in a valley — that is, at something that looks
like real data.

**To evaluate, you read off the height.** Given an image, its energy tells you how
plausible the model thinks it is.

## Why this is the most flexible thing you can do

Every other generative family constrains its architecture to make something
tractable:

- A [normalizing flow](/architectures/generative-models/) must be **invertible**
  with a computable Jacobian determinant.
- An [autoregressive model](/architectures/generative-models/) must impose an
  **ordering** and factorise along it.
- A [VAE](/encoders/neural-encoders/) needs a **tractable posterior** to bound
  the likelihood.

An EBM needs **none of that**. Any neural network that outputs one number defines a
valid energy function and therefore a valid probability distribution. You can use
any architecture, any depth, any structure.

That is a genuinely remarkable amount of freedom, and it has one bill attached.

## The bill: comparing to everything

Energy tells you a configuration is *good*. It does not tell you *how likely* it
is, because likelihood is relative — you need to know how good everything else is.

To turn energies into probabilities you divide by the total:

$$
p(x) = \frac{e^{-E(x)}}{\sum_{\text{all possible } x'} e^{-E(x')}}
$$

That denominator is called the **partition function**, written $Z$, and it sums over
every configuration in the space.

For a $32\times32$ colour image there are $256^{3072}$ configurations — a number
with roughly seven thousand digits. **You cannot compute $Z$. You cannot
approximate $Z$ well. $Z$ is the entire difficulty of this field.**

<Aside type="tip" title="The trade, in one line">
Other families constrain the architecture so the normaliser is computable. EBMs
keep total architectural freedom and give up the normaliser. Neither is free — and
knowing which constraint you are paying is the useful thing to carry away.
</Aside>

## The escape

Here is the observation that rescues everything, and it is worth pausing on because
it is genuinely elegant.

You cannot compute $p(x)$. But suppose you only need to know **which direction
increases probability** — which way is downhill on the landscape. Then:

$$
\log p(x) = -E(x) - \log Z
$$

and $Z$ is a **constant** — it does not depend on $x$. So when you differentiate
with respect to $x$:

$$
\nabla_x \log p(x) = -\nabla_x E(x)
$$

**The intractable term vanishes.** The slope of the landscape is computable even
though its absolute height is not.

That quantity — the gradient of the log-density — is called the **score**, and
learning it directly is what [diffusion models](/architectures/diffusion/) do.
Diffusion is not a rival to energy-based modelling; it is energy-based modelling
that stopped trying to compute $Z$.

## The three ways to avoid $Z$

Every training method here is one of three strategies. Getting this taxonomy
straight is most of understanding the field.

<Steps>

1. **Approximate the negative phase with MCMC.** Run a Markov chain to get samples
   from the model, use them for the second term. Contrastive divergence, PCD,
   Langevin-based methods. *Correct in principle, expensive and fragile.*

2. **Change the objective so $Z$ cancels.** Score matching compares gradients
   rather than densities, and gradients do not see $Z$. *No sampling required at
   all.*

3. **Turn it into classification.** Noise-contrastive estimation asks the model to
   distinguish data from noise; the normaliser becomes a learnable parameter.
   *Turns an estimation problem into a supervised one.*

</Steps>

## Strategy 1 — MCMC

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Langevin dynamics">

The standard sampler for continuous EBMs. Walk downhill on the energy, with noise:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \frac{\eta}{2}\nabla_x E_\theta(x_t) + \sqrt{\eta}\,\epsilon_t, \qquad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
$$

**Read the two terms.** The gradient term is gradient descent on energy — it walks
toward valleys. The noise term prevents you from getting stuck in the first valley
you find, and it is what makes this *sampling* rather than optimisation.

As $\eta \to 0$ and $t \to \infty$ this converges to the true distribution. Both
limits are the problem: small steps mean slow mixing, and "infinite time" in
practice means "you stopped early and your samples are wrong".

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Contrastive divergence">

Hinton's 2002 shortcut, and the reason RBMs were trainable at all.

Instead of running the chain to convergence, **start it at a data point and run $k$
steps** — often $k=1$. The intuition: the data distribution is already close to the
model distribution if training is going well, so a short chain from data lands
somewhere useful.

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CD-}k} \approx \nabla_\theta E_\theta(x_{\text{data}}) - \nabla_\theta E_\theta(x_k),
$$

with $x_k$ the state after $k$ steps from $x_{\text{data}}$.

**It is a biased estimator and it is not the gradient of anything.** CD does not
minimise any objective function — it is a gradient-like update that happens to work
in practice, and there are known cases where it converges to the wrong answer.
Hinton was explicit about this. It worked well enough to power the 2006 deep
learning revival, which is a reasonable defence.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Persistent CD">

Tieleman's fix for CD's main weakness. CD restarts the chain from data every
iteration, so it never explores regions far from the data — which is exactly where
spurious low-energy valleys form.

**PCD keeps the chain going across parameter updates.** Maintain a set of persistent
"fantasy particles"; each iteration, run a few MCMC steps from where they were last
time rather than from data.

The justification is that parameters change slowly, so the chain from the previous
model is a good starting point for the current one. Better exploration, better
models, and it introduces a new failure — if the particles get stuck, the negative
phase becomes stale and the model is free to place mass where the particles are not.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Modern deep EBMs">

Du & Mordatch (2019) made MCMC-based training work on real images, and the paper is
mostly a catalogue of what it takes:

- **A replay buffer** of past samples, reinitialised from noise a small fraction of
  the time. Effectively PCD with a large particle population.
- **Spectral normalisation** on the energy network, bounding its Lipschitz constant
  so the Langevin steps behave.
- **Gradient clipping** on the Langevin updates themselves.
- **An L2 penalty on the energy magnitudes**, preventing the two phases from
  diverging to $\pm\infty$ while keeping their difference constant.

Nijkamp et al. (2019) then dissected what these models are actually doing, and the
finding is uncomfortable: with short-run MCMC, the model does **not** learn the
data distribution. It learns an energy landscape for which the *specific short
chain you run* produces good samples. Change the chain length at test time and
sample quality collapses.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="danger" title="The Nijkamp result, stated plainly">
Short-run MCMC training does not give you an EBM whose Gibbs distribution matches
the data. It gives you a **learned sampler** — an energy function tuned so that $K$
steps of Langevin from noise lands on something good. That object is useful, and it
is not the thing the maths says you are estimating.

The diagnostic is simple and rarely reported: **run the chain much longer at test
time.** If samples degrade, you have a short-run sampler, not a density model.
</Aside>

## Strategy 2 — Score matching

The elegant escape. Instead of matching densities, match their **gradients**.

Since $\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x)$ and $Z$ has no $x$ in it,
the score is computable with no sampling and no normaliser.

**The problem it creates.** The obvious objective compares your score to the data's
score, and you do not know the data's score. Hyvärinen's result is that integration
by parts eliminates it, leaving an objective in terms of quantities you *can*
compute — at the price of needing the trace of a Hessian, which is expensive in high
dimensions.

**Two practical resolutions:**

- **Denoising score matching** (Vincent, 2011). Add noise to the data and match the
  score of the *noised* distribution, which is known in closed form. This turns
  score matching into denoising regression — and it is **exactly the diffusion
  objective**.
- **Sliced score matching** (Song et al., 2019). Project onto random directions and
  match one-dimensional scores, replacing the Hessian trace with cheap
  Hessian-vector products.

## Strategy 3 — Noise-contrastive estimation

Turn density estimation into binary classification.

Mix your real data with samples from a known noise distribution, and train a
classifier to tell them apart. **Treat $\log Z$ as just another learnable
parameter.** If the classifier is optimal, its decision function recovers the
density ratio between data and noise — and since you know the noise density, you
recover the data density.

**Why this is more than a trick.** It is the same structure as
[InfoNCE](/training/self-supervision/) in contrastive learning, and the same
structure as a GAN discriminator. All three learn a density ratio by classification.
Recognising that connection makes a lot of otherwise separate literatures look like
one idea.

**The catch.** The noise distribution must be close enough to the data that
discrimination is hard. If real and noise samples are trivially separable, the
classifier learns a shortcut and the estimate is poor — the same requirement as
hard negatives in contrastive learning.

## Comparison

| Method | Needs sampling | Unbiased | Cost | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Exact MLE** | yes (exact) | yes | impossible | never |
| **CD-$k$** | yes (short) | **no** | cheap | RBMs, historical |
| **PCD** | yes (persistent) | asymptotically | moderate | RBMs, deep EBMs |
| **Langevin + buffer** | yes | approximately | expensive | modern image EBMs |
| **Score matching** | **no** | yes | Hessian trace | low dimensions |
| **Denoising SM** | **no** | yes (for noised $p$) | cheap | **diffusion — everywhere** |
| **Sliced SM** | **no** | yes | cheap | practical alternative |
| **NCE** | noise only | yes | cheap | language models, embeddings |

## The Boltzmann distribution

$$
p_\theta(x) = \frac{\exp\big(-E_\theta(x)\big)}{Z_\theta}, \qquad Z_\theta = \int \exp\big(-E_\theta(x)\big)\,dx .
$$

The form is not arbitrary. It is the **maximum-entropy distribution** subject to a
constraint on expected energy — i.e. the least-committed distribution consistent
with what you have specified. Maximising $H(p) = -\int p\log p$ subject to
$\int p\,E = \bar E$ and $\int p = 1$, with Lagrange multipliers $\beta$ and
$\lambda$:

$$
\frac{\delta}{\delta p}\left[-\!\int p\log p - \beta\!\int pE - \lambda\!\int p\right] = -\log p - 1 - \beta E - \lambda = 0
\ \implies\ p \propto e^{-\beta E}.
$$

**So the exponential form is derived, not chosen**, and $\beta$ (inverse
temperature, usually absorbed into $E$) is the multiplier on the energy constraint.
This is why the same functional form appears in statistical physics, in softmax
classifiers, and in [RLHF's KL-constrained
optimum](/training/post-training/) — all three are maximum-entropy problems.

## The maximum-likelihood gradient

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\big[E_\theta(x)\big] + \log Z_\theta .
$$

Differentiating the log-partition term:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log Z_\theta = \frac{1}{Z_\theta}\nabla_\theta\!\int e^{-E_\theta(x)}dx
= \frac{1}{Z_\theta}\int e^{-E_\theta(x)}\big(-\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\big)dx
= -\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\big].
$$

Hence the two-phase gradient:

$$
\boxed{\ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\big]}_{\text{positive phase}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\big]}_{\text{negative phase}}\ }
$$

**Three things to read off this.**

First, $Z$ never appears — only an *expectation under the model*. The intractable
integral became an intractable expectation, which is progress: expectations can be
approximated by sampling.

Second, at the optimum the two phases are equal, so the gradient vanishes when
$p_\theta = p_{\text{data}}$ — as it must.

Third, **the negative phase is not optional.** Drop it and the gradient is
$\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\nabla_\theta E]$, which is minimised by driving
$E \to -\infty$ everywhere. Nothing anchors the scale but the negative phase.

## Contrastive divergence, and what it actually minimises

CD-$k$ replaces the model expectation with samples from a $k$-step chain
initialised at data. Hinton's original motivation is that CD approximately minimises

$$
\mathrm{CD}_k = \mathrm{KL}\big(p_0\,\|\,p_\theta\big) - \mathrm{KL}\big(p_k\,\|\,p_\theta\big),
$$

with $p_0$ the data and $p_k$ the distribution after $k$ MCMC steps. As $k\to\infty$
the second term vanishes and CD becomes maximum likelihood.

**The honest statement, which the original paper makes.** The CD update is *not*
the exact gradient of $\mathrm{CD}_k$ — a term is dropped, and that dropped term is
not always negligible. So CD is a heuristic update rule rather than a descent
direction on a well-defined objective, and there exist constructed cases where it
converges to a non-optimal point.

**Improved CD** (Du et al., 2020) identified the dropped term explicitly and showed
that including a KL-divergence correction — which pushes samples toward regions the
model over-weights — substantially improves both stability and sample quality. The
missing term was doing real work.

## Score matching

Define the **Fisher divergence** between model and data:

$$
J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\Big[\big\|\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) - \nabla_x \log p_{\text{data}}(x)\big\|^2\Big].
$$

Since $\nabla_x\log p_\theta = -\nabla_x E_\theta$, the model side is computable.
The data side is not — but Hyvärinen showed it can be eliminated.

**The derivation.** Expand the square:

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac12\|\nabla_x\log p_\theta\|^2\right] - \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_x\log p_\theta^{\top}\nabla_x\log p_{\text{data}}\big] + \text{const}.
$$

The middle term is the problem. Write it as an integral and use
$p_{\text{data}}\nabla_x\log p_{\text{data}} = \nabla_x p_{\text{data}}$:

$$
-\!\int\! p_{\text{data}}(x)\,\nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)^{\top}\nabla_x \log p_{\text{data}}(x)\,dx = -\!\int\! \nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)^{\top}\nabla_x p_{\text{data}}(x)\,dx .
$$

Integrate by parts, assuming $p_{\text{data}}(x)\nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)\to 0$ at
the boundary:

$$
= \int p_{\text{data}}(x)\,\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\nabla_x^2 \log p_\theta(x)\big)\,dx .
$$

Giving the **implicit score matching** objective:

$$
\boxed{\ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\left[\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\nabla_x^2\log p_\theta(x)\big) + \tfrac12\big\|\nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)\big\|^2\right] + \text{const}\ }
$$

**Everything here is computable from the model alone.** No $Z$, no sampling, no data
score.

**The two costs, both real.** The Hessian trace requires $O(d)$ backward passes for
a $d$-dimensional input — prohibitive for images. And the boundary condition can
fail for distributions with bounded support, which is why score matching on
$[0,1]$-valued pixel data needs care.

## Denoising score matching — and the bridge to diffusion

Vincent's result removes the Hessian entirely. Perturb the data with a known kernel
$q_\sigma(\tilde x\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I)$ and match the
score of the *perturbed* distribution:

$$
J_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) = \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\sigma(\tilde x, x)}\Big[\big\|\nabla_{\tilde x}\log p_\theta(\tilde x) - \nabla_{\tilde x}\log q_\sigma(\tilde x\mid x)\big\|^2\Big].
$$

For the Gaussian kernel the target has a closed form:

$$
\nabla_{\tilde x}\log q_\sigma(\tilde x \mid x) = -\frac{\tilde x - x}{\sigma^2},
$$

so with $\tilde x = x + \sigma\epsilon$:

$$
J_{\text{DSM}} = \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_{x,\epsilon}\left[\left\|s_\theta(x + \sigma\epsilon) + \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}\right\|^2\right].
$$

**Compare with the DDPM objective**
$\mathbb{E}\big[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2\big]$ from
[diffusion](/architectures/diffusion/). They are the same objective up to
reparameterisation: $s_\theta = -\epsilon_\theta/\sigma$.

Vincent's theorem is that minimising $J_{\text{DSM}}$ is equivalent to minimising
$J$ for the *smoothed* density $p_{\sigma}$. So you get an exact score-matching
objective for a slightly blurred version of the data, with no Hessian and no
sampling.

<Aside type="note" title="This is the whole history in one line">
Energy-based models were flexible, principled and untrainable because of $Z$. Score
matching removed $Z$ but needed a Hessian. Denoising score matching removed the
Hessian but only gave you the noised distribution. **Running it at many noise
levels and chaining them together recovers the clean distribution — and that is
diffusion.**

So the modern generative-modelling mainstream is the energy-based programme, with
three obstacles removed in sequence. Diffusion did not defeat EBMs; it is what
EBMs became once the estimation problem was solved.
</Aside>

## Sliced score matching

Song et al.'s alternative to denoising, when you want the clean distribution.
Project the score onto random directions $v\sim p_v$:

$$
J_{\text{SSM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_v}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\left[v^{\top}\nabla_x^2\log p_\theta(x)\,v + \tfrac12\big(v^{\top}\nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)\big)^2\right].
$$

The key term $v^\top \nabla^2_x \log p_\theta\, v$ is a **Hessian-vector product**,
computable in one extra backward pass via

$$
v^{\top}\nabla_x^2 f(x)\,v = v^{\top}\nabla_x\big(v^{\top}\nabla_x f(x)\big),
$$

rather than the $O(d)$ passes the full trace requires. With $v$ drawn from a
distribution satisfying $\mathbb{E}[vv^\top] = I$ (Gaussian or Rademacher), the
estimator is unbiased for the full objective — it is a Hutchinson trace estimator
applied to score matching.

## Noise-contrastive estimation

Treat $\log Z$ as a parameter $c$, so the model is
$\log p_\theta(x) = -E_\theta(x) - c$ with $c$ learned. Draw noise from a known
$q(x)$ and train a logistic classifier to distinguish data from noise:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log h_\theta(x)\big] - \nu\,\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log\big(1 - h_\theta(x)\big)\big],
$$

$$
h_\theta(x) = \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x) + \nu\,q(x)} = \sigma\Big(\log p_\theta(x) - \log q(x) - \log\nu\Big),
$$

with $\nu$ the noise-to-data ratio.

**Why $c$ becomes identifiable.** In an unnormalised model, $E$ and $E + \text{const}$
define the same distribution — the constant is unidentifiable. But the classifier's
output depends on the *ratio* $p_\theta/q$, and $q$ is a known normalised density,
so a shift in $c$ changes the classification loss. **The comparison to a normalised
reference is what pins down the scale**, and this is the whole trick.

**Consistency** requires $q > 0$ wherever $p_{\text{data}} > 0$, and the estimator's
variance grows as $q$ diverges from $p_{\text{data}}$ — the formal version of "the
noise must be hard to distinguish".

## Langevin dynamics, and why it converges

The overdamped Langevin SDE

$$
dx_t = -\nabla_x E(x_t)\,dt + \sqrt{2}\,dW_t
$$

has stationary distribution $p(x)\propto e^{-E(x)}$. To see it, write the
Fokker–Planck equation for the density $\rho(x,t)$:

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\rho\,\nabla E\big) + \nabla^2\rho .
$$

Setting $\partial_t\rho = 0$ and substituting $\rho = e^{-E}/Z$:

$$
\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(e^{-E}\nabla E\big) + \nabla^2 e^{-E} = \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(e^{-E}\nabla E\big) - \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(e^{-E}\nabla E\big) = 0 .
$$

**So the Boltzmann distribution is stationary**, and the drift and diffusion terms
exactly balance: drift pushes probability mass downhill, diffusion spreads it back.

The Euler–Maruyama discretisation is the update rule from earlier. Discretisation
introduces bias of $O(\eta)$, which Metropolis–Hastings correction removes (MALA) at
the cost of an accept/reject step — usually skipped in deep learning, where the bias
is accepted.

**The practical failure is mixing time.** For a multimodal $p$ with well-separated
modes, the expected time to cross between modes scales as $e^{\Delta E}$ where
$\Delta E$ is the barrier height. **Exponential in the barrier.** This is why
Langevin sampling from a deep EBM on images does not reliably explore the full
distribution, and no amount of tuning fixes an exponential.

## Your classifier is already an EBM

The observation behind JEM, and it reframes a lot.

A standard classifier outputs logits $f_\theta(x)[y]$ and forms
$p_\theta(y\mid x) = \exp(f_\theta(x)[y]) / \sum_{y'}\exp(f_\theta(x)[y'])$.

Reinterpret the logits as **negative energies of joint configurations**:

$$
E_\theta(x,y) = -f_\theta(x)[y], \qquad p_\theta(x,y) = \frac{e^{-E_\theta(x,y)}}{Z_\theta}.
$$

Marginalising over $y$ gives a density on $x$ alone:

$$
p_\theta(x) = \frac{\sum_y e^{f_\theta(x)[y]}}{Z_\theta}
\ \implies\
E_\theta(x) = -\log\sum_y e^{f_\theta(x)[y]} = -\operatorname{LogSumExp}_y f_\theta(x)[y].
$$

**The logsumexp of your classifier's logits is an energy function over inputs**, and
it was there all along — softmax discards it, because softmax is invariant to adding
a constant to all logits and that constant is exactly what carries the density
information.

JEM trains this joint model with the standard cross-entropy for $p(y\mid x)$ plus a
Langevin-based EBM objective for $p(x)$, and reports improved calibration,
out-of-distribution detection and robustness alongside competitive accuracy.

**The connection to note.** Softmax classification *is* a Boltzmann distribution
where $Z$ is tractable, because the configuration space is the label set — ten
classes, not $256^{3072}$ images. **EBMs are not exotic; they are what happens when
the configuration space is too large to normalise over.**

## Modern Hopfield networks

The classical Hopfield network stores patterns $\{\xi^\mu\}$ as minima of

$$
E(x) = -\tfrac12 x^{\top}Wx, \qquad W = \sum_{\mu}\xi^\mu(\xi^\mu)^{\top},
$$

and retrieves by descending the energy. Its capacity is famously poor: about
$0.14\,d$ patterns for $d$ neurons before spurious minima dominate.

**Krotov & Hopfield's insight** is that capacity depends on the *sharpness* of the
energy's dependence on overlap. Replace the quadratic with a rapidly-growing
function $F$:

$$
E(x) = -\sum_{\mu}F\big((\xi^\mu)^{\top}x\big),
$$

and capacity grows with the sharpness of $F$ — polynomial $F$ of degree $n$ gives
capacity $O(d^{n-1})$, and **exponential $F$ gives capacity exponential in $d$**.

**Ramsauer et al.'s continuous version** takes the exponential case:

$$
E(x) = -\operatorname{lse}\big(\beta,\, X^{\top}x\big) + \tfrac12 x^{\top}x + \text{const},
$$

with $\operatorname{lse}$ the log-sum-exp and $X$ the stored patterns. The update
that minimises this by concave–convex procedure is

$$
x^{\text{new}} = X\,\operatorname{softmax}\big(\beta X^{\top}x\big),
$$

which **is the transformer attention update**, with $x$ the query and $X$ the
keys/values.

**So attention is one step of retrieval in an exponential-capacity associative
memory.** That equivalence is exact, it is not an analogy, and it means every
transformer contains an energy-based associative memory whose energy function is
written down above. It also shows retrieval converges in **one step** for
well-separated patterns, which is why a single attention layer suffices.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**The energy-based programme won by changing its name.** Diffusion and score-based
models are trained with denoising score matching, which is an energy-based
estimation method, and they dominate high-quality generation. The word "energy" is
mostly absent from those papers, which obscures a real intellectual continuity.

**Explicit EBMs remain niche and have a defensible niche.** Where you need
compositionality — combining several constraints by *adding* their energies, which
requires no retraining — EBMs do something no other family does cleanly:

$$
E_{\text{combined}}(x) = E_1(x) + E_2(x) \iff p_{\text{combined}} \propto p_1 \cdot p_2 .
$$

Composing constraints is a product of experts, and it is free.

**Short-run MCMC training is understood to be training a sampler, not a density.**
Nijkamp et al.'s diagnosis has been broadly accepted. Papers in this area now
generally report whether the energy landscape is valid independent of the chain, and
many acknowledge it is not.

**Modern Hopfield networks made the attention–memory equivalence exact**, and the
practical consequences are still being worked out. It gives a clean account of what
attention layers store and retrieve, and connects transformer capacity to a
well-studied associative-memory literature.

**JEM-style hybrid training is a live and underused idea.** Getting calibration and
OOD detection improvements essentially for free from a training-time change is
attractive, and adoption has been limited by the instability of the Langevin
component.

## Open problems

- **Sampling in high dimensions is unsolved.** Mixing time is exponential in energy
  barriers. Every practical method sidesteps this rather than solving it — diffusion
  by annealing the noise level, short-run methods by not mixing at all.

- **Evaluation is genuinely hard.** You cannot compute the likelihood, so you cannot
  report it. Annealed importance sampling estimates $Z$ with high variance and
  unclear reliability in high dimensions. Sample-based metrics measure the sampler as
  much as the model.

- **CD's bias is characterised but not eliminated.** The improved-CD correction
  helps and is not exact.

- **Compositionality is promising and shallow.** Adding energies composes
  constraints in principle; in practice the composed distribution is often dominated
  by whichever energy has the largest scale, and calibrating relative scales is
  unsolved.

- **Discrete EBMs lag.** Langevin needs gradients, so discrete spaces need Gibbs
  sampling or gradient-free variants, and both scale poorly. This is the same
  obstacle discrete diffusion faces.

## Where EBMs are the right choice

<Card title="A decision procedure" icon="right-arrow">
**Combining independently-trained constraints?** EBMs, and nothing else is close —
sum the energies and you have the product of the distributions, with no retraining.

**Need calibration or OOD detection from a classifier?** JEM-style joint training.
The logsumexp energy is already in your network.

**Structured prediction with hard constraints?** Energy-based learning was designed
for this — LeCun's tutorial is about ranking structured outputs, not about
generative modelling.

**Want the best samples?** Not an EBM. Use diffusion, which is the same idea with
the estimation problem solved.

**Want an exact likelihood?** Not an EBM. Use a [normalizing
flow](/architectures/generative-models/) or an autoregressive model.

**Studying associative memory or the theory of attention?** Modern Hopfield
networks, where the equivalence is exact and the capacity results are sharp.
</Card>

## Reading path

1. **LeCun et al. (2006), tutorial** — read it for the framing that energy-based
   learning is about *ranking*, not about probability. It predates and outlasts the
   generative framing.
2. **Hyvärinen (2005)** — the score matching derivation. Do the integration by parts
   yourself once; it is the pivot the whole field turns on.
3. **Vincent (2011)** — denoising score matching. Short, and it is the exact moment
   EBMs became diffusion.
4. **Song & Kingma (2021), How to Train Your EBM** — the best single survey, and it
   organises the methods by exactly the taxonomy on this page.
5. **Nijkamp et al. (2019)** — read it before trusting any short-run MCMC result.
6. **Ramsauer et al. (2020)** — for the attention–Hopfield equivalence, once the rest
   is in place.
