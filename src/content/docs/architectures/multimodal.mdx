<Abstract>
A language model consumes token embeddings. An image is a grid of pixels. The
entire field of vision-language modelling is about bridging that gap, and the
surprising empirical finding is that the bridge can be almost trivial — a single
linear layer, trained on a modest amount of data, is enough to make a frozen
vision encoder legible to a frozen language model. What is *not* trivial is
resolution, and that turns out to be where most real capability differences live.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Two ways in: projection or cross-attention.** Project image features into the token space and prepend them, or leave the text stream alone and let it attend to image features through inserted layers. The first is simpler and now dominant; the second is cheaper at long context.
- **The connector can be a single linear layer.** LLaVA's result — that a linear projection plus modest instruction tuning produces a capable VLM — was the finding that made this accessible. It works because CLIP-style encoders already produce language-aligned features.
- **Resolution is the hidden bottleneck.** A 336×336 image at patch 14 gives 576 tokens and cannot resolve small text or fine detail. Nearly every "the model can't read this chart" failure is a resolution failure, not a reasoning failure.
- **Contrastive and generative pretraining teach different things.** CLIP learns to *match* images and text, which is retrieval. It does not learn to *describe*, which is why VLMs bolt a generative decoder onto a contrastively-trained encoder.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The translation problem

A language model has one input format: a sequence of vectors, each standing for a
token. It has no notion of pixels.

So to show it an image you must turn the image into vectors *of the same kind* —
vectors that sit in the same space as word embeddings and mean something to the
same machinery.

The remarkable part is how little work this takes. Run the image through a
pretrained vision encoder, multiply the output by one learned matrix, and paste the
result into the prompt where words would go. The language model treats them as if
they were tokens, and it broadly works.

## Why one matrix is enough

This only works because of what the vision encoder already learned.

A CLIP-style encoder was trained to place images near their *captions* in a shared
space. So its output is already organised by linguistic meaning — a photo of a dog
sits near wherever "a photo of a dog" sits. The encoder did the hard alignment
work during its own pretraining.

The projection layer therefore has an easy job: not to *create* meaning, but to
rotate an already-meaningful space into the particular coordinates the language
model happens to use. That is close to a change of basis, and a linear map can do
it.

<Aside type="tip" title="The consequence worth drawing">
Your VLM's ceiling is largely set by the vision encoder, not the connector. If the
encoder never learned to distinguish two things, no projection layer or
instruction-tuning recipe will recover the distinction. Debug the encoder first.
</Aside>

## What actually breaks

Ask a modern VLM to describe a photograph and it does well. Ask it to read the
axis labels on a chart, count seventeen objects, or tell you what a small sign in
the background says, and it often fails.

The reason is almost always **resolution**. A 336×336 input divided into 14×14
patches gives 576 patches — and a caption-sized piece of text in the corner of the
original photo occupies a fraction of one patch. The information is gone before
any reasoning begins.

This is why so much recent work is about *seeing more pixels* rather than
reasoning better: tiling the image, running the encoder at higher resolution, or
processing crops alongside a global view.

## The lineage

<Steps>

1. **CLIP (2021)** — contrastive image/text pretraining. Produces a shared
   embedding space, excellent for retrieval and zero-shot classification. Cannot
   generate text.

2. **Flamingo (2022)** — freeze a vision encoder and a language model, insert
   gated cross-attention layers between them. First convincing few-shot
   vision-language model. Architecturally invasive.

3. **BLIP-2 (2023)** — insert a small trainable transformer (the *Q-Former*)
   between frozen encoder and frozen LLM, using a fixed set of learned query
   vectors to extract a compact summary. Bridges the gap with very few trainable
   parameters.

4. **LLaVA (2023)** — replace the Q-Former with **one linear layer**, train on
   GPT-generated instruction data. Comparable results at a fraction of the
   complexity. This is what made VLMs reproducible in academic labs, and the
   projection approach has dominated since.

5. **High-resolution era (2024–)** — the bottleneck moves from alignment to
   perception. Tiling, dynamic resolution, and multi-encoder designs.

</Steps>

## Handling resolution

Vision transformers are trained at a fixed resolution, and naively upscaling
breaks the position embeddings. The practical approaches:

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Tiling">

Split a high-resolution image into a grid of tiles, each at the encoder's native
resolution, and encode each separately. Add a downscaled *global* view so the model
retains overall layout.

A 2×2 tiling plus global view at 576 tokens each gives 2,880 image tokens — which
is why high-resolution VLMs consume so much context. NVLM's contribution here was
1-D tile tagging: mark tile positions explicitly in the token stream, so the model
knows how the pieces fit together.

**This is the dominant approach**, and its cost is linear in tile count.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Interpolated positions">

Interpolate the ViT's position embeddings to the new grid size and fine-tune
briefly. Cheaper than tiling, and quality degrades as you move further from the
training resolution — the encoder's learned features were tuned for a particular
patch-to-object scale.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Resampling">

Use a fixed number of learned queries to attend over however many patches the
encoder produced, compressing to a constant token count regardless of input size.
The Q-Former and Perceiver Resampler both do this.

Fixed cost, and it is a bottleneck by construction: 32 or 64 query vectors cannot
carry the information in a dense 4K document scan. Good for photographs, poor for
text-heavy images.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Multiple encoders">

Run several vision encoders with different strengths — CLIP for semantics, a
self-supervised encoder like DINOv2 for spatial structure, an OCR-focused encoder
for text — and concatenate their features.

Cambrian-1's systematic study found vision-encoder choice matters far more than
the connector design, and that combining encoders with complementary training
objectives beats any single one. Cost is running several encoders per image.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

<Aside type="caution" title="Most VLM failures are perception, not reasoning">
Before concluding a model "cannot reason about" a chart, check whether it can
*read* the chart. Crop the region of interest and ask again at full resolution. In
practice a large share of apparent reasoning failures resolve, which tells you the
information never reached the language model.
</Aside>

## The contrastive objective

CLIP trains paired encoders on $N$ image-text pairs with a symmetric InfoNCE loss.
With normalised embeddings $u_i = E_I(x_i)/\|\cdot\|$, $v_j = E_T(t_j)/\|\cdot\|$
and learned temperature $\tau$:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[
\log \frac{e^{u_i^{\top} v_i/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{u_i^{\top} v_j/\tau}}
+ \log \frac{e^{u_i^{\top} v_i/\tau}}{\sum_{j} e^{u_j^{\top} v_i/\tau}}
\right].
$$

Two directions because matching is symmetric: the right caption for an image, and
the right image for a caption.

**The batch-size problem.** The softmax normalises over the whole batch, so the
loss requires a global $N \times N$ similarity matrix. That forces an all-gather
across devices and makes batch size both a quality parameter and a systems
constraint — CLIP used 32,768.

**SigLIP's fix.** Replace softmax with an independent sigmoid on every pair:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \log \sigma\big(z_{ij}\,(t\,u_i^{\top}v_j + b)\big), \qquad z_{ij} = \begin{cases} +1 & i = j \\ -1 & i \neq j \end{cases}
$$

Because there is no normalisation across the batch, each pair's loss is
independent — no all-gather, and the computation shards cleanly. It works better
at small batch sizes *and* scales further, which is unusual; most techniques trade
one for the other. The learned bias $b$ matters because the pair set is
overwhelmingly negative ($N$ positives against $N^2 - N$ negatives), and $b$
absorbs that prior.

## The projection connector

Given patch features $H \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times d_v}$ from the vision encoder,
project into the language model's embedding dimension:

$$
Z = H W, \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v \times d_\ell},
$$

then form the input sequence by concatenation:

$$
X = \big[\,z_1, \dots, z_P,\ e(\text{tok}_1), \dots, e(\text{tok}_L)\,\big].
$$

Parameter count is $d_v d_\ell$ — for $d_v = 1024$, $d_\ell = 4096$ that is 4.2M,
against billions in the models it connects. LLaVA later used a two-layer MLP,
which helps modestly.

**Why linear suffices.** If the vision encoder's output space $\mathcal{V}$ and the
LM's embedding space $\mathcal{E}$ both encode semantics, and the semantic
structure is approximately preserved up to a linear transformation, then a matrix
suffices to align them. CLIP training is what makes the antecedent approximately
true — it explicitly optimised $\mathcal{V}$ for agreement with a *text* encoder.

The prediction this makes, and it holds: a **non**-language-aligned encoder needs a
more expressive connector. Purely self-supervised encoders like DINOv2 do worse
with a bare linear projection than CLIP encoders do.

## Token cost

For an image of side $S$ with patch size $p$:

$$
P = \left(\frac{S}{p}\right)^{2}.
$$

At $S=336$, $p=14$: $P = 576$. Under $T \times T$ tiling plus a global view:

$$
P_{\text{total}} = (T^2 + 1)\left(\frac{S}{p}\right)^{2}.
$$

For $T = 2$: 2,880 tokens. Since attention is quadratic in sequence length, the
cost contribution of the image alone is

$$
O\!\left(\left(\left(T^2+1\right)\tfrac{S^2}{p^2}\right)^{2}\right),
$$

quartic in tile count. **This is why high-resolution VLMs are expensive**, and why
resampling to a fixed token budget keeps reappearing despite its information
bottleneck.

## Gated cross-attention

Flamingo inserts layers computing

$$
h \leftarrow h + \tanh(\alpha)\cdot \operatorname{CrossAttn}\big(Q = hW_Q,\ K = ZW_K,\ V = ZW_V\big),
$$

with $\alpha$ a learned scalar initialised to **zero**. Since $\tanh(0) = 0$, the
inserted layer is exactly the identity at initialisation and the frozen language
model is bit-for-bit unchanged. The gate opens only as training finds the visual
information useful.

This is the same trick as LoRA's zero-initialised $B$ and the same reason: **start
as a no-op so the pretrained model is not disrupted at step zero.** It recurs
across the field and is worth recognising as a pattern.

Compute comparison per layer, for $L$ text tokens and $P$ image tokens:

$$
\text{cross-attention} = O(L \cdot P \cdot d), \qquad \text{concatenation} = O\big((L+P)^2 d\big).
$$

Cross-attention is linear in $P$; concatenation is quadratic in the total. For
large $P$ — high-resolution or video — cross-attention is substantially cheaper,
which is why the Flamingo approach persists for video despite projection winning
for single images.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Projection won for images; cross-attention persists for video.** The simplicity
of prepending tokens beat architectural surgery for the single-image case. Video
has enough frames that quadratic cost in total tokens becomes prohibitive, so the
cheaper cross-attention formulation remains competitive there.

**Vision encoder choice dominates connector design.** Cambrian-1's systematic
sweep is the clearest evidence: swapping the encoder moves benchmarks far more
than swapping the connector, and combining encoders trained with different
objectives beats any one of them. The field spent two years optimising the wrong
component.

**Native multimodal pretraining is displacing the bolt-on recipe.** Rather than
gluing a trained vision encoder to a trained LLM, frontier models increasingly
train on interleaved image-text data from the start. Better integration, far more
expensive, and it forecloses the swap-in-a-better-encoder option.

**Resolution is where the visible capability differences now are.** Chart reading,
document understanding, OCR, and fine-grained counting all improved primarily
through seeing more pixels rather than through better reasoning.

## Open problems

- **Fine-grained spatial reasoning is weak.** Counting, relative position, and
  precise localisation remain poor relative to how well these models describe
  scenes. Patch-level features may simply be the wrong granularity.

- **Modality imbalance.** Vision-language models are overwhelmingly text-driven;
  ablating the image often changes the answer less than it should. Benchmarks that
  can be solved from the text alone are common and inflate reported performance.

- **No good multimodal evaluation.** Many VQA benchmarks are answerable from
  language priors. Reported scores frequently measure how well the model guesses
  what a question about an image usually asks.

- **Video is largely unsolved.** Long videos have too many frames to encode
  densely; frame sampling discards temporal detail. Nothing handles hour-long video
  well.

- **Cross-modal hallucination.** Models describe objects that are not present,
  particularly plausible co-occurring ones — a bicycle near a bike rack. The
  language prior overrides the visual evidence and there is no reliable fix.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Liu et al. (2023), LLaVA** — read it for the surprise that a linear layer
