<Abstract>
A JEPA predicts the *representation* of one part of a signal from the
representation of another part. It never reconstructs the input, never uses
negative examples, and never requires you to hand-specify what to be invariant
to. Those three absences are the entire design — and they buy a problem in
exchange: with nothing forcing the encoder to distinguish inputs, the optimal
solution is to output a constant. Preventing that is what the whole literature
is about.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The defining move is where the loss lives.** Not pixel space (autoencoders), not a similarity ranking against negatives (contrastive) — a distance between two embeddings, one of which is produced by a copy of the encoder that receives no gradient.
- **Collapse is the global optimum, not a bug.** If the encoder outputs a constant, the prediction is trivially perfect. Every architectural choice in a JEPA exists to make that solution unreachable, and none of them come with a guarantee.
- **There are exactly four anti-collapse mechanisms in use.** Contrastive repulsion, asymmetry (stop-gradient plus EMA), redundancy reduction, and explicit variance floors. Knowing which one a method uses tells you its failure mode immediately.
- **Masking beats augmentation as a way to define the prediction task.** Augmentations encode *your* assumptions about what should be ignored. I-JEPA's mask-based formulation removes that hand-specification, which is the strongest argument for the family.

</KeyPoints>

## The idea

Cover the bottom half of a photograph. You can describe what is probably down
there — *more grass, the rest of the dog, a shadow* — without being able to paint
it. You know the *gist*, not the pixels.

That distinction is the whole point. Asking a model to paint the missing half
forces it to commit to details it cannot possibly know: the exact texture of each
blade of grass, the precise noise in the shadow. It will get them wrong, and the
gradient from getting them wrong is mostly noise.

**A JEPA asks for the gist instead.** Predict what the hidden part *means*, in
the model's own internal language, and don't ask about pixels at all.

## Why this is the more sensible request

Consider what a pixel-reconstruction loss is actually demanding. A photo of a
lawn contains millions of bits describing individual grass blades — high entropy,
essentially random, and completely irrelevant to any question you'd ask about the
image. A reconstruction objective spends model capacity and gradient signal on
exactly the content that is hardest to predict *and* least useful.

Latent prediction lets the encoder simply throw the grass away. The predictor's
target is whatever the encoder chose to keep, so unpredictable detail can be
discarded rather than modelled.

<Aside type="tip" title="The one-sentence version">
Autoencoders ask "can you rebuild it?" Contrastive methods ask "can you tell these
two apart?" JEPAs ask "given this part, what does the rest look like *to you*?" —
and letting the model define "to you" is the trick.
</Aside>

## The catch, which is severe

There is an obvious way to cheat. If the encoder ignores its input and outputs the
same vector for everything, then predicting one embedding from another is trivial:
predict that constant. The loss goes to zero. The representation is worthless.

This is **representation collapse**, and it is not an edge case you engineer
around — it is the objective's global minimum. A JEPA is, structurally, an
optimisation problem whose best answer is useless, plus a set of tricks that make
the best answer unreachable.

That framing is worth holding onto, because it explains why this literature looks
the way it does: a stream of papers each proposing a different way to break the
degenerate solution, with limited theory saying which should work.

## The five ways it collapses

"Collapse" is used loosely to mean several distinct failures. Distinguishing them
is diagnostically useful, because they look different in the metrics.

<Steps>

1. **Complete collapse.** Every input maps to the same vector. Loss ≈ 0, embedding
   variance ≈ 0. Unmistakable.

2. **Dimensional collapse.** Embeddings vary, but only within a low-dimensional
   subspace of the space you allocated. A 768-dim encoder effectively using 30
   dimensions. Loss looks fine; the singular-value spectrum of the embedding
   matrix has a cliff. This is the common one and it is easy to miss.

3. **Cluster collapse.** Everything maps to a small number of points. Variance is
   non-zero, so naive checks pass, but within-class structure is gone.

4. **Informational collapse.** Dimensions are individually varied but heavily
   correlated with one another — the *effective* rank is far below the nominal
   one. This is precisely what Barlow Twins and VICReg's covariance term target.

5. **Shortcut collapse.** The encoder solves the task with a spurious feature that
   happens to be predictable — colour histogram, JPEG artefacts, border pixels.
   Not degenerate by any variance measure; just useless downstream.

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution" title="Always check the spectrum, not just the variance">
Total embedding variance can look healthy while the representation lives on a
handful of directions. Compute the singular values of a batch of embeddings and
look at the decay. If a small number of components carry nearly all the energy,
you have dimensional collapse regardless of what the loss curve says.
</Aside>

## The four anti-collapse mechanisms

Every method in this space uses one of four strategies. This table is the most
useful thing on the page.

| Mechanism | How it works | Representative | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contrastive repulsion** | explicit term pushing unlike pairs apart | SimCLR, MoCo, CPC | needs many negatives; large batches or a queue |
| **Asymmetry** | stop-gradient + EMA teacher + predictor head | BYOL, SimSiam, DINO, I-JEPA | no guarantee; sensitive to EMA rate |
| **Redundancy reduction** | drive cross-correlation matrix toward identity | Barlow Twins | $O(d^2)$ in embedding dim |
| **Variance floor** | hinge loss forcing per-dimension std above a threshold | VICReg | explicit, tunable, three terms to balance |

The asymmetry row is the interesting one, because it is the least principled and
the most widely used. SimSiam's contribution was showing that the EMA is *not*
required — stop-gradient plus a predictor head alone prevents collapse. That
narrowed the mystery without resolving it.

## The lineage, briefly

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="CPC → BYOL">

**CPC (2018)** introduced the predict-in-latent-space framing with InfoNCE, and
is the direct ancestor. It still used negatives.

**BYOL (2020)** was the surprise: it removed negatives entirely and still worked.
The community initially suspected batch normalisation was providing implicit
contrast; follow-up work showed BYOL works without BN given careful
initialisation, so the asymmetry really is doing the work.

**SimSiam (2020)** stripped it further — no EMA, no large batches, just
stop-gradient and a predictor — and still no collapse. The paper frames the
mechanism as an implicit expectation-maximisation, which remains the best
available intuition but not a proof.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Barlow / VICReg">

These attack collapse *statistically* rather than architecturally, and are worth
knowing because their objectives are transparent in a way the asymmetry methods
are not.

**Barlow Twins** computes the cross-correlation matrix between embeddings of two
views and pushes it toward the identity: diagonal to 1 (invariance), off-diagonal
to 0 (decorrelation). One loss, one hyperparameter, no negatives, no asymmetry.

**VICReg** makes the three concerns explicit and separable: **V**ariance (hinge
each dimension's std above a floor), **I**nvariance (MSE between views),
**C**ovariance (penalise off-diagonal covariance). Because the terms are
separate, you can diagnose which one is failing — which is why VICReg is a good
teaching example even where it is not the top performer.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="DINO → I-JEPA → V-JEPA">

**DINO (2021)** is self-distillation with centring and sharpening of the teacher
output to prevent collapse. Its famous result is emergent: attention maps segment
objects with no segmentation supervision at all. **DINOv2** scaled the recipe and
produced the general-purpose visual features most practitioners now reach for.

**I-JEPA (2023)** made the decisive change: drop augmentations, use *masking*.
Predict the representations of several target blocks from one context block. No
hand-specified invariances at all — which removes the standard objection to
contrastive methods, that you chose the invariances yourself and may have chosen
wrong.

**V-JEPA (2024)** carried this to video, where the temporal dimension supplies the
prediction task naturally. **V-JEPA 2 (2025)** added an action-conditioned
predictor and reached zero-shot robot planning, which is where this page connects
to [world models](/world-models/).

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## The objective

Let $x$ be the input, $x_{\text{ctx}}$ a context view, $x_{\text{tgt}}^{(i)}$ the
$i$-th target view, and $m_i$ a positional specification of that target. With
student parameters $\theta$, predictor $\phi$, and teacher $\bar\theta$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{JEPA}} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \Big\| P_\phi\big(E_\theta(x_{\text{ctx}}),\, m_i\big) \;-\; \operatorname{sg}\big[E_{\bar\theta}(x_{\text{tgt}}^{(i)})\big] \Big\|_1,
$$

$$
\bar\theta \;\leftarrow\; \lambda \bar\theta + (1-\lambda)\theta, \qquad \lambda \in [0.996, 1.0)\ \text{on a schedule}.
$$

Three details that are easy to skim past and shouldn't be:

**$\ell_1$ rather than $\ell_2$.** Embedding dimensions have no natural scale, and
squared error is dominated by whichever few dimensions happen to have large
magnitude. $\ell_1$ weights dimensions more evenly.

**The predictor is conditioned on $m_i$.** Without knowing *where* the target is,
the best prediction is the average over all possible targets — which is a
blurring pressure toward collapse. Positional conditioning makes the task
well-posed.

**$\lambda$ schedules toward 1.** Early in training the teacher tracks the
student closely (fast learning); late in training it is nearly frozen (stable
targets). Fixing $\lambda$ at either extreme degrades results.

## Why the asymmetry works — the argument, and its limits

Consider the degenerate solution $E_\theta(\cdot) \equiv c$. For the loss to
vanish we need $E_{\bar\theta}(\cdot) \equiv c$ too. But $\bar\theta$ is an
exponential moving average, so it reaches $c$ only after the student has been
approximately constant for $O(1/(1-\lambda))$ steps — thousands of steps at
$\lambda = 0.999$. Throughout that window the loss is non-zero and its gradient
pushes the student away from $c$.

Formally, write the student's update as gradient descent on
$\|P_\phi(E_\theta(x)) - \operatorname{sg}[E_{\bar\theta}(x')]\|$. Because of the
$\operatorname{sg}$, the target is a *constant* from the optimiser's perspective.
The student is chasing a point it cannot move. Collapse would require the student
and teacher to converge to a constant *simultaneously*, and the EMA lag makes that
trajectory dynamically unstable.

<Aside type="note" title="This is an argument about dynamics, not a theorem">
There is no proof that the asymmetry prevents collapse, and empirically it fails
when $\lambda$ is too small — the teacher tracks the student closely enough that
the pair *can* descend to a constant together. The mechanism is real, the
guarantee is not, and papers in this area are generally honest about that.
SimSiam's EM interpretation is the most developed theoretical account and it is
still an interpretation.
</Aside>

## VICReg, term by term

The clearest formalisation of collapse prevention, because each concern gets its
own term. For a batch of embeddings $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ and $Z'$ from
the second view:

$$
v(Z) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d} \max\big(0,\; \gamma - \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_{:,j}) + \epsilon}\big)
$$

$$
c(Z) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i \neq j} \big[C(Z)\big]_{i,j}^2, \qquad C(Z) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(z_k - \bar z)(z_k - \bar z)^{\top}
$$

$$
s(Z, Z') = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|z_k - z'_k\|_2^2
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VICReg}} = \mu\, s(Z,Z') + \alpha\big[v(Z) + v(Z')\big] + \nu\big[c(Z) + c(Z')\big].
$$

Read the roles directly off the algebra:

- $v$ is a **hinge**: it is zero once each dimension's standard deviation exceeds
  $\gamma$ (typically 1), and grows only when a dimension starts to die. This
  directly forbids complete and dimensional collapse.
- $c$ penalises off-diagonal covariance, forcing dimensions to carry
  *non-redundant* information. This is the term aimed at informational collapse.
- $s$ is the only term that wants the two views to agree — on its own it would
  collapse immediately.

The three-way balance is the whole method, and the weights matter: $\alpha$ too
low and variance dies, $\nu$ too high and the embedding is forced to be
artificially isotropic.

## Barlow Twins

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}} = \sum_{i} (1 - \mathcal{C}_{ii})^2 + \beta \sum_{i}\sum_{j \neq i} \mathcal{C}_{ij}^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{C}_{ij} = \frac{\sum_b z^A_{b,i} z^B_{b,j}}{\sqrt{\sum_b (z^A_{b,i})^2}\sqrt{\sum_b (z^B_{b,j})^2}} .
$$

$\mathcal{C}$ is the cross-correlation between the two views' embeddings, computed
over the batch. Driving it to the identity says: dimension $i$ of view A should
agree with dimension $i$ of view B (invariance) and be uncorrelated with every
other dimension (decorrelation).

The information-theoretic reading is that this maximises information content
subject to invariance — the same objective as the information bottleneck,
approached through second-order statistics rather than density estimation. Note
the $O(d^2)$ cost in embedding dimension, which is why these methods use large
projector heads but bounded embedding widths.

## Relation to the information bottleneck

All of these optimise, implicitly, the same trade-off:

$$
\max_\theta \; I(Z; Y) - \beta\, I(Z; X)
$$

with $Y$ the unknown downstream target. The families differ in how they keep
$I(Z;X)$ from hitting zero without access to $Y$:

- reconstruction sets a **floor** on $I(Z;X)$ by requiring invertibility;
- contrastive learning adds an explicit **repulsive** term;
- redundancy reduction enforces a **rank** condition on second-order statistics;
- asymmetry relies on a **dynamical** obstruction.

Only the first three admit a static characterisation. The fourth — the most used —
does not, and that is the honest state of the theory.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**JEPAs work, and the reasons remain partly empirical.** I-JEPA and V-JEPA produce
strong general-purpose features without augmentations or negatives, and V-JEPA 2
extended this to action-conditioned prediction supporting zero-shot robot planning
at roughly 65–80% success on manipulation tasks in unseen labs. That is a real
capability that did not exist three years earlier.

**The reconstruct-versus-predict argument is not settled.** Masked autoencoders
reconstruct raw pixels and remain competitive, which the theoretical argument says
they shouldn't be. The usual reconciliation — that a 75% mask ratio makes the task
semantic enough that pixel detail stops dominating the gradient — is plausible and
unproven. Treat "latent prediction is strictly better" as directional.

**DINOv2 is the practical default, and it is a self-distillation method rather
than a JEPA proper.** Worth noting because the taxonomy and the leaderboard don't
line up: the most-used general visual encoder is from the DINO line, not the JEPA
line, even though they share the asymmetry mechanism.

## Open problems

- **No theory of collapse prevention.** Given a proposed asymmetry, nothing
  predicts whether it suffices. Every new method establishes this by running it.

- **The predictor's role is unclear.** SimSiam showed the predictor head is
  necessary and the EMA is not. Why a small MLP between student output and loss
  changes the optimisation landscape so decisively is unexplained.

- **Masking introduces its own biases.** I-JEPA's pitch is that it avoids
  hand-specified invariances — but block masking encodes assumptions about spatial
  scale and locality. Whether those are meaningfully weaker assumptions than
  augmentations is asserted more than demonstrated.

- **Embedding spaces still aren't interoperable.** Two JEPAs trained identically
  from different seeds produce incomparable spaces. This forces hierarchical
  systems to train levels jointly in a shared space rather than composing
  independently trained encoders.

- **Evaluation remains circular.** Linear probing is the standard metric and is
  known to be a poor proxy; alignment/uniformity is better but defined relative to
  an augmentation family, which is precisely what these methods claim to avoid
  needing.
