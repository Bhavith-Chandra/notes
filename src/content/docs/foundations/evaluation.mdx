<Abstract>
Every claim in machine learning rests on a measurement, and the measurements are
in worse shape than the models. Benchmarks leak into training data, the standard
comparison is reported without error bars, and the metric you choose can
manufacture a discontinuity that is not in the underlying model. None of this
means evaluation is hopeless — it means reading a number requires knowing how it
was produced.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Contamination is the default assumption, not the exception.** If a benchmark predates a model's training cutoff and exists on the public internet, assume some of it was seen. The burden of proof is on the claim of cleanliness.
- **A 1-point difference on a 1,000-item benchmark is noise.** The standard error is around 1.5 points. Most reported model comparisons do not clear their own error bars, and almost none report them.
- **Emergence may be a property of your metric.** Exact-match accuracy is discontinuous by construction; swap to a continuous metric and many "sudden" capability jumps become smooth curves. The models may not be doing anything abrupt.
- **LLM judges are cheap, correlate well with humans, and share their biases.** They prefer longer answers, favour the first option presented, and rate their own family's outputs higher. Optimising against a judge is reward hacking one level up.

</KeyPoints>

## The exam you accidentally published

You write an exam to test students. You post it online with the answer key so
others can use it. Next year's students study from the internet.

They ace your exam. Did they learn the subject, or did they memorise your paper?

**This is the situation every public benchmark is in.** MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K —
all published, all scraped, all plausibly inside the training corpus of any model
released afterwards. Their scores are some mixture of capability and recall, and
the mixture is not measurable from the outside.

## Why one number is not enough

"Model A scores 87.3, model B scores 86.9."

Before treating that as A winning, three questions:

**How many items?** On a 1,000-question benchmark, the standard error on a score
near 87% is about 1.1 points. A 0.4-point gap is well inside the noise.

**How was it prompted?** Few-shot examples, their order, the answer format, and
whether chain-of-thought was allowed can move scores by several points — more than
most reported differences between models.

**Was it contaminated?** If A's training data included the test set and B's did
not, you have measured data curation, not capability.

<Aside type="caution" title="The reasonable default">
Treat differences under about two points on a standard benchmark as
indistinguishable unless the paper reports confidence intervals and a matched
prompting protocol. Most do not. This is not cynicism — it is what the sample size
supports.
</Aside>

## The emergence argument

A widely-cited observation: some capabilities appear *suddenly* at scale. Below
some model size, near-zero performance; above it, the ability appears.

Schaeffer et al. proposed a deflationary explanation worth taking seriously.
Consider a task scored by **exact match** on a five-digit answer. If per-digit
accuracy improves smoothly from 0.70 to 0.95, exact-match accuracy goes

$$
0.70^5 = 0.17 \quad\longrightarrow\quad 0.95^5 = 0.77,
$$

but the curve in between is sharply non-linear — it looks like a phase transition
because the *metric* is a product of five smooth terms, not because anything
discontinuous happened inside the model.

Swap to per-digit accuracy or log-likelihood and the sharpness vanishes.

**This does not prove emergence is never real.** It shows that a discontinuous
metric can manufacture the appearance of one, so the burden falls on the claimant
to show the discontinuity survives a continuous measure.

## Contamination, and how to detect it

The problem is straightforward and the detection is not, because you generally do
not have access to the training corpus.

<Steps>

1. **N-gram overlap.** Search the training data for verbatim spans from the test
   set. Only possible with open data, and defeated by paraphrase.

2. **Canary strings.** Benchmarks embed a unique GUID and ask trainers to filter
   on it. Depends entirely on good faith and on the filter running.

3. **Memorisation probes.** Give the model the first half of a test item and see
   whether it completes the second half verbatim. Suggestive rather than
   conclusive.

4. **Temporal split.** Evaluate only on items created *after* the model's training
   cutoff. The most reliable method available, and it means benchmarks have a
   shelf life.

5. **Perturbation.** Rewrite items while preserving the answer. A large drop
   suggests the model was matching surface form rather than reasoning.

</Steps>

The structural fix is **dynamic benchmarks** — continuously refreshed with new
items, so there is no fixed set to memorise. SWE-bench is a partial example: it
draws from real GitHub issues, and new issues keep appearing.

## LLM judges

Using a strong model to score outputs is cheap, fast, and correlates with human
preference at roughly 80% agreement — which is close to the agreement rate
*between humans*.

The biases are documented and consistent:

| Bias | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Length** | prefers longer responses regardless of quality | control for length; report length alongside score |
| **Position** | prefers whichever option came first | evaluate both orders, average |
| **Self-preference** | rates its own family's outputs higher | never judge with a model related to one under test |
| **Style over substance** | confident formatting beats correct content | use reference answers where possible |

<Aside type="danger" title="Judges are a proxy, and proxies get gamed">
This is the same structure as [reward hacking](/training/post-training/). If you
optimise a model against a judge, you will find the judge's blind spots — verbosity
and confident formatting most reliably. A judge used for *measurement* is
reasonable; a judge used as a *training signal* needs the same scepticism you would
apply to any learned reward model.
</Aside>

## Confidence intervals, which almost nobody reports

Benchmark accuracy is a binomial proportion. For $n$ items and observed accuracy
$\hat p$:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p (1 - \hat p)}{n}}, \qquad
\text{95\% CI} \approx \hat p \pm 1.96\,\operatorname{SE}.
$$

For MMLU-scale evaluation, $n \approx 14{,}000$ and $\hat p = 0.85$:

$$
\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.85 \times 0.15}{14000}} \approx 0.0030,
$$

so the CI is roughly $\pm 0.6$ points. For a 1,000-item subject subset:

$$
\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.85 \times 0.15}{1000}} \approx 0.0113 \implies \pm 2.2 \text{ points}.
$$

**A two-point difference on a thousand-item benchmark is not significant.** Read
per-subject MMLU breakdowns with this in mind — most of those subsets are a few
hundred items, giving error bars of ±4 points or more.

## Use a paired test

Comparing two models on the *same* items means the observations are paired, and
treating them as independent throws away power and inflates the variance estimate.

McNemar's test uses only the discordant pairs. Let $b$ be the count where model A
is right and B wrong, $c$ the reverse:

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c}, \qquad \text{df} = 1.
$$

Items both models get right or both get wrong carry no information about which is
better, and excluding them is why the paired test is more sensitive. For small
$b+c$, use the exact binomial test on $b \sim \operatorname{Bin}(b+c, 0.5)$
instead.

## The emergence argument, formalised

Let per-token accuracy be $a(N)$, smooth and increasing in model size $N$. For a
task requiring $L$ tokens all correct:

$$
\operatorname{EM}(N) = a(N)^{L}.
$$

Differentiating:

$$
\frac{d\,\operatorname{EM}}{dN} = L\, a(N)^{L-1}\,a'(N).
$$

For small $a$ this is near zero (the $a^{L-1}$ factor crushes it); as $a \to 1$ it
approaches $L\,a'(N)$. So the exact-match curve is **flat, then steep, then
saturating** — an S-curve — while $a(N)$ itself is smooth throughout.

The sharpness scales with $L$: longer required outputs produce sharper apparent
transitions from identical underlying improvement.

**The diagnostic.** Plot a continuous metric — token-level accuracy, edit
distance, or log-likelihood of the correct answer — on the same axis. If the
sharpness survives, the discontinuity is in the model. If it dissolves, it was in
the metric.

## Scoring rules

Accuracy discards the model's confidence. For a calibrated view use the **Brier
score**:

$$
\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(p_i - y_i\big)^2,
$$

which is *proper* — uniquely minimised by reporting your true belief, so it cannot
be gamed by strategic over- or under-confidence. It decomposes as

$$
\text{BS} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(p - \bar y_{p})^2\big]}_{\text{calibration}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\bar y_{p} - \bar y)^2\big]}_{\text{resolution}} + \underbrace{\bar y(1-\bar y)}_{\text{irreducible}},
$$

separating "are your stated probabilities honest" from "do they discriminate".
Two models can share an accuracy and differ enormously on both.

## Elo and its assumptions

Chatbot Arena ranks models by pairwise human preference using an Elo-style rating.
Under the Bradley–Terry model — the same one behind [reward
modelling](/training/post-training/) — the probability A beats B is

$$
P(A \succ B) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}.
$$

Three assumptions worth surfacing, because they are load-bearing and often
violated:

- **Transitivity.** If A beats B and B beats C then A beats C. Real preferences on
  heterogeneous tasks are not reliably transitive.
- **A single latent dimension.** One number per model, so "better at code, worse at
  prose" is not representable.
- **A representative query distribution.** The rating reflects what Arena users
  happen to ask, which is not what your users ask.

Arena is the most robust preference signal available and it measures preference
under a particular distribution — not capability in general.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Static benchmarks saturate and then mislead.** MMLU, HumanEval and GSM8K are all
in the range where remaining headroom is dominated by label noise and ambiguous
items. Improvements at the top are partly fitting the errors in the test set.

**The field has moved toward held-out, dynamic, and agentic evaluation.**
Benchmarks built from post-cutoff material, continuously refreshed item pools, and
tasks with programmatic verification. SWE-bench-style evaluation — real issues,
real test suites — is now more informative than any multiple-choice benchmark.

**Arena-style human preference is the most trusted single signal**, and it is
measuring preference on a particular query distribution, with all the caveats
above. It is not a capability measure and is frequently read as one.

**LLM judges are ubiquitous for development and under-validated for publication.**
They are appropriate for iteration; using one as the primary evidence in a model
comparison requires reporting agreement with human labels on the specific task.

## Open problems

- **No good measure of contamination.** Every detection method is heuristic and
  most require training-data access nobody has.

- **Benchmarks measure what is easy to measure.** Multiple choice, short answers,
  verifiable outputs. The things people actually want — judgement, taste,
  reliability over long horizons — resist automatic scoring entirely.

- **Construct validity is rarely examined.** Does MMLU measure "massive multitask
  language understanding"? It measures the ability to answer those particular
  multiple-choice questions. The gap between the two is assumed away.

- **Agentic evaluation is stochastic and expensive.** The same task can succeed or
  fail across seeds. Getting a stable estimate needs many runs, and many runs cost
  real money, so most reported agent numbers rest on very few samples.

- **No standard reporting protocol.** Prompting, few-shot count, decoding
  temperature, and answer extraction all vary between papers and all move scores by
  more than the differences being claimed.
