<Abstract>
A language model never sees text. It sees integers, and something upstream
decided which integers. That decision is made once, before training, by a
compression algorithm from 1994 that knows nothing about language — and it
propagates into everything downstream: why models miscount letters, why some
languages cost four times more to serve, why certain strings make models
behave bizarrely, and why "how many r's in strawberry" was ever hard.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The tokenizer is frozen before the model exists.** It is fitted on a corpus, its vocabulary is fixed, and every downstream property inherits from it. You cannot fix a tokenization problem by training longer.
- **BPE is a compression algorithm, not a linguistic one.** It merges frequent adjacent pairs. Nothing in it knows about morphemes, words or meaning — that it produces roughly morpheme-like units for English is a side effect of English statistics.
- **Models cannot see inside tokens.** `strawberry` may be three tokens, none of which is a letter. Asking how many r's it contains is asking about information the model never received, and this explains a whole family of "surprisingly stupid" failures.
- **Tokenizers are unfair across languages, measurably.** The same sentence can take 1.5× to over 4× more tokens in some languages than in English — which directly multiplies cost, latency, and effective context length for those speakers.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The problem

Neural networks do arithmetic on numbers. Text is a sequence of characters. Something has to bridge that, and there are only three real options.

<Steps>

1. **One number per word.** Small sequences, and the vocabulary is unbounded —
   there are always new words, names, typos and compounds. Anything unseen becomes
   `<UNK>` and its information is destroyed.

2. **One number per character.** Vocabulary of ~100, nothing is ever unknown, and
   sequences become enormously long. Since [attention is
   quadratic](/architectures/transformers/), long sequences are expensive.

3. **One number per frequent chunk.** Common words stay whole; rare words split
   into pieces. Bounded vocabulary, moderate sequence length, nothing unrepresentable.

</Steps>

Option 3 is **subword tokenization**, and essentially every modern model uses it.

## How the pieces are chosen

The dominant algorithm is **byte-pair encoding**, and it is startlingly simple.

Start with every character as its own token. Then repeat: **find the most frequent
adjacent pair in your corpus, and merge it into a single new token.** Do that
50,000 times. The merges you performed, in order, are your tokenizer.

That is the whole algorithm. Notice what is absent — no grammar, no dictionary, no
notion of a word or a morpheme. It is pure frequency counting, and it was invented
in 1994 as a **data compression** algorithm, then repurposed for NLP two decades
later.

<Aside type="tip" title="Why it produces sensible-looking pieces anyway">
Frequent character sequences in English *are* mostly morphemes — `ing`, `tion`,
`un`, `re` recur constantly because English builds words from them. So BPE
rediscovers morphology by counting, without representing it. That is elegant and it
is also why the pieces get much worse for languages whose statistics differ.
</Aside>

## What this costs you

**Models cannot see inside a token.** If `berry` is one token, the model receives
one integer. The letters are not in there. Asking it to count the r's in
`strawberry` is asking about information that was discarded before the model saw
anything.

This is not a reasoning failure. It is a **representation** failure, and it
explains a cluster of otherwise baffling behaviours: character counting, reversing
strings, rhyming, and some arithmetic.

**Numbers tokenize badly.** Depending on the tokenizer, `1234` might be `1234`, or
`123|4`, or `12|34`. The digit grouping is inconsistent and does not align with
place value, so the model must learn arithmetic across pieces that do not respect
the structure of arithmetic. Modern tokenizers often force single-digit splitting
for exactly this reason.

**Some languages cost far more.** The tokenizer was fitted on a corpus that was
mostly English. A language poorly represented in that corpus gets split into more,
smaller pieces — so the same meaning costs more tokens, more money, more latency,
and consumes more of the context window.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="BPE">

<Steps>

1. Split the corpus into characters.
2. Count every adjacent pair.
3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol; record the merge.
4. Repeat until you reach the target vocabulary size.

</Steps>

**Encoding** applies the recorded merges in the same order — deterministic and
fast.

**Used by**: GPT-2 onward, Llama, most open models.

**Its known weakness** is that greedy frequency is a poor proxy for usefulness.
BPE will happily merge across a morpheme boundary because the pair is common, and
the merge order is a hard commitment made once.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="WordPiece">

Same greedy structure, different scoring. Instead of raw frequency, pick the pair
maximising

$$
\frac{P(\text{merged})}{P(\text{first})\,P(\text{second})}
$$

which is the **pointwise mutual information** between the two pieces.

**What this changes.** Raw frequency favours pairs where either half is itself
common. PMI favours pairs that co-occur *more than chance would predict* — pieces
that genuinely belong together. `th` is extremely frequent and not especially
informative; PMI down-weights it relative to a rarer but more cohesive pair.

**Used by**: BERT, DistilBERT, and the encoder-only family generally.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Unigram">

Inverts the procedure. Start with a large candidate vocabulary, define a
probabilistic model over segmentations, and **iteratively remove** the pieces whose
deletion loses the least likelihood.

**What the inversion buys.** You end with $p(\text{piece})$ for every piece, so a
given string has many possible segmentations, each with a probability. You can find
the best one by Viterbi — or **sample** among them.

That sampling is **subword regularisation**: use a different segmentation of the
same text on each training epoch. It is data augmentation for text, it measurably
improves robustness, and BPE cannot do it because its segmentation is fixed.

**Used by**: T5, ALBERT, XLNet, and multilingual models generally.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="SentencePiece">

Not an algorithm — an **implementation** that runs BPE or Unigram, and the thing
that made tokenization language-agnostic.

**The key design choice**: treat the input as a raw stream including spaces, with
the space encoded as a visible character (`▁`). Previous tokenizers pre-split on
whitespace, which assumes the language *has* whitespace-delimited words. Japanese,
Chinese and Thai do not.

The consequence is that detokenization becomes exactly reversible — concatenate the
pieces and replace `▁` with a space. No language-specific rules, no lossy
round-trip.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Byte-level fallback

What happens when text contains a character the tokenizer never saw — an unusual
emoji, a rare script, corrupted bytes?

**The old answer** was `<UNK>`, which destroys the information irrecoverably.

**GPT-2's answer** was byte-level BPE: run BPE over **raw UTF-8 bytes** rather than
Unicode characters. Since there are only 256 possible bytes and all of them are in
the base vocabulary, *every possible string* is representable. There is no
out-of-vocabulary case, by construction.

**The cost.** A character outside the tokenizer's training distribution splits into
its individual UTF-8 bytes — up to four tokens for one character, none of which is
meaningful on its own. So coverage is universal and *efficiency* is not.

<Aside type="danger" title="This is where multilingual unfairness comes from">
Petrov et al. measured tokenized length for the same content across languages and
found ratios exceeding **4×** relative to English for some languages, with a long
tail of worse cases.

That number is not abstract. API pricing is per token, latency scales with tokens,
and context windows are measured in tokens. **A speaker of a poorly-tokenized
language pays several times more for the same conversation and gets a fraction of
the effective context.** It is a design decision made once, at tokenizer-fitting
time, that produces a persistent cost asymmetry between language communities.
</Aside>

## Glitch tokens

A genuinely strange failure mode, and instructive about how tokenizers and models
interact.

The tokenizer is fitted on one corpus; the model is trained on another. If a string
was frequent enough in the *tokenizer's* corpus to earn a token, but essentially
absent from the *model's* training data, that token's embedding is **barely
trained** — it sits wherever initialisation put it.

Feed such a token to the model and behaviour becomes erratic: refusal to repeat it,
hallucination, evasion, or unrelated output. The `SolidGoldMagikarp` case (a Reddit
username that entered a tokenizer's corpus via scraped data) became the canonical
example.

Land & Bartolo turned this into a detection method — under-trained tokens can be
found automatically by examining embedding statistics, without needing to discover
them by accident. The general lesson: **a mismatch between tokenizer corpus and
model corpus leaves detectable dead weight in the vocabulary.**

## BPE, formally

Let $\mathcal{V}_0$ be the set of base symbols and $C$ the corpus as sequences over
$\mathcal{V}_0$. At each step,

$$
(a, b)^{\star} = \arg\max_{(a,b)}\ \operatorname{count}_C(ab),
$$

$$
\mathcal{V}_{t+1} = \mathcal{V}_t \cup \{ab\}, \qquad C \leftarrow C[ab / (a,b)],
$$

repeated $k$ times for target vocabulary $|\mathcal{V}_0| + k$.

**Complexity.** Naively, recounting all pairs each step is $O(N)$ for corpus length
$N$, giving $O(kN)$. The standard implementation maintains a priority queue over
pair counts and updates only the counts affected by the merge, which is
$O(N + k\log N)$ in practice — this is why fitting a tokenizer on a large corpus is
minutes rather than hours.

**Greedy, therefore not optimal.** BPE maximises the *immediate* count reduction at
each step, and the resulting vocabulary is not the one minimising total encoded
length for its size. Finding that vocabulary is combinatorial, and nobody does it.

**Encoding is a different algorithm from training.** Training learns an ordered
merge list; encoding applies it. Since the merges are applied in learned order,
BPE encoding is deterministic and there is exactly one segmentation per string.

## WordPiece's scoring

$$
\text{score}(a,b) = \frac{P(ab)}{P(a)\,P(b)} = \frac{\operatorname{count}(ab)/N}{\big(\operatorname{count}(a)/N\big)\big(\operatorname{count}(b)/N\big)}.
$$

Taking logs, this is exactly pointwise mutual information:

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(a,b) = \log\frac{P(a,b)}{P(a)P(b)}.
$$

**Why this is the right correction.** Under a unigram model, merging $a$ and $b$
into one symbol changes the corpus log-likelihood by approximately
$\operatorname{count}(ab)\cdot\operatorname{PMI}(a,b)$ — the merge is worth doing in
proportion to both how often the pair occurs *and* how much more often than
independence predicts. Frequency alone captures only the first factor.

## The Unigram language model

Model a segmentation $\mathbf{x} = (x_1,\dots,x_M)$ as independent draws:

$$
P(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^{M} p(x_i), \qquad \sum_{x\in\mathcal{V}} p(x) = 1 .
$$

The probability of a *string* $X$ marginalises over all valid segmentations:

$$
P(X) = \sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S(X)} P(\mathbf{x}),
$$

and the best segmentation is

$$
\mathbf{x}^{\star} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{x}\in S(X)} P(\mathbf{x}),
$$

found by **Viterbi** in $O(|X| \cdot L)$ for maximum piece length $L$.

**Training** is EM over a fixed candidate set:

<Steps>

1. **E-step.** Compute expected counts of each piece via the forward-backward
   algorithm over segmentation lattices.
2. **M-step.** Set $p(x)$ proportional to its expected count.
3. **Prune.** For each piece, compute the **loss** $\ell_x$ — the drop in total
   corpus log-likelihood if $x$ were removed and its occurrences re-segmented.
   Drop the bottom fraction by $\ell_x$.
4. Repeat until the target vocabulary size.

</Steps>

**Subword regularisation** samples from the segmentation posterior instead of
taking the Viterbi path, with a temperature $\alpha$:

$$
P_\alpha(\mathbf{x}\mid X) \;\propto\; P(\mathbf{x})^{\alpha}.
$$

$\alpha \to \infty$ recovers Viterbi (deterministic); $\alpha = 1$ samples from the
true posterior; $\alpha \to 0$ approaches uniform over segmentations. Kudo's result
is that intermediate $\alpha$ improves translation quality, particularly in
low-resource settings — the augmentation matters most when data is scarce.

**BPE-dropout** is the analogous trick for BPE: randomly skip merges during
encoding with probability $p$. Cruder, and it recovers some of the benefit.

## Fertility, and measuring a tokenizer

The standard metric is **fertility** — tokens per word:

$$
F = \frac{\text{number of tokens}}{\text{number of words}} .
$$

$F = 1$ means every word is one token. English on a modern tokenizer sits around
1.3; poorly-supported languages can exceed 4.

**Why compression is not the whole story.** It is tempting to treat lower fertility
as strictly better — fewer tokens, cheaper inference. Schmidt et al. tested this
directly and found compression correlates with downstream performance only weakly.
A tokenizer can compress well by merging across morpheme boundaries, producing
pieces that are efficient and semantically incoherent.

**What else matters**, empirically: alignment of token boundaries with morpheme
boundaries, consistency of a given word's segmentation across contexts, and even
distribution of token frequencies (a vocabulary where a few tokens dominate wastes
capacity).

## Vocabulary size, as a trade-off

Let $V$ be vocabulary size, $d$ the model dimension, $L(V)$ the expected sequence
length. Two costs move in opposite directions:

$$
\underbrace{\text{embedding + unembedding params} = 2Vd}_{\text{grows with } V}
\qquad
\underbrace{\text{attention cost} \propto L(V)^2}_{\text{shrinks with } V}
$$

Larger $V$ means shorter sequences (cheaper attention, more content per context
window) and more parameters spent on the vocabulary — parameters that see very
sparse gradients, since rare tokens appear rarely.

**The empirical sweet spot** has moved upward over time: 30k–50k was standard
around 2019, and 100k–260k is common now. The driver is multilinguality — covering
many scripts requires more pieces — plus the observation that the softmax over $V$
is a small share of total compute in a large model.

**The sparse-gradient problem is real.** A token appearing once per billion tokens
receives essentially no gradient signal, and its embedding stays near
initialisation. That is precisely the glitch-token mechanism, viewed from the
optimisation side.

## Why arithmetic is hard

Consider adding two numbers. Place value requires aligning digits by position. If
the tokenizer produces

$$
1234 \to [\,\texttt{123},\ \texttt{4}\,], \qquad 5678 \to [\,\texttt{56},\ \texttt{78}\,],
$$

the model must recover digit positions from tokens whose boundaries do not
correspond to any place value, and the grouping differs between the two operands.
The information is present but the structure is adversarial.

**The fix is a tokenizer constraint**, not a modelling one: force each digit to be
its own token. Several modern tokenizers do this explicitly, and arithmetic
performance improves measurably. Note what this means — a persistent "reasoning"
weakness was substantially a preprocessing bug.

**The same argument covers character-level tasks.** Counting letters, reversing
strings, and detecting rhymes all require sub-token information. The model can
sometimes reconstruct it from context (it has seen spellings discussed in text) but
it is inference, not perception.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Tokenizer-free models exist and have not displaced subwords.** ByT5 operates on
raw bytes and is genuinely more robust to noise, misspellings and unseen scripts —
at a substantial compute cost, because byte sequences are several times longer and
attention is quadratic.

**Hierarchical byte models are the serious attempt.** MEGABYTE splits the sequence
into patches, runs a large model over patches and a small one within them,
recovering most of the efficiency. **Byte Latent Transformer** (2024) goes further:
patch boundaries are determined **dynamically by the entropy of a small byte-level
model** — allocating more compute where the text is unpredictable and less where it
is not. That is a genuinely different idea from a fixed vocabulary, and its scaling
results are competitive with BPE-based models at matched compute.

**Whether tokenization survives is a live question.** The argument for removal is
that every problem on this page is a tokenizer artefact. The argument against is
that fixed vocabularies are enormously efficient and the efficiency gap has not
closed. As of 2026 production models are overwhelmingly still subword-based.

**Multilingual unfairness is acknowledged and partly addressed.** Newer tokenizers
are fitted on more balanced corpora and are measurably fairer than GPT-2-era ones.
The asymmetry has narrowed and has not disappeared, and it is now standard to report
fertility across languages rather than only aggregate compression.

## Open problems

- **No principled way to choose vocabulary size.** It is swept. The interaction with
  model size, data volume and language coverage is not characterised.

- **Tokenizers cannot be changed after training.** Improving the tokenizer means
  retraining from scratch, so a model is permanently stuck with a decision made
  before it existed. Vocabulary-transfer methods exist and are lossy.

- **Compression is a poor objective and we keep optimising it.** Schmidt et al.
  showed the correlation with downstream quality is weak, and no better fitting
  objective has replaced it.

- **Domain mismatch.** A tokenizer fitted on web text handles code, mathematics and
  protein sequences badly. Domain-specific tokenizers help and fragment the
  ecosystem.

- **Under-trained tokens are detectable and not routinely audited.** The tooling
  exists; checking for them is not standard practice before release.

## Practical guidance

<Card title="Working with tokenizers" icon="setting">
**Always inspect the actual tokenization** of representative inputs before debugging
model behaviour. Many "the model can't do X" reports dissolve on looking at the
token IDs.

**Check fertility on your domain.** If your text tokenizes at 3× the tokenizer's
design point, your costs and effective context are 3× worse than the headline
numbers suggest.

**For arithmetic-heavy work**, prefer a model whose tokenizer splits digits
individually. This is checkable in one line.

**Never assume token count equals word count** for pricing, context budgeting or
truncation logic. Measure it.

**If you fine-tune on a new domain**, the tokenizer is unchanged — added vocabulary
requires embedding surgery, and new tokens start untrained.
</Card>

## Reading path

1. **Sennrich et al. (2015)** — BPE for NMT. Short, and the algorithm is two pages.
2. **Kudo (2018), Subword Regularization** — for the Unigram model and why a
   probabilistic segmentation is worth having.
3. **Petrov et al. (2023)** — the multilingual measurements. Concrete, and it
   reframes tokenization as a fairness question rather than an engineering detail.
4. **Schmidt et al. (2024)** — the argument that compression is the wrong objective.
5. **Pagnoni et al. (2024), Byte Latent Transformer** — the strongest current case
   that fixed vocabularies are avoidable.
