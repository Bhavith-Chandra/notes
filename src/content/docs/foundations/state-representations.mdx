<Abstract>
A *state* is whatever a system carries forward in time to decide what to do next.
That leaves an enormous amount unspecified, and the choices are not
interchangeable — a belief state, a successor representation and a
value-equivalent latent answer different questions and fail in different ways.
This page catalogues every kind, states precisely what each one is *sufficient
for*, and gives the one criterion that unifies them: **a representation is
adequate exactly when discarding what it discards costs you nothing you needed.**
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Sufficiency is always relative to a question.** There is no "correct" state. A representation is sufficient for prediction, or for a fixed reward, or for a family of rewards, or for optimal control — and these are genuinely different, strictly ordered requirements.
- **Markov is the property everything is chasing.** A state is Markov if the future is independent of the past *given* it. Every representation here is an attempt to construct something Markov from observations that are not.
- **The most abstract representation that preserves what you need is the best one.** Extra detail is not free: it costs sample complexity, invites [model exploitation](/world-models/planning-and-mpc/), and does not generalise. This is the whole argument for value-equivalence over reconstruction.
- **Predictive and successor representations are the two genuinely different ideas here.** Both define state by what it *predicts* rather than what it *contains* — PSRs by future observations, successor representations by future state occupancy. Both make certain transfers trivial that are otherwise hard.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## What a state is for

You are playing a game. At each moment you must decide what to do. **What do you
need to remember?**

Not everything you have ever seen — that would be unmanageable and mostly
irrelevant. Not just the current screen either, because a single frame does not
tell you whether the ball is moving up or down.

You need exactly enough that **nothing else from the past would change your
decision.** That is the whole specification, and it has a name.

## The Markov property

A state is **Markov** if, given it, the future is independent of the past. Knowing
how you got here adds nothing.

$$
P(\text{future} \mid \text{state}, \text{past}) = P(\text{future} \mid \text{state})
$$

This is the property everything on this page is trying to construct. Raw
observations usually are *not* Markov — one video frame does not contain velocity,
one sensor reading does not contain the robot's hidden joint temperature — so you
have to build something that is.

<Aside type="tip" title="The two failure directions">
**Too little state** and you cannot act well, because the information you needed is
gone and no amount of computation recovers it. **Too much state** and you need vastly
more data to learn anything, because you are learning over a space where most
distinctions do not matter. Almost every design choice here is a position on that
axis.
</Aside>

## Sufficient for what?

Here is the idea that organises the entire catalogue, and it is worth slowing down
for.

"Sufficient" is not a property of a representation. It is a property of a
**representation and a question together**. Consider four increasingly demanding
questions:

<Steps>

1. **Can I predict the next observation?** The hardest requirement — you must retain
   everything that affects anything you might see, including irrelevant detail.

2. **Can I predict reward under one reward function?** Much weaker. You can discard
   everything that does not affect *this* reward.

3. **Can I predict reward under any reward in a family?** In between. You keep what
   any member of the family might need.

4. **Can I act optimally?** Weakest of all, in a specific sense: two states with
   the same optimal action and value can be merged even if they look completely
   different and predict different futures.

</Steps>

**These are strictly ordered.** Anything sufficient for (1) is sufficient for the
rest. The reverse fails — and that failure is not a defect, it is the *point*. A
representation sufficient only for (4) can be enormously more compact, and
compactness is what buys sample efficiency.

## The families

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Raw & engineered">

**Raw observation.** Use $o_t$ directly. Simple, and almost never Markov — a single
frame has no velocity, a single reading has no trend.

**Frame stacking.** Concatenate the last $k$ observations. The standard fix in
Atari (DQN used $k=4$), and it works because velocity is recoverable from
consecutive frames. Fails when the required memory exceeds $k$, and the state
grows linearly in $k$.

**Hand-engineered features.** A domain expert writes down what matters: joint
angles, ball position, inventory counts. Compact, interpretable, and it caps you at
what the expert thought of. Still the right answer for well-understood
low-dimensional systems, and this is under-appreciated — a robot with accurate
proprioception rarely needs a learned encoder.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Belief states">

The principled answer for partial observability. Maintain a **distribution** over
the true hidden state:

$$
b_t(s) = P\big(s_t = s \mid o_{1:t},\, a_{1:t-1}\big).
$$

Updated by Bayes' rule as observations arrive. **The belief state is provably
Markov and provably sufficient** — a POMDP over observations becomes a fully
observed MDP over beliefs.

**Why this is not the end of the story.** The belief is a probability distribution,
so for $|S|$ discrete states it lives on an $(|S|-1)$-simplex — continuous, and
high-dimensional the moment $|S|$ is large. Planning in belief space is
PSPACE-hard in general. It is the correct object and rarely a tractable one.

Modern latent-state models are best read as **learned approximations to belief
states**: an RSSM's stochastic latent is doing the job of $b_t$, learned rather than
computed.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Predictive states">

A genuinely different idea, and underrated.

Instead of "what is the hidden state?", ask "**what will I observe if I do
things?**" A *test* is a sequence of actions and the observations they would
produce. The predictive state is the vector of probabilities of a set of core
tests succeeding.

$$
p_t = \big[\,P(t_1 \mid h_t),\ P(t_2\mid h_t),\ \dots,\ P(t_k\mid h_t)\,\big].
$$

**Why this matters.** A PSR is grounded entirely in **observable quantities**. There
is no hidden state to posit, no model of unobservables to get wrong, and the
representation is verifiable against data by construction. Littman et al. showed
PSRs are at least as compact as POMDPs and sometimes exponentially more so.

**Why they stayed niche.** Discovering the core tests is hard, learning algorithms
were fragile, and deep latent models arrived and worked well enough empirically.
The idea is better than its adoption, and it is worth knowing because it reappears:
**a JEPA world model defines state by what it predicts, which is the same move.**

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Successor representation">

Dayan's 1993 idea, and one of the most elegant here.

Separate **where you will go** from **what it is worth**. The successor
representation stores expected discounted future occupancy:

$$
M(s, s') = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,\mathbb{1}\{s_t = s'\}\ \Big|\ s_0 = s\right].
$$

Then for any reward vector $r$:

$$
V(s) = \sum_{s'} M(s,s')\,r(s') = \big[Mr\big](s).
$$

**The payoff is immediate.** Change the reward, and you recompute the value with a
single matrix-vector product — **no re-learning of dynamics at all**. The SR
captures the environment's structure independent of what you want from it.

**Successor features** generalise this to continuous spaces: replace state
indicators with features $\phi(s)$ and assume $r(s) = \phi(s)^\top w$. Then
$\psi(s) = \mathbb{E}[\sum \gamma^t \phi(s_t)]$ and $Q(s,a) = \psi(s,a)^\top w$ —
transfer across any reward that is linear in your features.

**It is also a genuine model of biology.** Successor-representation-like coding has
been proposed for hippocampal place cells, which is unusually strong convergence
between an RL construct and neuroscience.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Bisimulation">

**Merge states that behave identically.** Two states are bisimilar if they have the
same immediate reward and transition to bisimilar states with the same
probabilities. It is the correct notion of "these are the same situation" for
control.

Exact bisimulation is too brittle — a floating-point difference breaks it — so
Ferns et al. defined a **bisimulation metric**:

$$
d(s_i, s_j) = \max_a\Big[\,\big|r_{s_i}^a - r_{s_j}^a\big| + \gamma\,W_1\big(P^a_{s_i},\, P^a_{s_j};\, d\big)\Big],
$$

with $W_1$ the Wasserstein distance under $d$ itself — a fixed point, computed by
iteration.

**DBC** (Zhang et al. 2020) made this practical: learn an encoder whose latent
distances *approximate* the bisimulation metric. The result is a representation
that provably ignores task-irrelevant visual variation — background clutter,
distractor objects, lighting — because those do not affect reward or transitions.

**This is the theoretically cleanest answer to "what should a latent state
contain?"** and the practical difficulty is that estimating the Wasserstein term
from samples is noisy.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Value-equivalent">

The most aggressive abstraction. Do not require the latent to represent the
observation *at all* — require only that it predicts reward, value and policy
correctly.

Grimm et al. formalised it: two models are value-equivalent with respect to a
policy set $\Pi$ and value set $\mathcal{V}$ if they induce identical Bellman
updates:

$$
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m_1} v = \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m_2} v \quad \forall\, \pi\in\Pi,\ v\in\mathcal{V}.
$$

**The key theorem**: the space of value-equivalent models *shrinks* as $\Pi$ and
$\mathcal{V}$ grow, and searching over value-equivalent models is strictly easier
than searching over accurate ones. You are not approximating the world; you are
approximating its *decision-relevant shadow*.

MuZero is the flagship. Its latent has no decoder and cannot be turned back into a
game screen — see the [world model catalogue](/world-models/catalogue/).

**The cost**: the representation is defined relative to a reward, so it does not
transfer to a new task, and it cannot be learned from reward-free data.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Object-centric">

Represent the scene as a **set of objects**, each with its own vector, rather than
one monolithic embedding.

**Slot Attention** is the standard mechanism: $K$ "slots" compete via an attention
softmax *over slots* rather than over inputs, so each input feature is assigned to
one slot, and slots iteratively refine to bind to distinct objects.

**Why anyone wants this.** Compositional generalisation. If a scene is a set of
object vectors, a model can in principle handle three cubes having only seen two,
because the representation composes. A monolithic embedding of "two cubes" has no
structure that extends.

**Why it has not taken over.** It works on synthetic scenes with clean object
boundaries and degrades on natural images, where "object" is not well-defined
(is a person's arm an object?). The binding problem — which features belong to
which object — is not solved in general.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## How the modern learned versions are trained

The families above are *what* to represent. These are *how* to get there from
pixels.

| Method | Signal | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Reconstruction** (PlaNet, Dreamer) | rebuild the observation | retains everything, including irrelevant detail |
| **Contrastive** (CURL) | augmented views of the same state agree | invariance to whatever you augmented |
| **Self-predictive** (SPR) | predict your own future latents | dynamics-aware, no decoder, no negatives |
| **Bisimulation** (DBC) | latent distance ≈ behavioural distance | provably discards task-irrelevant variation |
| **Value-equivalent** (MuZero) | predict r, v, π | maximally compact, reward-specific |
| **Augmentation only** (DrQ) | none — just augment the input | often matches the above |

<Aside type="danger" title="The DrQ result, which the field partly ignored">
Kostrikov et al. showed that simply applying random crops and shifts to the input —
with **no** representation-learning objective at all — matched or beat CURL and
several contemporaries on the standard pixel-control benchmarks.

The implication is uncomfortable and worth stating: a substantial part of what was
attributed to clever representation learning was regularisation. **Run the
augmentation-only baseline before believing a representation-learning result.** This
is the same lesson as [DLinear in forecasting](/domains/time-series/), in a
different subfield.
</Aside>

## Sufficiency, formally

Let $\phi: \mathcal{O}^* \to \mathcal{Z}$ map histories to a representation. Define
increasingly weak sufficiency conditions.

**Observation-sufficient (predictive).** For all histories $h, h'$:

$$
\phi(h) = \phi(h') \implies P\big(o_{t+1:\infty}\mid h,\, a_{t:\infty}\big) = P\big(o_{t+1:\infty}\mid h',\, a_{t:\infty}\big).
$$

**Reward-sufficient (model-irrelevance abstraction).**

$$
\phi(s) = \phi(s') \implies R(s,a) = R(s',a)\ \wedge\ \sum_{s''\in\phi^{-1}(z)}\!\!P(s''\mid s,a) = \!\!\sum_{s''\in\phi^{-1}(z)}\!\!P(s''\mid s',a)\ \ \forall a, z.
$$

**$Q^\pi$-irrelevance.** $\phi(s)=\phi(s') \implies Q^\pi(s,a) = Q^{\pi}(s',a)$ for
all $\pi, a$.

**$a^*$-irrelevance.** $\phi(s)=\phi(s') \implies \arg\max_a Q^*(s,a) =
\arg\max_a Q^*(s',a)$.

Li, Walsh & Littman's hierarchy:

$$
\text{observation} \subset \text{model-irrelevance} \subset Q^\pi \subset Q^* \subset a^* .
$$

**Read the containments carefully.** Each is a *set of abstractions*, and the
larger sets contain coarser abstractions. So $a^*$-irrelevance permits the most
aggressive merging — and their theorem is that value iteration converges to the
optimal policy under model-irrelevance and $Q^*$-irrelevance abstractions, but
**not necessarily** under $a^*$-irrelevance, because merging states with the same
optimal action can break the Bellman backup. Aggressiveness has a limit and it is a
precise one.

## Belief states

For a POMDP $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},T,O,R,\gamma)$, the belief update
is

$$
b'(s') = \frac{O(o\mid s',a)\sum_{s} T(s'\mid s,a)\,b(s)}{P(o\mid b,a)},
\qquad
P(o\mid b,a) = \sum_{s'}O(o\mid s',a)\sum_s T(s'\mid s,a)b(s).
$$

**The sufficiency theorem.** $b_t$ is a sufficient statistic of the history for
predicting the future — no additional information from $o_{1:t}$ improves
prediction. So the POMDP over $\mathcal{S}$ is exactly an MDP over the belief
simplex $\Delta(\mathcal{S})$.

**And the catch.** $\Delta(\mathcal{S})$ is $(|S|-1)$-dimensional and continuous.
The optimal value function over beliefs is piecewise linear and convex —
representable as a set of $\alpha$-vectors:

$$
V^{*}(b) = \max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\ \alpha^{\top} b ,
$$

but $|\Gamma|$ grows doubly exponentially in the horizon in the worst case. Exact
POMDP planning is PSPACE-complete for finite horizons and undecidable for infinite
ones. **This intractability is why the field learns approximate latent states
instead**, and an RSSM's stochastic path is precisely a learned, compressed stand-in
for $b_t$.

## Predictive state representations

A **test** is $t = a_1o_1a_2o_2\cdots a_ko_k$. Its prediction given history $h$ is

$$
p(t \mid h) = P\big(o_1\cdots o_k \mid h,\, a_1\cdots a_k\big).
$$

The **system dynamics matrix** $D$ has rows indexed by histories, columns by tests,
entries $p(t\mid h)$. A set of tests $Q = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$ is a **core set** if
its columns span the column space of $D$; then every test's prediction is a
function of the core predictions:

$$
p(t \mid h) = f_t\big(p(q_1\mid h),\dots,p(q_k\mid h)\big),
$$

linear in the case of linear PSRs. The predictive state is
$p(h) = [p(q_1\mid h),\dots,p(q_k\mid h)]^\top$, and it is updated in closed form
after each action-observation pair:

$$
p_i(hao) = \frac{m_{aoq_i}^{\top}\,p(h)}{m_{ao}^{\top}\,p(h)} .
$$

**The compactness result**: the number of core tests equals $\operatorname{rank}(D)$,
which is at most $|S|$ and can be far smaller. So a PSR is never larger than the
equivalent POMDP and is sometimes exponentially smaller.

**The conceptual point, which is the durable one.** State is *defined by
predictions about observables*. There is no unobservable posited, so the
representation is falsifiable against data. This is exactly the move a
[JEPA world model](/architectures/jepa/) makes — define the latent by what it
predicts — arrived at independently two decades earlier.

## Successor representation and features

Tabular SR, as a matrix:

$$
M = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,P^{t} = \big(I - \gamma P\big)^{-1},
$$

the resolvent of the transition matrix. Value becomes $V = M r$ — **linear in the
reward**. Change $r$, recompute in one product.

It obeys a Bellman equation of its own, so it is learnable by TD:

$$
M(s,s') \leftarrow M(s,s') + \alpha\Big[\mathbb{1}\{s = s'\} + \gamma M(s_{t+1}, s') - M(s,s')\Big].
$$

**Successor features.** Assume $r(s,a,s') = \phi(s,a,s')^{\top}w$. Define

$$
\psi^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\phi_{t+1}\ \Big|\ s_t=s, a_t=a\right],
\qquad
Q^{\pi}_{w}(s,a) = \psi^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top} w .
$$

**Generalised policy improvement.** Given policies $\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n$ with known
$\psi^{\pi_i}$, and a *new* reward $w$, the policy

$$
\pi(s) = \arg\max_a\ \max_i\ \psi^{\pi_i}(s,a)^{\top}w
$$

is guaranteed at least as good as every $\pi_i$ on the new task — with **no
learning at all**. That is a genuine transfer theorem, and it holds whenever the
new reward is linear in $\phi$.

**The limitation is exactly that condition.** Rewards outside the span of $\phi$ get
no guarantee, and choosing $\phi$ is the unsolved part.

## Bisimulation metrics

$$
d(s_i,s_j) = \max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big[\big|R(s_i,a) - R(s_j,a)\big| + \gamma\,W_1\big(P(\cdot\mid s_i,a),\ P(\cdot\mid s_j,a);\ d\big)\Big].
$$

$d$ appears on both sides — it is defined as the fixed point of an operator
$\mathcal{F}$. Ferns et al. showed $\mathcal{F}$ is a contraction with modulus
$\gamma$, so by Banach the fixed point exists and is unique, and value iteration
on the metric converges.

**The value bound** is what makes it useful:

$$
\big|V^{*}(s_i) - V^{*}(s_j)\big| \;\leq\; \frac{d(s_i,s_j)}{1-\gamma}.
$$

States close in bisimulation distance have close optimal values, so **collapsing
them costs a bounded amount**. This is a guarantee no reconstruction-based
representation offers.

DBC's practical version learns $\phi$ minimising

$$
J(\phi) = \Big(\big\|\phi(s_i) - \phi(s_j)\big\|_1 - \big|\hat r_i - \hat r_j\big| - \gamma\,W_2\big(\hat P(\cdot\mid \bar\phi(s_i), a_i),\ \hat P(\cdot\mid\bar\phi(s_j), a_j)\big)\Big)^2,
$$

using a Gaussian latent dynamics model so the $W_2$ term has a closed form. The
encoder is trained so that **latent distance equals behavioural distance** — task-
irrelevant variation is then provably compressed away, because it contributes
nothing to either term.

## Value equivalence, formally

For model $m = (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R})$, policy set $\Pi$ and value set
$\mathcal{V}$, define

$$
\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\mathcal{V}) = \big\{\, m\ :\ \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m} v = \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m^{*}} v\quad \forall \pi\in\Pi,\ v\in\mathcal{V} \,\big\},
$$

the set of models value-equivalent to the true one.

**Monotonicity.** $\Pi \subseteq \Pi'$ and $\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}'$ imply
$\mathcal{M}(\Pi',\mathcal{V}')\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\mathcal{V})$. Fewer
constraints, more admissible models.

**The two extremes are informative.** With $\Pi$ all policies and $\mathcal{V}$ all
value functions, $\mathcal{M}$ contains only the true model — you are back to exact
modelling. With small $\Pi, \mathcal{V}$, the set is large and contains models far
simpler than the truth that nonetheless induce the same decisions.

**So the practical claim is a capacity argument**: with limited capacity, aim at the
value-equivalent set rather than at the true model, because members of that set can
be represented and the truth cannot. This is the formal statement of why MuZero's
latent is allowed to be unlike the world.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Self-predictive representations are the practical default.** Predicting your own
future latents — SPR, and the whole [JEPA](/architectures/jepa/) family — gives
dynamics-aware representations without a decoder, without negatives, and without
requiring reward. This converged from two directions: the RL representation
literature and the self-supervised vision literature arrived at the same objective.

**Bisimulation is the best theory and the weakest practice.** The value bound is
exactly the guarantee you want, and estimating Wasserstein distances between
learned transition distributions from samples is noisy enough that DBC-style
methods are finicky. Nobody has closed the gap between the theory's cleanliness and
the estimator's variance.

**Augmentation remains a suspiciously strong baseline.** DrQ's result has been
replicated and is still not fully absorbed — many representation-learning papers in
RL do not report an augmentation-only control, and they should.

**Object-centric representations are stuck at the natural-image boundary.** Slot
Attention and successors work well on synthetic scenes and degrade on real ones,
because "object" is not a well-posed notion outside curated data. This is the main
obstacle to the compositional generalisation the approach was meant to deliver.

**Successor features are having a quiet revival** in the context of transfer and
of reward-free pretraining, because the linear-reward assumption is much more
palatable when $\phi$ is a large pretrained feature vector.

## Open problems

- **Choosing the abstraction level automatically.** Nothing decides how much to
  discard. It is a design choice made by sweep, and the correct answer depends on a
  reward family you may not know in advance.

- **Reward-free representations that transfer.** Value-equivalence needs reward;
  self-prediction does not but gives no guarantee about decision-relevance. A
  representation that is both reward-free and provably sufficient for control is not
  available.

- **The binding problem.** Deciding which features belong to which object, in
  natural scenes, remains unsolved. Every object-centric method assumes a
  segmentation the world does not provide.

- **Estimating behavioural metrics from samples.** Bisimulation distance requires
  comparing distributions; the estimators are high-variance in exactly the
  high-dimensional settings where the metric would be most useful.

- **Non-stationarity.** All of this assumes a fixed environment. When dynamics
  change, a representation tuned to old dynamics is wrong, and there is no
  established way to detect that from the representation alone.

## Choosing one

<Card title="A decision procedure" icon="right-arrow">
**Low-dimensional, well-understood system?** Hand-engineered features. Genuinely.
Do not learn what you can write down.

**Partial observability with a known model?** Belief state — it is the correct
object. Approximate it if exact planning is intractable.

**Pixels, one task, reward available?** Value-equivalent or self-predictive, plus
augmentation. Run the augmentation-only baseline first.

**Pixels, many tasks, one environment?** Successor features, if rewards are
plausibly linear in your features. The transfer guarantee is real.

**Distractors and task-irrelevant visual variation?** Bisimulation-based, if you can
make the estimator behave.

**Reward-free data only?** Self-predictive / JEPA. It is the only family that trains
without reward and still learns dynamics.

**Need compositional generalisation over objects?** Object-centric — accepting that
it currently works on clean scenes.
</Card>
