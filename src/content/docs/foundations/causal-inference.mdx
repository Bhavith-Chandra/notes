<Abstract>
Machine learning answers "what is associated with what". Almost every question
people actually ask a model — did the treatment work, will this change help, what
would have happened otherwise — is causal, and **no amount of observational data
answers a causal question by itself.** You need assumptions the data cannot check.
The discipline of causal inference is about making those assumptions explicit,
knowing when they suffice, and only then estimating.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Identification comes before estimation, and it is not a statistical question.** Whether the effect is recoverable from your data depends on the causal structure you assume. Once identification holds, ML is excellent at the estimation. If it fails, no model and no sample size rescues you.
- **The fundamental problem: you never see both outcomes.** A person either received the treatment or did not. The other outcome is a counterfactual that was never observed, so every causal estimate is an imputation problem in disguise.
- **Controlling for more variables is not safer.** Conditioning on a *collider* — a common effect of treatment and outcome — creates a spurious association where none existed. "Adjust for everything you measured" is actively wrong advice.
- **Double machine learning is the bridge.** Use flexible ML for the nuisance functions, then a bias-corrected score with cross-fitting, and you recover $\sqrt{n}$-consistent effect estimates with valid confidence intervals despite slowly-converging ML components.

</KeyPoints>

## The question ML cannot answer

A model trained on hospital data finds that patients given a certain drug die more
often. Should you stop prescribing it?

Obviously not — not on that evidence. The drug is given to the sickest patients.
The correlation is real, the prediction is accurate, and the causal conclusion is
backwards.

**No amount of additional data fixes this**, because the data is *consistent* with
both stories: the drug kills people, or the drug is given to people who were
already dying. Distinguishing them requires knowing something about *how the data
came to be* — and that knowledge is not in the data.

## The ladder

Pearl's framing, and it is the most useful organising idea here.

<Steps>

1. **Association** — $P(y \mid x)$. What do I see together? Answered by any
   predictive model. *"Patients on this drug die more."*

2. **Intervention** — $P(y \mid do(x))$. What happens if I *act*? Requires a causal
   model or an experiment. *"If I give the drug, does survival change?"*

3. **Counterfactual** — $P(y_x \mid x', y')$. What *would have* happened? Requires
   a fully specified structural model. *"Would this specific patient, who took the
   drug and died, have survived without it?"*

</Steps>

**The rungs are strictly ordered.** You cannot answer a rung-2 question with only
rung-1 information, no matter how much of it you have or how good your model is.
This is a theorem, not a difficulty.

<Aside type="tip" title="What this means for machine learning specifically">
A large model trained on observational text has, in this framing, seen rung-1
information about the world plus rung-2 and rung-3 *claims* other people wrote
down. It can repeat causal knowledge; it did not derive it. That distinction
matters when you ask a model whether an intervention will work.
</Aside>

## Why more controls can hurt

The instinct "adjust for everything you measured" is common and wrong, and the
reason is worth seeing.

Suppose talent and beauty are independent in the population, and either one gets
you into acting. Now look only at *actors*. Among them, someone who is not
beautiful is probably talented — otherwise how did they get in? So within the
selected group, talent and beauty are **negatively** correlated.

You created an association by conditioning. The variable you conditioned on
(being an actor) is a **collider**: both talent and beauty point into it.

**So which variables to adjust for is a causal question, not a statistical one.**
You need the structure to know, and the structure is an assumption.

## The potential outcomes framework

The other main formalism, equivalent in expressive power and often easier to
compute with.

Each unit $i$ has two potential outcomes: $Y_i(1)$ if treated, $Y_i(0)$ if not. The
individual effect is $\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$.

**You observe exactly one of them.** $Y_i^{\text{obs}} = T_i Y_i(1) + (1-T_i)
Y_i(0)$. The other is a counterfactual, and it is missing not at random — it is
missing *because* of the treatment assignment.

This is why Holland called it the **fundamental problem of causal inference**:
individual effects are not identified, ever. Only averages are, and only under
assumptions.

## The assumptions you cannot test

<Steps>

1. **Consistency.** $Y_i^{\text{obs}} = Y_i(T_i)$ — the observed outcome is the
   potential outcome for the treatment received. This requires the treatment to be
   well-defined; "obesity" fails, because losing weight by exercise and by illness
   are different treatments.

2. **Ignorability / no unmeasured confounding.** $\big(Y(0), Y(1)\big) \perp T \mid
   X$. Given the covariates, assignment is as good as random. **This is the big
   one, and it is untestable** — by definition you cannot check for a confounder
   you did not measure.

3. **Positivity / overlap.** $0 < P(T=1\mid X=x) < 1$ for all $x$ with support. If
   nobody with a certain covariate profile ever gets treated, you have no evidence
   about them and estimation there is extrapolation.

4. **SUTVA.** One unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome. Violated by
   vaccines (herd immunity), by network effects, by market prices.

</Steps>

<Aside type="danger" title="Positivity fails silently and is checkable">
Unlike ignorability, positivity leaves a trace: plot the estimated propensity
scores by treatment group. If they barely overlap, your estimator is extrapolating
into regions with no data, and a flexible ML model will do so confidently and
wrongly. **Always plot this.** It is the cheapest diagnostic in the field and it is
routinely skipped.
</Aside>

## When randomisation is unavailable

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Adjustment">

Condition on enough covariates to close all backdoor paths, then compare treated
and untreated within strata:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[Y \mid do(T{=}1)\big] = \sum_{x} \mathbb{E}\big[Y \mid T{=}1, X{=}x\big]P(X{=}x).
$$

**Requires**: measuring all confounders. The strongest assumption and the most
common one.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Instrumental variables">

Find a variable $Z$ that affects $T$, affects $Y$ *only* through $T$, and is
unconfounded with $Y$. Then variation in $Z$ acts like randomisation.

The canonical example is randomised *encouragement*: you cannot force people to
take a drug, but you can randomly assign who gets a reminder.

**Requires**: the exclusion restriction — $Z$ has no direct path to $Y$ — which is
untestable and frequently implausible. Also, IV estimates a **local** effect: the
LATE, on the subpopulation whose behaviour the instrument actually changed. That
subgroup is not identified, so you do not know whose effect you measured.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Difference-in-differences">

Compare the change over time in a treated group with the change in an untreated
group. Any time-invariant confounding differences out.

**Requires**: parallel trends — absent treatment, both groups would have moved
together. Untestable for the treatment period, though pre-treatment trends are
suggestive evidence.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Regression discontinuity">

When treatment is assigned by a threshold on a running variable — a test score, an
income cutoff — compare units just above and just below.

**Requires**: no precise manipulation of the running variable near the cutoff.
Checkable by looking for bunching in the density.

**The most credible design here**, because near the threshold assignment really is
close to arbitrary, and it estimates only a local effect at the cutoff.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## The do-operator and identification

$P(y \mid do(x))$ is defined by *graph surgery*: delete every edge into $X$, set
$X = x$, and compute in the mutilated graph. This is not the same as conditioning:

$$
P(y \mid do(x)) \neq P(y \mid x) \quad\text{in general}.
$$

Conditioning selects a subpopulation; intervening changes the data-generating
process. They coincide only when nothing causes $X$ — which randomisation
arranges by construction.

**The backdoor criterion.** A set $Z$ is admissible for $(X, Y)$ if

1. no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and
2. $Z$ blocks every path from $X$ to $Y$ that begins with an arrow *into* $X$.

Then

$$
P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_{z} P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z).
$$

Condition 1 excludes mediators and colliders on causal paths; condition 2 closes
the confounding routes. Note it says *which* variables — this is the formal content
of "adjusting for everything is wrong".

**The front-door criterion** handles a case adjustment cannot: identification
through a fully-mediating variable even with an *unmeasured* confounder between $X$
and $Y$:

$$
P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_{m}P(m\mid x)\sum_{x'}P(y\mid m, x')P(x').
$$

That an effect can be identified despite unmeasured confounding was genuinely
surprising, and it is the clearest demonstration that identification is a
structural question rather than a data-quantity question.

## The ATE and its estimators

$$
\tau_{\text{ATE}} = \mathbb{E}\big[Y(1) - Y(0)\big].
$$

Under ignorability and positivity, with $\mu_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T{=}t,
X{=}x]$ and propensity $e(x) = P(T{=}1\mid X{=}x)$:

**Outcome regression (G-computation).**

$$
\hat\tau = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\Big[\hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i)\Big].
$$

Consistent if $\hat\mu$ is correct. Biased if it is misspecified.

**Inverse propensity weighting.**

$$
\hat\tau = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\left[\frac{T_i Y_i}{\hat e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)Y_i}{1 - \hat e(X_i)}\right].
$$

Consistent if $\hat e$ is correct. **High variance when propensities approach 0 or
1** — a single unit with $\hat e = 0.01$ receives weight 100 and dominates the
estimate. This is the positivity problem showing up numerically.

**Augmented IPW (doubly robust).**

$$
\hat\tau_{\text{AIPW}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\left[\hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i) + \frac{T_i\big(Y_i - \hat\mu_1(X_i)\big)}{\hat e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)\big(Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i)\big)}{1 - \hat e(X_i)}\right].
$$

**Doubly robust**: consistent if *either* $\hat\mu$ or $\hat e$ is correct, not
necessarily both. The regression provides the estimate; the weighted residual term
corrects it. If $\hat\mu$ is right the residuals are mean-zero and the correction
vanishes; if $\hat e$ is right the weighting fixes whatever bias $\hat\mu$ left.

## Double machine learning

The result that makes flexible ML usable for causal estimation.

**The problem it solves.** Plugging an ML estimate of $\mu$ into the outcome
regression introduces **regularisation bias** — ML estimators are biased by design,
trading bias for variance — and that bias does not vanish at $\sqrt{n}$, so
confidence intervals are invalid.

**The two ingredients.**

**Neyman orthogonality.** Construct a score $\psi$ whose expectation is
insensitive to first-order errors in the nuisance functions $\eta = (\mu, e)$:

$$
\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\psi\big(W;\ \tau,\ \eta_0 + r(\eta - \eta_0)\big)\Big]\right|_{r=0} = 0 .
$$

The AIPW score has this property. Nuisance error then enters the estimate only at
*second* order, so a nuisance rate of $n^{-1/4}$ — achievable by many ML methods —
gives a product term of $n^{-1/2}$, which is exactly the rate that vanishes
relative to the estimator's own $\sqrt{n}$ convergence.

**Cross-fitting.** Split the sample into $K$ folds; estimate nuisances on $K-1$
folds and evaluate the score on the held-out fold. This removes **overfitting
bias** — using the same data to fit $\hat\mu$ and to evaluate residuals against it
creates a correlation that biases the estimate, and it is the reason naive
plug-in with a flexible model fails even when the model is good.

Together:

$$
\sqrt{n}\big(\hat\tau_{\text{DML}} - \tau_0\big) \ \xrightarrow{d}\ \mathcal{N}\big(0,\ \sigma^2\big),
$$

so you get valid confidence intervals while using gradient boosting, random forests
or neural networks for the nuisances. **This is the technical bridge between ML and
causal inference**, and it is why "use ML for causal effects" became respectable.

## Heterogeneous effects

Often the average is not the question. The **conditional average treatment effect**
is

$$
\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}\big[Y(1) - Y(0)\ \big|\ X = x\big].
$$

**Meta-learners** reduce CATE estimation to standard supervised problems:

- **S-learner.** One model with treatment as a feature: $\hat\tau(x) = \hat\mu(x,1)
  - \hat\mu(x,0)$. Simple, and regularisation can shrink the treatment
  coefficient toward zero — biasing the effect toward *no effect*, which is the
  worst direction to be biased.
- **T-learner.** Separate models per arm. Avoids that shrinkage, and cannot share
  structure, so it is inefficient when the arms are similar and one is small.
- **X-learner.** Impute each unit's counterfactual using the *other* arm's model,
  then regress the imputed effects, then combine weighted by the propensity.
  Substantially better when treatment groups are very unbalanced.

**Causal forests** adapt random forests by splitting to maximise *heterogeneity in
the treatment effect* rather than in the outcome, and use honest estimation — one
subsample chooses the splits, a disjoint one estimates the effects in each leaf.
Honesty is what buys asymptotic normality and therefore valid pointwise confidence
intervals, which ordinary random forests do not have.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**DML and causal forests are standard practice** in tech-company experimentation
and increasingly in economics. The methodology is settled; the bottleneck has moved
to identification, which is where it belongs.

**Causal representation learning is ambitious and immature.** The goal — recovering
causal variables from raw observations rather than assuming them given — connects
directly to [representations](/foundations/representations/) and to
[world models](/world-models/). Identifiability results exist under strong
assumptions (interventions available, sparse mechanisms) and general recovery from
purely observational data is provably impossible.

**LLMs and causality is a live and confused question.** Models reproduce causal
claims from their training data well. Whether they *reason* causally, or retrieve
memorised causal statements, is contested — and existing benchmarks largely cannot
distinguish those, since the answers are in the training corpus.

**The assumption-checking culture is improving.** Sensitivity analysis — how strong
would an unmeasured confounder need to be to overturn this conclusion? — is
increasingly expected rather than optional, which is the right response to
untestable assumptions.

## Open problems

- **Unmeasured confounding is undetectable in principle.** Sensitivity analysis
  bounds the damage; nothing rules it out. This is a hard limit, not a gap.

- **Causal discovery from observational data alone.** Structure is identifiable only
  up to a Markov equivalence class without interventions or strong parametric
  assumptions. Many published discovery results quietly rely on those assumptions.

- **Interference and networks.** SUTVA fails whenever units interact — social
  networks, marketplaces, epidemics — which is most interesting settings. Methods
  exist and are far less mature.

- **Continuous and high-dimensional treatments.** Most theory assumes binary
  treatment. Dose-response and multi-dimensional interventions are much harder.

- **Transportability.** An effect estimated in one population need not hold in
  another. Formal transport conditions exist and require knowing how the
  populations differ structurally.
