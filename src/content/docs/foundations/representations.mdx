<Abstract>
"The network learns a representation" is the most-used and least-examined phrase
in machine learning. This page takes it seriously: what is actually being
claimed, how you would check it, and why the two obvious ways of checking — read
the neurons, train a linear probe — are both misleading. The honest answer is
that we can demonstrate representations are useful far more easily than we can
say what they contain.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **A representation is a re-coordinatisation, not a compression.** The point is not that the vector is smaller; it is that the geometry of the new space aligns with the structure of the problem, so that distance means similarity and simple decision boundaries suffice.
- **Neurons are the wrong unit of analysis.** Networks represent more features than they have dimensions, by storing them in overlapping non-orthogonal directions. This is *superposition*, and it explains why individual neurons look polysemantic.
- **Linear probes measure the probe as much as the representation.** High probe accuracy proves information is linearly accessible. Low accuracy proves nothing at all about whether the information is there.
- **Different models converge on similar representations.** Larger, better models trained on different data and even different modalities appear to align increasingly well — a striking empirical regularity with no settled explanation.

</KeyPoints>

## Coordinates that make the problem easy

Take a map of a city drawn with north up. Now redraw it with the main road as the
horizontal axis. Nothing about the city changed; every building sits where it did.
But questions like "is this address on the near side of the road?" went from
requiring geometry to requiring a glance at one number.

**That is what a representation is.** A re-description of the same data in
coordinates chosen so that the questions you care about become easy.

Notice what the definition does *not* say. It says nothing about being smaller.
You can represent something in more dimensions than you started with and it can
still be a better representation — kernel methods do exactly this. The goal is
alignment between the geometry and the task, not compression.

## The classic demonstration

Train a model to predict nearby words, and the resulting word vectors have a
property nobody built in: `king − man + woman` lands near `queen`. Directions in
the space correspond to *concepts* — one direction is roughly gender, another
roughly plurality, another roughly tense.

Nothing in the training objective mentioned gender. The structure fell out of the
requirement to predict context, because words that behave alike in sentences end
up nearby, and systematic differences between groups of words end up as systematic
displacements.

<Aside type="caution" title="This example is real and heavily oversold">
The analogy arithmetic works far less reliably than the famous examples suggest.
Later analyses showed a large share of the effect depends on excluding the three
query words from the nearest-neighbour search — without that exclusion the answer
is often just `king` again. Treat it as an illustration that structure *can*
emerge, not as evidence about how cleanly it does.
</Aside>

## Why "what does neuron 4,217 mean?" is the wrong question

The tempting picture is one neuron per concept: a cat neuron, a curve neuron, a
sarcasm neuron. Interpretability work found some of these, which made the picture
more tempting.

It is mostly wrong, for a reason that is simple once stated. A language model has
maybe 4,000 dimensions in its residual stream and needs to represent vastly more
than 4,000 concepts. It cannot give each one a dimension. What it does instead is
give each concept a *direction*, and pack in far more directions than dimensions
by allowing them to be slightly non-orthogonal.

This works because the features are **sparse** — of the millions of things a model
could be tracking, only a handful are present in any given input, so the
interference between overlapping directions rarely matters.

The cost is that a single neuron participates in many features and looks
incoherent when you inspect it. **Neurons look polysemantic because they are the
wrong basis**, not because the model is confused.

## Three things "representation" can mean

Distinguishing these prevents most of the confused arguments in this area.

| Sense | Claim | How you'd check |
|---|---|---|
| **Decodable** | the information is recoverable from the activations | train a probe |
| **Used** | the model's output actually depends on it | ablate or patch it, watch behaviour |
| **Structured** | it lives in a geometrically meaningful form (a direction, a subspace) | steer along it, check for linear separability |

These come apart constantly. A model can *decodably* contain the author's
nationality while never *using* it. A feature can be used without being
structured as a single direction. Papers frequently establish the first and claim
the third.

## Probing, and how to read it

Freeze the network, take activations from some layer, train a small classifier for
some property, report accuracy.

<Steps>

1. **Pick a layer** and extract activations for a labelled dataset.
2. **Train a probe** — usually linear, sometimes an MLP.
3. **Report** held-out accuracy against a baseline.

</Steps>

Two asymmetric conclusions, and getting them the wrong way round is the standard
error:

- **High accuracy ⟹ the information is linearly accessible.** Solid conclusion.
- **Low accuracy ⟹ ???** Could be that the information is absent. Could be that it
  is encoded non-linearly. Could be that your probe is under-trained or your
  dataset is small. **Low probe accuracy is nearly uninformative.**

<Aside type="danger" title="The capacity confound">
A sufficiently expressive probe can extract almost anything from almost any
representation — including from random features, which are a surprisingly strong
baseline. If your probe is an MLP, you may be measuring the probe's ability to
learn the task rather than the representation's contribution. Always report the
random-features baseline; a result that doesn't beat it says nothing.
</Aside>

## Superposition, concretely

The idea in one sentence: **a network with $n$ dimensions can represent $m \gg n$
features by assigning each a direction and accepting small interference.**

Elhage et al.'s toy models made the conditions precise. Superposition appears when:

- features are **sparse** (rarely co-occur), and
- the model has **more features to represent than dimensions**, and
- the loss rewards representing more features imperfectly over fewer perfectly.

Under those conditions the model organises features into geometric arrangements —
antipodal pairs, triangles, pentagons — that minimise interference for the given
count. These are visible in the toy models and are not an artefact.

The practical consequence is that recovering interpretable features means finding
an **overcomplete, sparse basis** rather than reading the neuron basis. That is
what sparse autoencoders do: train a wide autoencoder with an $\ell_1$ penalty on
the hidden layer, so it learns many more features than the layer has dimensions,
each active rarely. The recovered features are far more monosemantic than neurons.

## Definition

A representation is a map $\Phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Z}$. It is *good* for a
task family $\mathcal{G}$ if some simple $g \in \mathcal{G}$ composed with $\Phi$
achieves low risk:

$$
R(\Phi;\mathcal{G}) = \min_{g \in \mathcal{G}}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[\ell\big(g(\Phi(x)), y\big)\big].
$$

The quantifier structure carries the content. Quality is defined **relative to a
hypothesis class** $\mathcal{G}$. Take $\mathcal{G}$ to be all measurable
functions and the identity map is optimal, so the definition becomes vacuous.
Restrict $\mathcal{G}$ to linear maps and you recover linear probing, with all its
limitations. **There is no representation quality independent of what you plan to
do with it**, and this is the formal root of the evaluation problem.

## The information bottleneck, and the argument about it

Tishby's framing: find $Z$ minimising

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}} = I(Z; X) - \beta\, I(Z; Y),
$$

compressing $X$ while preserving what predicts $Y$. The endpoints are instructive:
$\beta \to 0$ gives $Z$ constant (all compression), $\beta \to \infty$ gives $Z =
X$ (no compression).

Tishby & Zaslavsky (2015) proposed that deep networks traverse an *information
plane* in two phases — a fitting phase where $I(Z;Y)$ rises, then a longer
compression phase where $I(Z;X)$ falls — and argued the compression phase explains
generalisation.

**Saxe et al. (2018) contested this substantially**, and the criticism is worth
knowing because it is a good case study in measurement artefacts:

- The observed compression depended on using saturating nonlinearities.
  ReLU networks often showed no compression phase while generalising fine.
- $I(Z;X)$ is *infinite* for deterministic networks with continuous inputs. The
  measured quantity depended entirely on the binning scheme used to estimate it,
  and the "compression" partly tracked activations spreading across bins.
- Compression and generalisation could be decoupled experimentally.

<Aside type="note" title="How to hold the IB">
As a *framing* — a representation trades off retaining input information against
retaining task-relevant information — it is genuinely clarifying and organises the
whole encoder literature. As an *empirical theory of why deep learning
generalises*, it did not survive scrutiny. Both statements are true at once, and
papers citing Tishby often blur them.
</Aside>

## Probing, formally

$$
\text{acc}_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi, y) = \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}}\ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{1}\{g(\Phi(x)) = y\}\big].
$$

Two properties, in tension:

**Monotone in $\mathcal{G}$.** If $\mathcal{G}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{G}_2$ then
$\text{acc}_{\mathcal{G}_1} \leq \text{acc}_{\mathcal{G}_2}$. Bigger probes always
score at least as high — so probe accuracy alone cannot be compared across papers
using different probes.

**Not monotone in information.** $\Phi_1$ can contain strictly more information
than $\Phi_2$ about $y$ while probing worse, if $\Phi_2$ has it in a linearly
accessible form. Probing measures *accessibility*, and accessibility is not
information.

The proposed fixes each have costs. **Control tasks** (Hewitt & Liang) probe for a
random relabelling and report *selectivity* — the gap between real-task and
control accuracy — which corrects for probe capacity. **Minimum description
length** probing scores how *quickly* the probe learns rather than its final
accuracy, which penalises probes that memorise. Both are better than raw accuracy
and neither is standard.

## Superposition, formally

Model a layer as recovering $m$ sparse features from $n < m$ dimensions. With
feature vector $f \in \mathbb{R}^m$ ($k$-sparse) and dictionary $W \in
\mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$:

$$
z = W f, \qquad \hat f = \operatorname{ReLU}(W^{\top} z + b).
$$

Since $m > n$, $W$ has a nontrivial null space and exact recovery is impossible in
general. But under sparsity, compressed-sensing results say recovery is possible
when the dictionary has small **coherence**

$$
\mu(W) = \max_{i \neq j} \frac{|\langle w_i, w_j\rangle|}{\|w_i\|\|w_j\|},
$$

roughly requiring $k \lesssim 1/\mu$. The optimal geometry therefore minimises
maximum pairwise coherence — the Welch bound problem — which is why toy models
settle into regular polytopes.

Interference between features $i$ and $j$ is $\langle w_i, w_j\rangle$. The
network tolerates it when the features rarely co-occur, so the expected cost
$\Pr[f_i \neq 0 \wedge f_j \neq 0]\cdot|\langle w_i, w_j\rangle|$ stays small.
**Sparsity is what buys the capacity**, and the sparser the features, the more can
be packed.

Sparse autoencoders invert this:

$$
\min_{W_e, W_d, b}\ \big\|z - W_d\, h\big\|_2^2 + \lambda \|h\|_1, \qquad h = \operatorname{ReLU}(W_e z + b),
$$

with $\dim(h) \gg \dim(z)$. The $\ell_1$ term enforces sparsity; the
overcompleteness allows one direction per feature rather than per dimension.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Sparse autoencoders are the main interpretability tool and they have known
problems.** They recover far more monosemantic features than the neuron basis, and
they also suffer *feature splitting* — increase the dictionary size and one
feature resolves into several finer ones, with no principled stopping point. The
number of "true" features is not defined by the method, and reconstruction error
versus sparsity is a trade-off curve rather than an optimum.

**The linear representation hypothesis holds better than expected but not
universally.** Many concepts are single directions, and steering along them works.
But circular and multi-dimensional representations have been found — days of the
week arranged on a circle, for instance — so "features are directions" is a good
default rather than a law.

**Representational convergence is a live and surprising result.** Huh et al.'s
Platonic Representation Hypothesis observes that as models get larger and more
capable, their representation spaces align increasingly well *across architectures
and even across modalities*. The proposed explanation — that all are approximating
the same underlying statistical structure of reality — is speculative. The
measurements are real and worth watching.

## Open problems

- **No task-agnostic quality measure, and the formalism says there cannot be one.**
  Quality is defined relative to $\mathcal{G}$. This is not a gap waiting to be
  filled; it is a property of the definition, and the field mostly proceeds as if
  it weren't.

- **Feature splitting has no principled resolution.** SAE dictionary size is a
  free parameter that changes what counts as a feature.

- **Decodable ≠ used.** Probing tells you information is present. Establishing the
  model *uses* it requires causal intervention, which is more work and much rarer
  in published results.

- **Cross-model comparison is unsolved.** Two models trained identically from
  different seeds produce incomparable spaces. Methods exist (CKA, SVCCA,
  Procrustes) and they disagree with each other, which is not reassuring.

- **We cannot predict what a training setup will represent.** Given an
  architecture, data, and objective, nothing forecasts which features emerge. Every
  finding here is post-hoc.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Elhage et al. (2022), Toy Models of Superposition** — the most important
   conceptual contribution here in a decade. Read the whole thing; it is
   well-written and the interactive figures carry the argument.
2. **Alain & Bengio (2016)** — short, and it inoculates you against over-reading
   probes.
3. **Saxe et al. (2018)** — read alongside Tishby & Zaslavsky, in that order. A
   model example of how a compelling theory meets careful measurement.
4. **Huh et al. (2024), Platonic Representation Hypothesis** — for the convergence
   result, held at appropriate arm's length.
