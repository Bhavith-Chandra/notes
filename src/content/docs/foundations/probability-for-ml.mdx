<Abstract>
ML papers assume a narrow, specific slice of probability theory — not a course,
about six ideas. This page covers exactly those: why almost every objective is an
expectation, what KL divergence measures and why its asymmetry is a design
choice, how to differentiate through a sampling step, and how variational bounds
turn intractable quantities into optimisable ones. If you can read an ELBO
derivation comfortably, skip this page.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Nearly every ML objective is an expectation you cannot compute.** You have samples, not distributions, so every loss you actually optimise is a Monte Carlo estimate — and the *variance* of that estimate often matters more than its form.
- **KL divergence is not a distance, and its asymmetry is load-bearing.** Forward KL makes your approximation cover everything; reverse KL makes it commit to one mode. Which one you use determines whether your model blurs or hallucinates.
- **The reparameterisation trick is the reason VAEs are trainable.** Not the ELBO — the ELBO was known. Moving randomness onto a parameter-free variable cuts gradient variance by orders of magnitude.
- **A variational bound converts "I can't compute this" into "I can optimise a lower bound".** The gap is itself a KL divergence, which tells you exactly when the bound is tight.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## Everything is an average you can't take

Almost every loss in machine learning has this shape:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ell(x; \theta)\big].
$$

"The average loss over all possible data." You cannot compute it — you do not have
all possible data, and you do not know $p_{\text{data}}$. You have a finite
sample, so you compute

$$
\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell(x_i; \theta),
$$

and hope it is close. That hope is what statistical learning theory formalises,
and it is the reason minibatch sizes, variance, and estimator design keep coming
up.

## Comparing two distributions

You have a true distribution and an approximation. How wrong is the
approximation?

The standard answer is **KL divergence**: on average, how surprised are you when
you predict with $q$ and reality follows $p$? It is zero when they match and grows
as they diverge.

It is **not symmetric**: $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$. That looks
like a defect and is actually the most useful thing about it, because the two
directions produce visibly different failure modes.

<Aside type="tip" title="How to remember which is which">
Look at where the expectation is taken. $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log
p/q]$ averages under $p$, so it is punished wherever $p$ has mass and $q$ doesn't
— $q$ must cover $p$. $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$ averages under $q$, so it is punished
wherever $q$ has mass and $p$ doesn't — $q$ must stay inside $p$. **The
distribution in the first slot is the one you must not miss.**
</Aside>

## Differentiating through randomness

Here is a problem that stops the obvious approach dead.

You want to train a network that *samples* — output a distribution, draw from it,
do something with the sample. To train it you need the gradient of the loss with
respect to the distribution's parameters. But "draw a random number" has no
derivative. The chain rule hits the sampling step and stops.

The fix is a change of variable. Instead of *"sample $z$ from a Gaussian with mean
$\mu$ and std $\sigma$"*, write *"sample $\epsilon$ from a standard normal, then
compute $z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon$"*.

Same distribution. But now the randomness sits in $\epsilon$, which has no
parameters, and $z$ is an ordinary differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$.
Gradients flow straight through.

That is the **reparameterisation trick**, and it is the technical reason VAEs
work.

## Expectation and the two estimators

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \quad x_i \sim p .
$$

Unbiased, with variance $\operatorname{Var}[f]/N$. The $1/\sqrt{N}$ convergence is
why enormous batches give diminishing returns.

When you need $\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)]$ — the parameters are in
the *distribution*, not the function — there are exactly two standard routes.

**Score function (REINFORCE).** Using $\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta$:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[f(x)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\big].
$$

Works for any $p_\theta$ you can sample and score, including discrete ones. Its
variance is high, because $f(x)$ multiplies the score without any information
about how $f$ changes — which is why practical RL spends so much effort on
baselines and control variates.

**Pathwise (reparameterisation).** If $x = g_\theta(\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon \sim
p(\epsilon)$ parameter-free:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\big[\nabla_\theta f\big(g_\theta(\epsilon)\big)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\big[f'\!\big(g_\theta(\epsilon)\big)\,\nabla_\theta g_\theta(\epsilon)\big].
$$

Much lower variance, because the gradient of $f$ carries local information about
the direction of improvement. Requires $x$ to be a differentiable function of
$\theta$ — so it does not apply to discrete variables without a relaxation like
Gumbel-Softmax.

<Aside type="note" title="This is the real content of the reparameterisation trick">
Both estimators are unbiased. The trick is not about correctness — it is about
*variance*. In practice the pathwise estimator has variance orders of magnitude
lower, and that gap is the difference between a VAE that trains and one that
doesn't.
</Aside>

## KL divergence

$$
\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] = \int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx .
$$

Properties worth having memorised:

- $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \geq 0$, with equality iff $p = q$ almost everywhere. (Jensen
  applied to $-\log$, which is convex.)
- Not symmetric, and does not satisfy the triangle inequality — it is a
  divergence, not a metric.
- $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \infty$ if $q(x) = 0$ where $p(x) > 0$. Absolute continuity
  is required, and violating it is a common source of `NaN`.

**Closed form for diagonal Gaussians against a standard normal** — the one you
will actually type:

$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(1 + \log\sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\Big).
$$

Note it is zero exactly when $\mu = 0, \sigma = 1$ — which is why an encoder that
ignores its input scores perfectly on this term. That is posterior collapse, read
straight off the formula.

**Relation to cross-entropy.** $H(p, q) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$. Since $H(p)$
does not depend on your parameters, minimising cross-entropy *is* minimising
forward KL. Every classifier you have trained was doing variational inference in
this sense.

## Jensen's inequality and where bounds come from

For convex $\varphi$: $\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \leq \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]$; the
inequality reverses for concave $\varphi$. Since $\log$ is concave:

$$
\log \mathbb{E}[X] \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}[\log X].
$$

**This single line generates most variational bounds in ML.** Whenever you see a
log of an expectation you cannot compute, pushing the log inside gives a
tractable lower bound.

## Deriving the ELBO

The canonical application. You want $\log p_\psi(x)$ but it requires marginalising
a latent:

$$
\log p_\psi(x) = \log \int p_\psi(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz .
$$

Introduce any distribution $q_\theta(z \mid x)$ with matching support, multiply and
divide:

$$
= \log\, \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\psi(x \mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\right]
\;\overset{\text{Jensen}}{\geq}\; \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\psi(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\right],
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\theta}\big[\log p_\psi(x\mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularisation}}.
$$

**How tight is it?** Exactly:

$$
\log p_\psi(x) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}} = \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\psi(z\mid x)\big).
$$

The gap is the KL between your approximate posterior and the true one. So the
bound is tight precisely when your encoder recovers the true posterior — which
tells you *why* a more expressive encoder helps, and gives you a target to
improve.

<Steps>

1. Write the intractable quantity as a log of an expectation.
2. Introduce a tractable $q$ by multiplying and dividing.
3. Apply Jensen to push the log inside.
4. Identify the gap as a KL, so you know when the bound is tight.

</Steps>

This recipe recurs: it produces the ELBO, the InfoNCE bound, and the information
bottleneck's variational form.

## Mutual information

$$
I(X;Y) = \mathrm{KL}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\right] = H(X) - H(X\mid Y).
$$

Symmetric, non-negative, zero iff independent. And **hard to estimate** —
notoriously so in high dimensions, which is why the contrastive literature uses
bounds rather than estimates.

The InfoNCE bound, from the same recipe:

$$
I(X;Y) \;\geq\; \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} .
$$

Note the $\log N$ ceiling: with $N = 8192$, the bound cannot exceed about 9 nats
regardless of the true mutual information. **The bound is loose at any realistic
batch size**, so arguments that lean on it as though it were an estimate are
overclaiming. It explains why bigger batches help; it does not establish tightness.

## Two more identities worth knowing

**Law of total expectation.** $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}_Y\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid
Y]\big]$. Used constantly to split an expectation over a latent.

**Change of variables.** For an invertible, differentiable $f$ with $y = f(x)$:

$$
p_Y(y) = p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\left|\det \frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial y}\right| .
$$

This is the whole basis of normalising flows, and the reason they need
architectures with tractable Jacobian determinants.

## Practical notes that matter more than they look

**Numerical stability is not optional.**

- Always work in log-space. `log_softmax`, `logsumexp`, `log1p` exist because
  their naive forms overflow.
- Have the network output $\log \sigma^2$, never $\sigma$. This makes the
  positivity constraint automatic and keeps gradients well-scaled.
- Add $\epsilon$ inside square roots and logs, not outside.

**KL warmup.** Training a VAE with the full KL term from step zero usually
collapses the posterior — the KL is easy to minimise, reconstruction is not, so
the optimiser takes the easy win. Annealing the KL weight from 0 to 1 over the
first epochs, or "free bits" (allowing a fixed KL budget per dimension without
penalty), both fix it. This is a standard trick and rarely stated in papers.

**Sample count.** VAEs typically use a *single* sample per datapoint to estimate
the expectation in the ELBO. This works because the reparameterised estimator's
variance is low enough and minibatching averages over datapoints. It surprises
people; it is deliberate.

## What this page deliberately leaves out

Measure-theoretic foundations, most of the classical concentration inequalities,
Bayesian model selection, and MCMC. Not because they are unimportant, but because
you can read essentially any modern deep-learning paper without them, and this
page is scoped to what the papers assume rather than to what a probability course
covers.

If you want the full treatment, the standard references are Murphy's *Probabilistic
Machine Learning* for breadth and Bishop for the classical Bayesian material.
