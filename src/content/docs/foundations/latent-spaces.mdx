<Abstract>
A latent space is where a model keeps its understanding — the vectors between
encoder and decoder, or between input and output head. Almost every intuition
people import about it is wrong: it is not a nice isotropic cloud, it does not
have one meaning per dimension, its usable dimensionality is far below its
nominal one, and the pictures you have seen of it are mostly artefacts of the
plotting algorithm. This page builds the correct picture from nothing, and ends
at the results that are still being argued about.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **The space is not shaped how you imagine.** Learned embeddings occupy a narrow anisotropic cone, not a ball. Random pairs of BERT embeddings have cosine similarity around 0.6 — near-identical by the metric everyone uses — which makes raw cosine similarity close to meaningless without correction.
- **Directions carry meaning; dimensions usually do not.** A concept is a direction in the space, and it is almost never axis-aligned. Reading dimension 4,217 is a category error, and it is the single most common mistake people make about latent spaces.
- **Unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive bias — this is a theorem.** Locatello et al. proved it and then demonstrated it empirically across 12,000 models. Any paper claiming purely unsupervised disentanglement is either assuming something unstated or measuring badly.
- **Your t-SNE plot is not a picture of the space.** Cluster sizes, inter-cluster distances and empty space are all artefacts. It preserves local neighbourhoods and nothing else, and people routinely read global structure off it.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## Where the model keeps its understanding

Feed an image into a network. Somewhere in the middle, before it produces an
answer, it holds a list of numbers — say 768 of them. That list is what the model
has decided the image *is*, in its own terms.

That list is a point in a 768-dimensional space, and **the latent space is the set
of all such points** for all possible inputs.

Everything interesting follows from one question: **what is the shape of that
set?**

## Why shape is the right question

If the space were arranged sensibly, then:

- Similar inputs would sit close together, so distance would mean similarity.
- Moving in a straight line between two points would pass through sensible
  intermediates.
- A direction would correspond to a concept, so you could add "make it older" as a
  vector.

All three of these *do* happen, sometimes, in some spaces. **And all three fail in
ways that catch people out.** The rest of this page is about which is which.

## The three intuitions people arrive with, and their status

<Steps>

1. **"Each dimension means something."** — **Mostly false.** Concepts are directions,
   and directions are almost never aligned to the coordinate axes you happened to
   get. See [superposition](/foundations/representations/).

2. **"Distance means similarity."** — **Conditionally true.** It depends entirely on
   what trained the space. A contrastive encoder was explicitly optimised to make
   cosine distance meaningful. A VAE was not, and using cosine on VAE latents is a
   silent error.

3. **"I can see the space in a 2-D plot."** — **Largely false.** t-SNE and UMAP
   preserve local neighbourhoods and deliberately distort everything else. The
   distances between clusters in your plot carry no information.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="The one-line correction that fixes most confusion">
**Latent spaces are shaped by their training objective, not by the data and not by
the architecture.** Ask "what did the loss force this space to do?" and the answers
follow. Contrastive losses shape angles. Reconstruction losses shape reachability.
Classification losses collapse classes to points. Nothing shapes what you did not
ask for.
</Aside>

<Tier level="mechanics" />

## Property 1 — Anisotropy

The single most practically important fact, and the least known outside NLP.

Take BERT, embed a large sample of random tokens, and compute the average cosine
similarity between random pairs. In an isotropic space this would be near **0**. In
practice, for contextual embeddings from later layers, it is far higher — Ethayarajh
measured values approaching **0.99** in GPT-2's upper layers.

**Everything occupies a narrow cone.** A cosine similarity of 0.85 sounds like
strong similarity and may be *below average*.

**Why it happens.** Gao et al. traced it to the softmax output layer: tokens that
appear rarely have their embeddings pushed in a common direction by the negative
gradient from every step in which they do not appear. The result is a shared
dominant component that dwarfs the semantic variation.

**The fixes, in increasing order of effort:**

<Steps>

1. **Subtract the mean.** Removes the dominant common component. Free, and often the
   bulk of the improvement.
2. **All-but-the-top.** Remove the mean, then project out the top few principal
   components. Mu et al. showed this improves word-similarity benchmarks
   substantially.
3. **Whitening.** Transform so the covariance is identity — mean-centre and multiply
   by $\Sigma^{-1/2}$. Makes the space isotropic by construction.
4. **Train for it.** A contrastive objective's uniformity term does this during
   training rather than after.

</Steps>

<Aside type="danger" title="Check this before you trust a similarity score">
Embed 10,000 random items from your corpus, compute pairwise cosine similarities,
and plot the histogram. If it is centred well above zero, your raw similarities are
dominated by a common component and your retrieval or clustering is degraded.
**This costs five minutes and is routinely skipped.**
</Aside>

## Property 2 — Intrinsic dimension

Your embedding has 768 dimensions. It is not using 768 of them.

**Intrinsic dimension** is the number of degrees of freedom the data actually
varies along. Pope et al. estimated it for common image datasets and found values
in the range of **roughly 20 to 40** — for images with tens of thousands of pixels.

The consequence they demonstrated is the useful part: **learning difficulty tracks
intrinsic dimension, not ambient dimension.** Datasets with lower intrinsic
dimension need fewer samples to reach a given accuracy, and this holds across
architectures.

**How to measure it.** The maximum-likelihood estimator on nearest-neighbour
distances (Levina–Bickel), or the two-nearest-neighbour method, or simply the
number of principal components needed for 95% of variance — the last is crude,
because it only detects *linear* dimension and the manifold is curved.

## Property 3 — The manifold hypothesis

The claim: real data lies on (or near) a low-dimensional manifold embedded in the
high-dimensional ambient space.

**Why it must be roughly true.** Sample uniformly from all $256^{224\times224\times3}$
possible images and you will never in the lifetime of the universe produce anything
that looks like a photograph. Photographs occupy a vanishingly small, highly
structured subset.

**And it is testable.** Fefferman, Mitter & Narayanan gave an algorithm that decides,
from finite samples, whether data lies near a manifold of bounded dimension and
curvature — turning a widely-assumed hypothesis into a checkable one.

**Why it matters here.** If the data manifold is $d$-dimensional and your latent is
$k$-dimensional with $k > d$, the model has spare capacity and the extra dimensions
will encode *something* — noise, dataset artefacts, or nothing at all. If $k < d$
you are forced to discard real structure. Neither is catastrophic, and knowing which
regime you are in explains a lot of otherwise mysterious behaviour.

## Property 4 — Directions and arithmetic

The famous result: `king − man + woman ≈ queen`.

**It is real and heavily oversold.** Linzen showed that a large part of the reported
accuracy depends on **excluding the three query words** from the nearest-neighbour
search. Without that exclusion, the nearest vector to `king − man + woman` is
frequently just `king` again — the offset is small relative to the distance between
words, so you land back near where you started.

**What survives the critique.** Directions *do* carry meaning; the analogy task is
simply a poor way to demonstrate it. Better evidence:

- **Steering.** Add $\alpha v$ to activations and behaviour changes predictably in
  the direction $v$ encodes. This is causal, not correlational.
- **GANSpace.** Run PCA on a GAN's intermediate latents and the top components are
  interpretable controls — pose, lighting, age — with no supervision at all.
- **Linear probes.** If a concept is linearly decodable, a direction encodes it.

## Property 5 — Interpolation, and the sphere

Walk in a straight line between two latent points and decode along the way. In a
well-behaved space you get a smooth semantic transition.

**Whether it works depends on the objective.** A VAE's KL term pulls the aggregate
posterior toward the prior, filling the space — so the midpoint decodes to something
plausible. A plain autoencoder has holes, and the midpoint decodes to noise.

**The high-dimensional trap.** Linear interpolation between two samples from a
Gaussian passes through a region of **atypically small norm**. In $d$ dimensions a
standard Gaussian concentrates on a shell of radius $\sqrt{d}$, and the midpoint of
two random points on that shell has expected norm about $\sqrt{d/2}$ — well inside
the shell, in a region the model effectively never saw during training.

**The fix is spherical interpolation (slerp)**, which moves along the great circle
and preserves norm:

$$
\operatorname{slerp}(z_1,z_2;t) = \frac{\sin\big((1-t)\Omega\big)}{\sin\Omega}z_1 + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}z_2,\qquad \Omega = \arccos\!\Big(\tfrac{z_1\cdot z_2}{\|z_1\|\|z_2\|}\Big).
$$

If your interpolations look washed out in the middle, this is almost certainly why.

## Property 6 — Disentanglement, and the impossibility result

The dream: one latent dimension per generative factor. Dimension 1 is rotation,
dimension 2 is colour, and they vary independently.

$\beta$-VAE and relatives appeared to achieve this by up-weighting the KL term.

**Then Locatello et al. proved it cannot be done.** For any generative model
$p(x,z)$ with a factorised prior, there exists an invertible transformation $q$ such
that $z' = q(z)$ is **completely entangled** while inducing exactly the same
distribution over $x$. Since the observed data is identical, no unsupervised
objective can distinguish them.

Then they trained over 12,000 models and confirmed empirically:

- Disentanglement metrics correlate poorly with each other.
- Random seed affects the score more than the choice of method.
- Better disentanglement scores did not yield better downstream performance.

<Aside type="caution" title="What the theorem does and does not say">
It says **purely unsupervised** disentanglement is impossible without inductive
bias. It does *not* say disentanglement is impossible. With weak supervision,
paired observations, temporal structure, or access to interventions, identifiability
results *do* exist — iVAE proves identifiability given an auxiliary observed
variable that the latents depend on.

So the correct reading is: **the assumptions must be made explicit.** Any method
that appears to disentangle from raw observations alone is relying on an unstated
architectural bias, and the field's job is to name it.
</Aside>

## Property 7 — Comparing two spaces

Two models trained on the same data produce different latent spaces. Are they
representing the same thing?

Naive comparison fails immediately — the spaces have different dimensions, and any
rotation of a space is equivalent to it. You need a similarity measure invariant to
the things that do not matter and sensitive to the things that do.

| Method | Invariant to | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| **CCA** | invertible linear transforms | too permissive — finds correlation in noise directions |
| **SVCCA** | as CCA, after truncating to top singular vectors | truncation threshold is arbitrary |
| **CKA (linear)** | orthogonal transforms, isotropic scaling | **not** invariant to general invertible transforms |
| **CKA (RBF)** | as above, non-linear | kernel bandwidth is another knob |

**CKA is the standard**, and Kornblith et al.'s argument for it is precisely that
invariance to *all* invertible linear transforms is too strong: such transforms can
destroy the structure you were trying to measure, so a measure invariant to them
tells you nothing. Invariance to orthogonal transforms and isotropic scaling is the
right amount.

## Property 8 — Neural collapse

At the end of training a classifier — past the point where training error is zero,
in the terminal phase — something startling happens.

Papyan, Han & Donoho documented four properties emerging together:

<Steps>

1. **Within-class variability collapses.** All examples of a class map to
   essentially the same point — the class mean.
2. **Class means form a simplex equiangular tight frame.** Maximally separated,
   equal pairwise angles.
3. **Classifier weights align with class means** (self-duality).
4. **Classification becomes nearest-class-mean.**

</Steps>

**Why it matters for latent spaces.** A supervised classifier's penultimate layer,
trained to convergence, has thrown away nearly all within-class information. Two
different cats are the same point.

**This is the geometric reason to prefer self-supervised features for transfer.** A
supervised representation is optimised to be *maximally uninformative* about
everything except the label — which is exactly wrong for a downstream task that
cares about something else.

## Anisotropy, measured

Define the expected cosine similarity between random pairs of embeddings:

$$
\text{aniso}(f) = \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim p_{\text{data}},\ x\neq y}\left[\frac{f(x)^{\top}f(y)}{\|f(x)\|\,\|f(y)\|}\right].
$$

For an isotropic distribution centred at the origin this is 0.
Write $f(x) = \mu + \delta(x)$ where the residual has zero mean:

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[f(x)^{\top}f(y)\big] = \|\mu\|^2 + \mathbb{E}\big[\delta(x)\big]^{\top}\mathbb{E}\big[\delta(y)\big] = \|\mu\|^2 ,
$$

since $\delta(x)$ and $\delta(y)$ are independent and mean-zero. **The anisotropy is
driven by $\|\mu\|^2$** — the squared norm of the common component — relative to the
variance. Which is exactly why subtracting the mean is the first and largest fix.

**Whitening**, formally: with empirical mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma = U\Lambda
U^\top$,

$$
\tilde f(x) = \Lambda^{-1/2}U^{\top}\big(f(x) - \mu\big),
$$

giving $\mathbb{E}[\tilde f] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\tilde f) = I$. All
directions now have equal variance. **The cost, which is real**: whitening amplifies
low-variance directions, and those often carry noise, so it can hurt if the tail of
the spectrum is junk. Truncating to the top $k$ components before whitening is the
usual compromise.

## Intrinsic dimension estimators

**Levina–Bickel MLE.** For point $x_i$ with distances $T_k(x_i)$ to its $k$-th
nearest neighbour, model the number of points within radius $r$ as a Poisson process
on a $d$-manifold. The likelihood gives

$$
\hat d_k(x_i) = \left[\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\log \frac{T_k(x_i)}{T_j(x_i)}\right]^{-1},
$$

averaged over points. **Sensitive to $k$** — too small and noise dominates, too
large and curvature violates the local-flatness assumption.

**TwoNN.** Uses only the two nearest neighbours. With $\mu_i = r_2(x_i)/r_1(x_i)$,
the ratio follows a Pareto distribution with parameter $d$, so

$$
\hat d = \frac{n}{\sum_i \log \mu_i}.
$$

Fewer assumptions about scale, and the trade is higher variance.

**The key empirical result.** Pope et al. found generalisation error scales with
intrinsic rather than extrinsic dimension: for a fixed architecture, sample
complexity to reach a target accuracy grew with $\hat d$ and was essentially
independent of pixel count. **This is why models work at all on images** — the
apparent dimensionality is not the effective one.

## The disentanglement impossibility theorem

**Theorem (Locatello et al.).** Let $z \sim p(z)$ with $p(z) = \prod_i p(z_i)$
factorised. Then for $d > 1$ there exist infinitely many invertible maps
$q: \operatorname{supp}(z)\to\operatorname{supp}(z)$ such that

$$
\frac{\partial q_i(u)}{\partial u_j} \neq 0 \quad \forall i,j \qquad\text{(completely entangled)}
$$

while $q(z)$ has the **same marginal distribution** as $z$, i.e. $P(z \leq u) =
P(q(z)\leq u)$.

**The construction.** Map each coordinate through its own CDF to uniform, apply a
suitable measure-preserving mixing map on the unit cube, and map back. Since the
resulting $z' = q(z)$ has the same distribution, the models $(q, p)$ and
$(\text{id}, p)$ are **observationally identical** — they induce the same $p(x)$.

**The conclusion.** No objective that depends only on $p(x)$ can prefer the
disentangled solution. Disentanglement requires either supervision or an inductive
bias that breaks the symmetry, and the bias must be stated.

**iVAE's escape route.** Khemakhem et al. showed identifiability is recovered given
an auxiliary variable $u$ (time index, class label, environment id) with

$$
p(z\mid u) = \prod_i \frac{Q_i(z_i)}{Z_i(u)}\exp\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k} T_{i,j}(z_i)\,\lambda_{i,j}(u)\right],
$$

a conditionally factorised exponential family. Given sufficient variability in $u$,
the latents are identifiable up to permutation and componentwise transformation.
**The auxiliary variable is the inductive bias, made explicit** — which is exactly
what the impossibility theorem demands.

## Linear representations, stated carefully

The **linear representation hypothesis**: a concept $W$ is represented as a
direction $\bar\ell_W$ such that the model's behaviour depends on the projection
$\bar\ell_W^{\top} \gamma$ of the representation $\gamma$.

Park, Choe & Veitch's contribution is to distinguish two notions that get conflated:

- **Embedding representation.** A concept is a direction in the *input embedding*
  space — the space where you would add a steering vector.
- **Unembedding representation.** A concept is a direction in the *output* space —
  the space that determines logits.

They are related by the model's internal geometry, and *not* the same direction in
general. The reconciliation uses a **causal inner product**: define

$$
\langle \gamma_1, \gamma_2\rangle_{C} = \gamma_1^{\top}\,\operatorname{Cov}(\gamma)^{-1}\,\gamma_2 ,
$$

i.e. the Mahalanobis inner product under the unembedding covariance. Under this
inner product, causally separable concepts become orthogonal, and the embedding and
unembedding representations become **duals of each other**.

**The practical consequence.** Steering vectors should be applied under the whitened
geometry, not the raw Euclidean one — the correct notion of "orthogonal concepts"
depends on the covariance, and ignoring it is why naive steering sometimes produces
unintended side effects.

## Alignment and uniformity as a coordinate system

For $\ell_2$-normalised embeddings on $S^{d-1}$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}(f;\alpha) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})}\big[\|f(x)-f(x^{+})\|_2^{\alpha}\big],
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{unif}}(f;t) = \log\ \mathbb{E}_{x,y\ \text{iid}}\big[e^{-t\|f(x)-f(y)\|_2^{2}}\big].
$$

Wang & Isola prove the uniformity term is minimised by the distribution minimising
the **Gaussian potential energy** on the hypersphere — which, as $t\to\infty$,
converges weakly to the uniform distribution. So "uniformity" is not a metaphor; it
is a precise energy-minimisation statement.

**Why this is the right pair of coordinates for diagnosing a space:**

| Alignment | Uniformity | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| good | good | healthy |
| good | poor | collapsing — everything clumping together |
| poor | good | positives too weak, or augmentations too aggressive |
| poor | poor | not training |

The loss value alone cannot distinguish these four, and the pair costs almost
nothing to compute.

## Neural collapse, formally

Let $h_{i,c}$ be the penultimate-layer feature of example $i$ in class $c$, with
class means $\mu_c$ and global mean $\mu_G$. Define within- and between-class
scatter:

$$
\Sigma_W = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{c}\sum_{i}\big(h_{i,c}-\mu_c\big)\big(h_{i,c}-\mu_c\big)^{\top},
\qquad
\Sigma_B = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c}\big(\mu_c - \mu_G\big)\big(\mu_c-\mu_G\big)^{\top}.
$$

**NC1 (variability collapse).** $\Sigma_W \,\Sigma_B^{\dagger} \to 0$.

**NC2 (simplex ETF).** The centred class means become equinorm and equiangular:

$$
\frac{\big(\mu_c - \mu_G\big)^{\top}\big(\mu_{c'}-\mu_G\big)}{\|\mu_c-\mu_G\|\,\|\mu_{c'}-\mu_G\|} \longrightarrow \frac{C}{C-1}\delta_{cc'} - \frac{1}{C-1}.
$$

For $c\neq c'$ this is $-1/(C-1)$ — the maximally-separated configuration for $C$
points on a sphere, a simplex equiangular tight frame.

**NC3 (self-duality).** $W^\top \propto M$, the classifier weights aligning with the
class-mean matrix.

**NC4.** Classification reduces to nearest class mean.

**The implication for transfer, stated precisely.** NC1 says within-class
information is destroyed. A downstream task requiring a distinction *within* a
training class — breed of dog, when trained on "dog" — has had its signal removed
from the representation by convergence itself. Training longer makes this worse,
which is the counterintuitive part and the reason early-stopped or self-supervised
features often transfer better.

## Visualisation, and what it does not show

t-SNE minimises

$$
\mathrm{KL}\big(P\,\|\,Q\big) = \sum_{i\neq j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}},
$$

with $p$ from Gaussian neighbourhoods in high dimensions and $q$ from a
Student-$t$ in two dimensions.

**The asymmetry of forward KL is the whole story.** Recall from
[probability](/foundations/probability-for-ml/) that forward KL is heavily
penalised when $p_{ij}$ is large and $q_{ij}$ is small — that is, when nearby points
are placed far apart. It is barely penalised in the reverse case. So t-SNE
**aggressively preserves local neighbourhoods and freely distorts global
structure.**

Three concrete things your plot does not show:

- **Cluster sizes are meaningless.** The Student-$t$ heavy tail expands dense
  regions and contracts sparse ones. A large blob is not a diverse class.
- **Inter-cluster distances are meaningless.** Nothing in the objective constrains
  them. Two clusters at opposite ends may be adjacent in the real space.
- **Perplexity changes the answer.** Different values produce qualitatively
  different pictures from identical data.

UMAP preserves somewhat more global structure through its fuzzy-simplicial-set
construction, and the same warnings apply in weaker form.

**What to do instead.** Report quantitative structure: intrinsic dimension, the
singular value spectrum, alignment/uniformity, $k$-NN retrieval accuracy. Use the
2-D plot as an illustration of a claim you established another way, never as the
evidence.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Sparse autoencoders are the current best tool for reading a latent space.** By
learning an overcomplete, sparse basis they recover far more monosemantic
directions than the neuron basis — millions of interpretable features from
production models. **Feature splitting** remains unresolved: enlarge the dictionary
and features subdivide indefinitely, with no principled stopping criterion. See
[interpretability](/foundations/interpretability/).

**The linear representation hypothesis holds better than expected, with documented
exceptions.** Many concepts are single directions and steering works. Circular
representations — days of the week, months — have been found, so "features are
directions" is a strong default rather than a law. Park et al.'s causal inner
product is the current best account of *which* geometry makes the hypothesis
precise.

**Representational convergence is a live and genuinely surprising result.** The
Platonic Representation Hypothesis reports that as models get larger and more
capable, their representation spaces align increasingly well — across
architectures, training objectives, and even *modalities*. Vision and language
models trained separately end up with measurably similar structure. The proposed
explanation (all are approximating the same statistical structure of reality) is
speculative; the measurements are not, and they are worth watching.

**Anisotropy is understood and still bites people.** The mechanism is known, the
fixes are cheap, and a great deal of deployed retrieval runs on raw cosine
similarity over uncorrected embeddings.

## Open problems

- **No task-agnostic quality measure, and the formalism says there cannot be one.**
  Representation quality is defined relative to a downstream hypothesis class. This
  is a property of the definition, not a gap awaiting a solution.

- **Feature splitting.** SAE dictionary size is a free parameter that changes what
  counts as a feature. Nothing says how many features a model "has".

- **Cross-model comparison remains unsettled.** CKA, SVCCA and Procrustes methods
  disagree with one another, and there is no principled way to adjudicate.

- **Non-linear feature geometry is under-explored.** Circular and manifold-valued
  representations have been documented, and no systematic account exists of when
  concepts are directions versus something else.

- **Predicting what a training setup will represent.** Given an architecture, data
  and objective, nothing forecasts which features emerge. Every finding is post-hoc.

## A working checklist

<Card title="Diagnosing a latent space you did not train" icon="setting">
**1. Histogram random-pair cosine similarities.** Centred well above zero means
anisotropy is dominating your metric. Mean-centre, then consider whitening.

**2. Plot the singular value spectrum** of a batch of embeddings. A sharp cliff
means dimensional collapse; a slow decay is healthy.

**3. Estimate intrinsic dimension** (TwoNN is two lines of code). It tells you how
much capacity is actually in use.

**4. Measure alignment and uniformity** if you have positive pairs. The 2×2 table
above diagnoses four distinct failure modes the loss value cannot.

**5. Run $k$-NN retrieval** as your quality metric before running a linear probe —
it needs no training and makes no linearity assumption.

**6. Only then plot it**, and treat the picture as illustration rather than
evidence.
</Card>
