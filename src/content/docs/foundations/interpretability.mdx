<Abstract>
Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse-engineer what a network actually
computes — not which inputs correlate with which outputs, but the algorithm
implemented in the weights. It has produced genuine results: induction heads,
factual recall localised to specific MLP layers, millions of interpretable
features extracted from a production model. It has also run into a structural
problem — features do not live in neurons — that shapes everything the field
currently does.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Correlation is not mechanism.** A neuron firing on cats does not mean the model uses it to detect cats. Establishing that requires intervention — change the activation, watch the output — which is why causal methods dominate.
- **The neuron basis is the wrong basis.** Superposition means a model represents more features than it has dimensions, packed as overlapping directions. Individual neurons therefore look polysemantic, and reading them is a category error.
- **Sparse autoencoders find the right basis, at a price.** Train a wide, sparse autoencoder on activations and you recover far more monosemantic features. But dictionary size is a free parameter, and increasing it splits features indefinitely with no principled stopping point.
- **Induction heads are the field's best result.** A two-head circuit that finds a previous occurrence of the current token and predicts what followed it. Mechanistically characterised, causally verified, and correlated with the phase change where in-context learning appears.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## Two questions that get confused

**"What makes this model output X?"** is a question about behaviour. You can answer
it with correlations — find inputs that produce X, look for patterns.

**"How does this model compute X?"** is a question about mechanism. It asks what
algorithm the weights implement, and it cannot be answered by correlation at all.

Mechanistic interpretability is the second question. The distinction matters
because the first is much easier and is routinely reported as though it answered
the second.

## Why looking at neurons fails

The intuitive approach is to find inputs that make a neuron fire, and name the
neuron after them.

This runs into two problems, and the second is fatal.

**Correlation without causation.** A neuron might reliably fire on images of cats
while playing no role in the model's cat classification — it could be a byproduct
of something else that happens to co-occur. To know it matters you have to
intervene: clamp it and see whether behaviour changes.

**Polysemanticity.** Most neurons respond to several unrelated things — a neuron
that fires on cat faces, the front of cars, and legal citations. This looks like
confusion and is not. A model with 4,000 dimensions needs to represent far more
than 4,000 concepts, so it assigns each concept a *direction* and packs in more
directions than dimensions, accepting slight overlap.

This works because concepts are **sparse** — very few are active at once — so the
interference rarely bites. The cost is that any single neuron sits at the
intersection of many feature directions, and reading it shows a mixture.

<Aside type="tip" title="The reframing that organises the field">
Features are directions, not neurons. The neuron basis is arbitrary — an accident
of how the network was parameterised. Interpretability's central technical problem
is **finding the right basis**, and sparse autoencoders are the current best
attempt at doing so automatically.
</Aside>

## What a circuit is

A circuit is a subgraph of the network that implements an identifiable
computation — a small set of attention heads and MLP layers, and the specific way
information flows between them, that together perform some function.

The canonical example is the **induction head**, and it is worth stating precisely
because it is the field's cleanest result.

Given text like `... Ms Dursley ... Ms D`, the model should predict `ursley`. Two
heads, in two different layers, cooperate:

1. A **previous-token head** in an early layer writes, at each position,
   information about the token that came *before* it.
2. An **induction head** in a later layer looks for earlier positions whose
   "previous token" matches the current token, then copies whatever came *after*
   those positions.

That is a complete algorithm — match-and-copy — implemented in attention weights,
verified by intervention, and it appears in essentially every transformer above a
certain size.

<Steps>

1. **Clean run.** Prompt that produces the behaviour. Cache every activation.
2. **Corrupted run.** Minimally different prompt that changes the answer.
3. **Patched run.** Run corrupted, but overwrite one component with its cached
   clean value.
4. **Measure recovery.** How much of the clean behaviour returns.
5. **Sweep.** Repeat for every layer and position to localise the computation.

</Steps>

**The choice of corruption is a modelling decision.** Swapping a token, adding
noise to embeddings, or using a different prompt entirely all define different
counterfactuals, and they can give different answers. Zhang & Nanda showed that
metric and corruption choices materially change conclusions — this is a live
methodological problem, not a settled recipe.

## Sparse autoencoders

If features are directions and there are more of them than dimensions, you need an
**overcomplete** basis. Train a wide autoencoder on the model's activations with a
sparsity penalty:

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="The method">

Take activations $x$ from some layer. Train an encoder into a much wider space
(8× to 100× the layer width) with an $\ell_1$ penalty forcing few features active,
then a decoder reconstructing $x$.

The learned dictionary directions turn out to be far more monosemantic than
neurons. Scaling Monosemanticity extracted millions of features from a production
model — features for the Golden Gate Bridge, for code errors, for sycophantic
praise, for internal conflict — and demonstrated they are causal by *steering*:
clamp a feature high and the model's behaviour changes accordingly.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Feature splitting">

The unresolved problem. Increase the dictionary size and a feature resolves into
several finer ones: "bird" becomes "bird", then "seabird" and "raptor", then
specific species.

None of these is wrong. There is no principled number of features, and no
criterion telling you when to stop. Reconstruction error versus sparsity is a
trade-off curve, not an optimum — so "we found N features" is a statement about
your hyperparameter as much as about the model.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Open dictionaries">

Gemma Scope released SAEs trained on every layer of an open model, which changed
the field's accessibility — before it, this work required either training your own
dictionaries at significant cost or working inside a frontier lab.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Attribution graphs

The 2025 development. Rather than analysing one component, use the SAE features as
nodes and trace the computational graph *between* them for a specific prompt —
which features activated, which caused which, and how they combined to produce the
output.

This moved the field from "here is an interesting component" to "here is the
computation this model performed on this input", and it surfaced behaviours worth
knowing about: planning ahead in poetry (the model selects a rhyme before writing
the line leading to it), and cases where the stated chain-of-thought does not match
the computation actually performed.

<Aside type="caution" title="Motivated reasoning, caught mechanistically">
The circuit-tracing work found instances where a model produces a plausible
argument for an answer it had already arrived at by other means — the reasoning
shown to the user was not the reasoning that determined the output. This matters
directly for anyone treating chain-of-thought as an explanation. It is a report of
the process, and the report is not always accurate.
</Aside>

## The residual stream decomposition

Elhage et al.'s framework starts from writing a transformer's computation as a sum
of paths. The residual stream at layer $\ell$ is

$$
x_\ell = x_0 + \sum_{i<\ell}\ \sum_{h}\ \operatorname{head}_{i,h}(x_i) + \sum_{i<\ell}\operatorname{MLP}_i(x_i),
$$

so every component *adds* to a shared bus rather than transforming a pipeline.

For an attention-only model this becomes an explicit sum over paths from input to
output, each path a product of the head matrices along it. The decomposition
separates each head into two circuits:

$$
W_{QK} = W_Q^{\top} W_K \quad (\text{which positions attend where}), \qquad
W_{OV} = W_O W_V \quad (\text{what gets written when they do}).
$$

**These are independent.** $W_{QK}$ determines the attention pattern; $W_{OV}$
determines what information moves given that pattern. Analysing them separately is
what makes head-level interpretation tractable, because a head's "job" factors into
where it looks and what it copies.

## Induction heads, formally

A two-layer composition. Let the current token at position $t$ be $A$, and suppose
the sequence contains an earlier occurrence `[A][B]` at positions $j, j+1$.

**Layer 1 — previous-token head.** Attends from each position to the one before,
so position $j+1$ accumulates information about token $A$:

$$
x_{j+1} \mathrel{+}= W_{OV}^{(1)}\, e(A).
$$

**Layer 2 — induction head.** Its query at position $t$ is built from $e(A)$; its
key at position $j+1$ was built from the $A$-information written in layer 1. So the
QK score

$$
\big(W_Q^{(2)} e(A)\big)^{\top}\big(W_K^{(2)} W_{OV}^{(1)} e(A)\big)
$$

is large exactly at positions following a previous $A$. The head then copies via
$W_{OV}^{(2)}$ the token at that position — which is $B$.

This is **K-composition**: layer 2's keys are constructed from layer 1's output
rather than from the embedding directly. The algorithm is match-and-copy, it is
readable off the weights, and it is causally verified by ablation.

**Why it matters beyond itself.** Olsson et al. showed induction heads form during
a narrow window of training that coincides with a visible bump in the loss curve
and with the sudden appearance of in-context learning. That is a mechanistic
account of an emergent capability — currently the field's strongest such result.

## Superposition and the capacity bound

From the [representations page](/foundations/representations/): a layer with $n$
dimensions represents $m > n$ features as

$$
x = \sum_{i=1}^{m} f_i\, w_i, \qquad w_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n}, \ \|w_i\| = 1,
$$

with $f$ sparse. Interference between features $i$ and $j$ is $\langle w_i, w_j
\rangle$, and the expected damage is

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{interference}] = \Pr\big[f_i \neq 0 \wedge f_j \neq 0\big]\cdot\big|\langle w_i, w_j\rangle\big|.
$$

Sparsity makes the first factor tiny, so substantial overlap is affordable. The
optimal packing minimises maximum coherence

$$
\mu(W) = \max_{i\neq j}\ \big|\langle w_i, w_j\rangle\big|,
$$

subject to the Welch bound

$$
\mu \;\geq\; \sqrt{\frac{m-n}{n(m-1)}},
$$

which is why toy models settle into regular polytopes — those are the
configurations achieving it.

## The sparse autoencoder objective

$$
h = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(W_e(x - b_d) + b_e\big), \qquad \hat x = W_d h + b_d,
$$

$$
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\|x - \hat x\|_2^2}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \lambda \underbrace{\sum_{i} |h_i|\,\|W_{d,i}\|_2}_{\text{sparsity}},
$$

with $\dim(h) = R \cdot \dim(x)$ for expansion factor $R \in [8, 100]$.

Two details that are easy to skip and matter:

**The $\ell_1$ term is weighted by decoder column norms.** Without it, the model
shrinks $h$ and grows $W_d$ to cheat the penalty while leaving the reconstruction
unchanged. Either this weighting or an explicit unit-norm constraint on decoder
columns is required.

**Subtracting $b_d$ before encoding** centres the input, which measurably improves
feature quality — activations have a large mean component that would otherwise be
absorbed into many features.

**The fundamental tension.** Raising $\lambda$ gives sparser, more interpretable
features and worse reconstruction; lowering it does the reverse. Since there is no
ground truth for "the right number of features", the operating point is chosen by
inspection. This is the feature-splitting problem in its formal dress.

Variants — TopK SAEs that fix the active count directly, JumpReLU with a learned
threshold — address the $\ell_1$ penalty's tendency to shrink activations, and none
resolves the dictionary-size question.

## Causal mediation

Activation patching is causal mediation analysis applied to networks. For a
mediator $M$ (some component) and treatment $T$ (clean vs corrupted input), the
**indirect effect** through $M$ is

$$
\text{IE} = \mathbb{E}\big[Y\big(T=0,\ M = M(T{=}1)\big)\big] - \mathbb{E}\big[Y(T=0)\big],
$$

i.e. run with the corrupted input but $M$ set to its clean value. That is exactly
the patched run.

The **direct effect** holds $M$ fixed and varies the input, and the two decompose
the total effect. Reporting both distinguishes a component that carries the
information from one that merely correlates with it.

Meng et al.'s ROME used this to localise factual associations to **mid-layer MLPs
at the subject token position** — and then went further, editing the MLP's weights
with a rank-one update to change the stored fact. That the edit works is strong
evidence the localisation was correct: prediction, intervention, confirmation.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Sparse autoencoders scaled to production models and the dictionary problem
remains.** Millions of interpretable features extracted from a frontier model,
demonstrably causal via steering. Feature splitting means the feature count is
still a hyperparameter, and there is no principled criterion for stopping.

**Attribution graphs moved the unit of analysis from components to computations.**
Tracing feature-to-feature causation on specific prompts produces something much
closer to "what the model did" than any previous method. It is labour-intensive and
currently applied to selected examples rather than at scale.

**The linear representation hypothesis mostly holds, with exceptions.** Many
concepts are single directions and steering along them works. Circular
representations — days of the week, months — have been documented, so "features are
directions" is a strong default rather than a law.

**Chain-of-thought is not reliably an explanation.** Mechanistic work has caught
models constructing post-hoc justifications for answers reached otherwise. Anyone
using reasoning traces as evidence of a model's process should hold that finding in
view.

## Open problems

- **No principled dictionary size.** Feature splitting continues indefinitely as
  you expand. Nothing says how many features a model "has".

- **Coverage is unknown.** SAEs reconstruct activations imperfectly, and what the
  residual contains is not characterised. Absence of a feature in the dictionary is
  not evidence of its absence in the model.

- **It does not scale by hand.** Circuit analysis is skilled manual work per
  behaviour. Automating it — using models to interpret models — is the obvious move
  and is not yet reliable.

- **Interpretability illusions.** A component can appear to implement a clean
  algorithm on your chosen distribution and behave differently off it. Findings
  validated on narrow prompt sets frequently do not generalise.

- **The gap to safety applications is real.** The stated goal is detecting
  deception, hidden goals, and dangerous capabilities. Current methods find
  features and small circuits; that is some distance from auditing a model's
  intentions, and the field is generally honest about the gap.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Elhage et al. (2021), Mathematical Framework** — the QK/OV decomposition. The
   foundation everything else builds on, and it repays slow reading.
2. **Olsson et al. (2022), Induction Heads** — the field's cleanest result,
   including the training-dynamics correlation.
3. **Elhage et al. (2022), Toy Models of Superposition** — why the neuron basis is
   wrong, argued from a solvable model.
4. **Templeton et al. (2024), Scaling Monosemanticity** — SAEs on a production
   model, with steering experiments that establish causality.
5. **Lindsey et al. (2025), On the Biology of a Large Language Model** — attribution
   graphs applied to real behaviours. The most concrete picture available of what a
   large model does internally.
