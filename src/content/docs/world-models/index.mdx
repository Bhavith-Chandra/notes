<Abstract>
A world model is a learned simulator. Instead of teaching an agent *what to do*,
you teach it *what happens next* — and then let it search over imagined futures
to decide. This page starts with no assumptions and ends at the open problems:
why the field abandoned pixel prediction for latent prediction, what breaks when
the horizon gets long, and why hierarchy is the current best answer.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **A world model is a learned transition function.** Given where you are and what you do, it predicts where you end up. Everything else on this page is a consequence of that one sentence.
- **Predicting pixels is the wrong objective.** Most of the bits in an image are irrelevant to control. Modern models predict in a learned *latent* space instead, which is both cheaper and more accurate on the things that matter.
- **Planning cost is exponential in the horizon.** This is the single constraint that shapes every design decision in the field, and the reason hierarchy keeps reappearing.
- **Errors compound.** A model that is 99% accurate per step is 37% accurate after 100 steps. Long-horizon world modelling is fundamentally a problem of error accumulation, not of model capacity.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## The idea in one paragraph

Before you reach for a cup, you have already imagined reaching for it. Not
vividly, and not in pixels — but somewhere in your head there is a fast,
approximate answer to the question *"if I move my arm like this, what happens?"*
You use that answer to reject bad plans before your body executes them. A **world
model** is the machine-learning version of that faculty: a neural network trained
to answer "what happens next?", which an agent can then query thousands of times
per second to decide what to do.

## Why this is a big deal

The dominant alternative is to learn a **policy** — a direct mapping from
situation to action. Policies are trained by trial and error, and they work well,
but they have three properties people find limiting:

1. **They are task-specific.** A policy trained to stack blocks cannot open a
   drawer. It has learned "what to do", and what to do changes with the task.
2. **They need a reward signal.** Someone has to define what success means,
   numerically, for every task.
3. **They need enormous amounts of on-task experience**, usually collected by the
   robot itself, which is slow and expensive in the real world.

A world model has none of these properties, because it never learns what to do.
It learns how the world behaves — which is the *same* whether you are stacking
blocks or opening drawers. Then, at deployment, you tell it the goal ("here's a
photo of the drawer open") and it searches for an action sequence that gets
there. No reward function, no task-specific training. That capability is called
**zero-shot planning**, and it is the reason the field cares.

<Aside type="tip" title="The one-line summary">
Policies learn *what to do*. World models learn *what happens*. The second is
reusable across tasks; the first is not.
</Aside>

## The catch, stated plainly

Everything good about world models comes from imagining futures. Everything hard
about them comes from the same place:

- **Imagined futures drift.** Each prediction step feeds the next, so small errors
  compound into nonsense. Ten steps is usually fine. A hundred is usually not.
- **There are too many futures to check.** If there are 10 plausible actions at
  each step, then looking 10 steps ahead means 10 billion possible plans. You
  cannot check them all, ever.

Keep both of these in your head. Almost every technique in the rest of this page
is an attack on one of them.

<Steps>

1. **Vision (V)** — an encoder that turns a high-dimensional observation (a
   $224 \times 224 \times 3$ image, say — 150,000 numbers) into a compact state
   vector $z_t$ (maybe 512 numbers). This is where almost all the compression
   happens.

2. **Memory (M)** — the predictor. Given the current state and an action, it
   outputs the next state: $\hat{z}_{t+1} = f(z_t, a_t)$. Historically an RNN,
   now usually a transformer. This *is* the world model, strictly speaking.

3. **Controller (C)** — decides the action. This is either a small learned policy
   (Dreamer-style) or a search procedure that rolls out many candidate action
   sequences through $M$ and picks the best (MuZero-style, and the MPC family).

</Steps>

## Reconstruction versus latent prediction

Here is the single most consequential design decision in the field, and it is
worth being precise about it.

**Reconstructive models** train the encoder and predictor so that the predicted
latent can be *decoded back into the next image*. The loss is measured in pixel
space. This is what the 2018 World Models paper, PlaNet, and the Dreamer line all
do.

**Joint-embedding predictive models (JEPAs)** never reconstruct anything. They
train the predictor to match the *encoder's own output* on the future frame. The
loss is measured entirely in latent space. V-JEPA 2 and DINO-WM are the current
representatives.

The argument for the second approach is an information argument, and it's clean:

<Aside type="caution" title="Why pixel prediction wastes capacity">
Consider a robot arm on a desk in front of a window. To predict the next frame in
pixels, the model must predict the leaves moving on the tree outside — which is
high-entropy, effectively random, and completely irrelevant to whether the gripper
closes on the cup. A reconstruction loss spends model capacity and gradient signal
on exactly the parts of the image that are hardest to predict *and* least useful.
Latent prediction lets the encoder simply discard the tree.
</Aside>

The counter-argument, which you should also hold: latent-only objectives have a
degenerate solution. If the encoder outputs a constant for every input, the
predictor's job becomes trivial and the loss goes to zero. This is called
**representation collapse**, and preventing it is the entire technical difficulty
of the JEPA line — handled through asymmetric architectures, stop-gradients, EMA
target encoders, and variance/covariance regularisation. Reconstruction has no
such failure mode, which is precisely why it was tried first.

|  | Reconstructive (Dreamer, PlaNet) | Latent-only (JEPA, DINO-WM) |
|---|---|---|
| Loss computed in | pixel space | embedding space |
| Wastes capacity on irrelevant detail | yes | no |
| Risk of collapse | none | central difficulty |
| Human-inspectable rollouts | yes (decode to video) | only via a separate decoder |
| Typical strength | sample efficiency, RL benchmarks | scaling to internet video, transfer |

<Aside type="note">
Note the last row of that table honestly: as of 2026 these two families are still
strongest in different regimes. Dreamer-family models dominate low-data RL
benchmarks; JEPA-family models dominate transfer from large-scale video. Anyone
telling you the debate is settled is ahead of the evidence.
</Aside>

## How planning actually works

Suppose you have a trained predictor and a goal. Planning by **Model Predictive
Control (MPC)** is the standard recipe, and it is simpler than it sounds:

<Steps>

1. **Sample** a few hundred candidate action sequences, each maybe 10–30 steps long.

2. **Roll out** each one through the world model, entirely in imagination. No robot
   moves. This is a batched forward pass.

3. **Score** each imagined trajectory by how close its final latent state is to the
   goal latent state — typically $\ell_1$ or cosine distance in embedding space.

4. **Refine.** Keep the best-scoring sequences, fit a distribution to them, resample
   from it. Repeat a handful of times. (This is the Cross-Entropy Method; MPPI is
   the other common choice.)

5. **Execute only the first action** of the best sequence. Then observe what
   actually happened and go back to step 1.

</Steps>

Step 5 is the part people miss, and it is what makes MPC robust. You plan 20 steps
ahead but commit to one step, so model error only has one step to hurt you before
you re-observe reality and re-plan. This is called the *receding horizon*, and it
is why MPC works at all with imperfect models.

<Aside type="danger" title="The exponential wall">
Sampling fixes nothing about the underlying combinatorics. With $A$ plausible
actions and horizon $H$, the space of plans is $A^H$. Doubling the horizon squares
the search space. This is not an implementation problem you can engineer around —
it is the reason flat planners fail on multi-stage tasks, and the reason the field
keeps returning to hierarchy.
</Aside>

## The setup

Model the environment as a partially observed Markov decision process. At time
$t$ the agent receives observation $o_t$, emits action $a_t$. We learn:

- an encoder $E_\theta: o_t \mapsto z_t$
- a predictor $P_\phi: (z_t, a_t) \mapsto \hat{z}_{t+1}$

For the **reconstructive** family we additionally learn a decoder $D_\psi$ and
optimise a variational bound. PlaNet's Recurrent State-Space Model factorises the
latent into a deterministic path $h_t$ and a stochastic path $s_t$, giving the
ELBO

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\sum_t \underbrace{\log p(o_t \mid h_t, s_t)}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{\beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(q(s_t \mid o_{\leq t}, a_{<t}) \,\|\, p(s_t \mid h_t)\big)}_{\text{consistency}}\Big].
$$

The deterministic path carries information reliably across many steps; the
stochastic path models genuine environment uncertainty. Dropping either one
measurably hurts — that ablation is the main empirical contribution of the PlaNet
paper.

For the **latent-only** family the objective collapses to a distance in embedding
space, with the target produced by a stop-gradiented or EMA copy of the encoder:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{JEPA}} = \sum_t \big\| P_\phi(z_t, a_t) - \operatorname{sg}\big[E_{\bar\theta}(o_{t+1})\big] \big\|_1 .
$$

The $\ell_1$ norm rather than $\ell_2$ is a deliberate choice — it is less
dominated by a few large-magnitude dimensions, which matters when the embedding
has no natural scale. The $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ and the separate parameters
$\bar\theta$ are what stop the trivial constant solution: the target cannot move
to meet the prediction.

## Planning as optimisation

Given a goal observation $o_g$ with embedding $z_g = E_\theta(o_g)$, planning
solves

$$
a^{\star}_{1:H} = \arg\min_{a_{1:H}} \; d\big(\hat{z}_{t+H},\, z_g\big), \qquad \hat{z}_{t+k+1} = P_\phi(\hat{z}_{t+k},\, a_{t+k}).
$$

Note what is *absent*: there is no reward function anywhere. The objective is
entirely "get the latent state close to the goal latent state". This is why the
approach is zero-shot — swapping the task means swapping $o_g$, nothing more.

## Why long horizons break: the compounding-error argument

Let $\epsilon$ be the per-step prediction error and assume the predictor is
$L$-Lipschitz in its state argument. Rolling out $k$ steps, the error at the end
is bounded by

$$
\|\hat{z}_{t+k} - z_{t+k}\| \;\leq\; \epsilon \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} L^{i} \;=\; \epsilon\,\frac{L^{k} - 1}{L - 1} \quad (L \neq 1).
$$

Read the two regimes off that expression:

- If $L < 1$ the sum converges to $\epsilon/(1-L)$ — error stays bounded forever.
  Contractive dynamics are forgiving.
- If $L > 1$ error grows *geometrically*. This is the normal case for anything
  interesting, because contact, collision and object interaction are locally
  expansive.

The practical consequence is that you cannot fix long-horizon planning by making
$\epsilon$ smaller. Halving the per-step error buys you one extra step of horizon
under geometric growth. You need to change $k$, not $\epsilon$ — which means
changing the *temporal resolution* at which you plan.

## Hierarchy, stated formally

That last observation is the whole argument for hierarchical world models, and it
is worth seeing why the numbers work.

Train two predictors sharing one latent space:

- a low-level predictor $P^{\text{lo}}(z_t, a_t)$ operating at primitive timestep
  resolution;
- a high-level predictor $P^{\text{hi}}(z_t, m_t)$ operating at stride $c$, where
  $m_t$ is a **macro-action** — a latent compression of $c$ primitive actions,
  produced by a learned action encoder $\Omega: a_{t:t+c} \mapsto m_t$.

Because both operate in the *same* latent space, a state predicted by the
high-level model is directly usable as a subgoal for the low-level model. No
translation layer, no learned skill vocabulary, no task-specific reward. That
shared-space property is the load-bearing idea.

The search cost changes from $A^{H}$ to

$$
\underbrace{M^{H/c}}_{\text{high level}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{H}{c}\cdot A^{c}}_{\text{low level, once per segment}}
$$

where $M$ is the size of the macro-action space. For $H = 32$, $A = 10$, $c = 8$:
flat search is $10^{32}$; hierarchical is roughly $M^{4} + 4 \cdot 10^{8}$. Even
generously sized macro-action spaces leave you many orders of magnitude better
off. And the compounding-error bound now applies with $k = c$ at the low level and
$k = H/c$ at the high level, so neither exponent is the full horizon.

<Aside type="note" title="This is not free">
Hierarchy buys tractability by assuming the task *decomposes* — that a good plan
can be described as a sequence of reachable intermediate states. Tasks where the
intermediate states are not meaningful, or where the right subgoal spacing varies
wildly within a single episode, do not benefit. Choosing $c$ is currently a
hyperparameter, and a sensitive one.
</Aside>

## Where the field actually is, as of 2026

**Zero-shot manipulation works, at moderate reliability.** V-JEPA 2-AC was
pre-trained on over a million hours of internet video, then adapted with fewer
than 62 hours of *unlabelled* robot trajectories from the DROID dataset. Deployed
on Franka arms in labs it had never seen, with no reward and no task-specific
training, it reached roughly 65–80% success on reach, grasp, and pick-and-place
from a single goal image. That is a genuine result and it was not possible three
years earlier. It is also nowhere near reliable enough to deploy.

**Flat planning fails on multi-stage tasks, and the failure is total, not
graceful.** The HWM results are stark: a single-level V-JEPA2-AC planner scores 0%
on end-to-end pick-and-place from a single goal image, and only succeeds when a
human manually decomposes the task into greedy subgoals. The hierarchical version
reaches 70% on the same task. A jump from 0 to 70 is not a tuning difference; it
says the flat formulation is structurally unable to represent the problem.

**Compute, not accuracy, is often the binding constraint.** Hierarchical planners
in that same work match or exceed flat-planner success at roughly 3× lower
planning cost. Planning-time compute is a first-class design axis now, in a way it
was not when the benchmarks were Atari.

## Open problems worth knowing about

- **Uncertainty is mostly unmodelled.** Almost no deployed latent world model
  represents *how confident* it is in a given prediction. Without that, a planner
  cannot distinguish a genuinely good plan from one that exploits a region where
  the model is simply wrong — the classic model-exploitation failure. This is, in
  my reading, the largest gap between current systems and reliable ones.

- **Subgoal spacing is hand-set.** The stride $c$ is a hyperparameter. Humans
  adapt their planning granularity continuously — coarse when walking across a
  room, fine when threading a needle. Nothing does this well yet.

- **Evaluation is weak.** Success rate on a handful of tabletop tasks is a low-
  resolution signal, and the benchmark suites differ enough between papers that
  cross-paper comparison is genuinely unreliable. Be sceptical of leaderboard-style
  claims in this subfield specifically.

- **The reconstruct-versus-latent question is empirically open.** See the note in
  Tier 2. The theoretical argument favours latent prediction; the benchmark
  evidence is split by regime.

- **Nobody has shown compositional generalisation.** Every current system plans
  within the distribution of dynamics it was trained on. Reasoning about a novel
  object with novel physics — the thing humans do effortlessly — remains out of
  reach.
