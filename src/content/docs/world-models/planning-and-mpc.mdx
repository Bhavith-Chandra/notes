<Abstract>
Model-predictive control is the trick that makes planning with a learned,
imperfect model work at all: search over imagined action sequences, execute only
the first action, then throw the rest away and re-plan from what actually
happened. Discarding 95% of your own computation sounds wasteful. It is the
single feature that keeps compounding model error from destroying you.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Executing one step of a long plan is the whole method.** The receding horizon means model error only gets one timestep to hurt you before reality corrects the state. Open-loop execution of the same plan fails catastrophically with the same model.
- **Sampling doesn't defeat the exponential, it just makes it tolerable.** The plan space is $A^H$. CEM and MPPI find good regions cheaply when the objective is smooth in action space, and both degrade sharply when it isn't.
- **The planner will find your model's bugs.** Optimising against a learned model means actively searching for inputs where it is wrong. This is *model exploitation*, and it is the reason a better model can produce a worse planner.
- **Planning-time compute is a design axis, not an implementation detail.** How much you spend at decision time trades against model quality, horizon, and success rate. Modern papers report it as a first-class number, and older ones mostly didn't.

</KeyPoints>

## Driving in fog

You are driving in fog with about fifty metres of visibility. You plan a route as
far as you can see — that gap, then the bend, then probably a straight. Then you
drive *five* metres, look again, and re-plan.

Why not drive the whole fifty? Because your view was blurry at the far end, and
after five metres you can see better. The plan was never meant to be executed; it
was a way of deciding what to do *right now*.

**That is model-predictive control.** Plan far, act barely, re-plan constantly.
The far-out part of the plan is not a commitment — it is context that makes the
immediate decision sensible.

## Why this rescues bad models

A learned world model is wrong, and its wrongness compounds: each prediction feeds
the next, so errors multiply. Twenty steps into an imagined rollout you may be
somewhere entirely fictional.

If you executed all twenty steps of a plan built on that rollout, you would be
acting on fiction by the end. But you execute *one* step, then observe where you
actually are and re-plan from there. The model's error gets one timestep to hurt
you, and then reality resets the accumulated drift to zero.

<Aside type="tip" title="The thing to actually remember">
MPC does not make the model accurate. It makes accuracy matter less, by never
letting error accumulate for more than one step. This is why a model good enough
for one-step prediction can support long-horizon behaviour it could never
*predict*.
</Aside>

## How you search

You cannot check every plan. With ten plausible actions per step and a
twenty-step horizon, that is $10^{20}$ sequences.

So you sample. Guess a few hundred plans at random, score them in imagination,
keep the best ones, and guess again *near those*. Repeat a handful of times. It is
a hill-climb over plans, and it works well when good plans have good neighbours —
which is usually but not always true.

## Cross-Entropy Method

The workhorse. It is a distribution-fitting loop, not a gradient method.

<Steps>

1. **Initialise** a Gaussian over action sequences: mean $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^{H
   \times |A|}$, diagonal std $\sigma$. On re-planning, shift last step's solution
   forward by one and reuse it — this warm start is most of why MPC is affordable.

2. **Sample** $N$ sequences from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, clipped to the
   action bounds. Typically $N = 100$–$1000$.

3. **Evaluate** each by rolling it through the model and scoring the terminal
   state against the goal. This is one batched forward pass, not $N$ passes.

4. **Select** the top $K$ — the *elites*. Usually $K/N \approx 0.1$.

5. **Refit** $\mu, \sigma$ to the elites, usually with momentum:
   $\mu \leftarrow \alpha\mu_{\text{elite}} + (1-\alpha)\mu$.

6. **Repeat** 4–10 times, then return $\mu$.

</Steps>

**Why it works.** Each iteration moves the sampling distribution toward the good
region and shrinks it. It is derivative-free, so it tolerates non-differentiable
models, discontinuous costs, and contact dynamics — which is exactly the regime
where gradient-based trajectory optimisation struggles.

**Where it fails.** A diagonal Gaussian cannot represent "go left *or* right, but
not straight". Faced with a bimodal optimum it fits a mean between the two modes
and produces a plan that is worse than either. This is the characteristic CEM
failure and it shows up whenever there is an obstacle to go around.

## MPPI

Same loop, different update. Instead of a hard top-$K$ cut, MPPI weights *every*
sample by the exponential of its (negative) cost:

$$
w_i = \frac{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,S_i\big)}{\sum_j \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\,S_j\big)}, \qquad \mu \leftarrow \sum_i w_i\, a^{(i)} .
$$

The soft weighting uses information from every rollout rather than discarding 90%
of them, and the temperature $\lambda$ interpolates continuously between "average
everything" and "take the argmax". MPPI has a derivation from stochastic optimal
control via the path-integral formulation, which CEM does not, and it is the
standard choice in real-time robotics.

## iCEM: the practical fixes

Pinneri et al. identified why vanilla CEM is sample-inefficient in real-time
settings and fixed it with a handful of changes that are now standard:

- **Coloured noise.** Sample temporally correlated (pink) noise instead of white.
  White noise produces jittery action sequences that mostly cancel out; correlated
  noise produces smooth, physically plausible motions and explores far more
  effectively per sample.
- **Elite memory.** Carry elites across re-planning steps rather than discarding.
- **Decaying sample count.** Spend more samples on early iterations, fewer later.
- **Clipping at the action bounds** rather than resampling.

The coloured-noise point is the one worth internalising: **the sampling
distribution should match the smoothness of plausible action sequences**, and for
physical systems that is not white.

## The problem

Given a learned dynamics model $f_\phi$, a state $z_t$, and a cost $c$:

$$
a_{t:t+H}^{\star} = \arg\min_{a_{t:t+H}} \; \sum_{k=0}^{H-1} \gamma^k\, c\big(\hat z_{t+k},\, a_{t+k}\big) \;+\; \gamma^H\, c_{\text{term}}\big(\hat z_{t+H}\big),
$$

$$
\text{subject to } \hat z_{t+k+1} = f_\phi(\hat z_{t+k}, a_{t+k}), \quad \hat z_t = z_t .
$$

In goal-conditioned world-model settings there is often no reward at all and the
terminal cost is a distance in latent space:

$$
c_{\text{term}}(\hat z_{t+H}) = d\big(\hat z_{t+H},\, z_g\big), \qquad z_g = E_\theta(o_g).
$$

Execute $a_t^\star$, observe $o_{t+1}$, set $z_{t+1} = E_\theta(o_{t+1})$, repeat.
Note what is *absent*: no reward function, no policy, no task-specific training.
Changing the task means changing $o_g$.

## Why the receding horizon works

Let $\epsilon$ bound the one-step model error and let $f_\phi$ be $L$-Lipschitz in
its state argument. After $k$ imagined steps:

$$
\|\hat z_{t+k} - z_{t+k}\| \;\leq\; \epsilon \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} L^{i} \;=\; \epsilon\,\frac{L^{k}-1}{L-1}, \qquad L \neq 1 .
$$

For $L > 1$ this grows geometrically. Now compare two execution strategies:

**Open loop** — execute all $H$ actions. The state error at execution time reaches
the bound at $k = H$, i.e. $O(\epsilon L^{H})$.

**Receding horizon** — execute one action, then re-observe. The state error at
execution is the $k=1$ bound, $O(\epsilon)$, every single step. The plan's tail
was computed on drifting predictions, but the tail is never executed; it only
influenced which first action looked best.

**The gap between $O(\epsilon L^H)$ and $O(\epsilon)$ is the entire value of MPC**,
and it is exponential in the horizon.

<Aside type="caution" title="What re-planning does not fix">
It bounds *execution* error, not *decision* error. If the model is badly wrong ten
steps out, the ranking of first actions can still be wrong — you will confidently
take a bad first step. Re-planning stops you compounding it, but a systematically
biased model produces systematically bad decisions no amount of re-planning
repairs.
</Aside>

## CEM, formally

$$
\mu^{(m+1)},\, \sigma^{(m+1)} \;=\; \text{MLE}\Big(\big\{a^{(i)} : i \in \mathcal{E}^{(m)}\big\}\Big),
$$

$$
\mathcal{E}^{(m)} = \operatorname{top-}K\Big(\big\{-S(a^{(i)})\big\}_{i=1}^{N}\Big), \qquad a^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mu^{(m)}, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^{(m)})^2\big).
$$

CEM is derived as minimising the KL divergence between the sampling distribution
and the optimal importance-sampling distribution $q^\star \propto
\mathbb{1}\{S(a) \leq \gamma\}\,p(a)$ — hence "cross-entropy". For a Gaussian
family the KL minimiser is just the MLE on the elites, which is why the update is
so simple.

**The diagonal covariance is the load-bearing approximation.** It cannot represent
correlations between action dimensions or across time, and cannot represent
multimodality at all. iCEM's coloured noise is a partial fix for the temporal part;
nothing in standard CEM addresses multimodality.

## MPPI, formally

MPPI derives from the stochastic Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. Under a
control-affine system with the noise entering through the control channel, the
optimal control admits a path-integral form, and the free-energy relation gives
the update

$$
u^{\star}_k \;=\; u_k + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda} S(\tau^{(i)})\big)\,\delta u^{(i)}_k}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda} S(\tau^{(i)})\big)},
$$

with $\delta u^{(i)}$ the injected noise on rollout $i$ and $S(\tau)$ the
trajectory cost.

Two readings of $\lambda$:

- $\lambda \to 0$: weights concentrate on the single best rollout. High variance,
  effectively random-shooting.
- $\lambda \to \infty$: uniform weights. The update is the mean of the noise, which
  is zero — no learning.

The derivation requires assumptions (control-affine dynamics, noise in the control
channel) that learned latent world models generally violate. MPPI is nonetheless
used in that setting and works; it is being applied outside its proof's
hypotheses, which is worth knowing when it behaves oddly.

## Model exploitation

The failure mode that makes planning against learned models genuinely hard.
Writing $f^\star$ for true dynamics:

$$
a^{\star} = \arg\min_a \; J_{f_\phi}(a) \quad\text{but we want}\quad \arg\min_a \; J_{f^\star}(a).
$$

The optimiser is free to find $a$ where $J_{f_\phi}(a) \ll J_{f^\star}(a)$ — that
is, regions where the model is *optimistically wrong*. And it is actively
searching for them, because low predicted cost is exactly what it selects for.

This produces a counterintuitive effect: **a model with lower average error can
produce a worse planner**, if its errors are concentrated in the region the
optimiser reaches. Average predictive accuracy is the wrong metric; what matters
is accuracy *on the distribution the planner induces*, which depends on the
planner.

The principled fix is uncertainty-aware planning — penalise trajectories the model
is unsure about:

$$
\tilde{J}(a) = J_{f_\phi}(a) + \beta\, \mathcal{U}(a),
$$

with $\mathcal{U}$ an epistemic uncertainty estimate, usually from an ensemble.
This works, and almost no deployed latent world model does it, because calibrated
uncertainty in a learned latent space is itself unsolved.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Planning compute is now reported as a headline number.** Older papers reported
success rate; current ones report success rate *versus* planning time, because the
trade-off is steep and practitioners need it. Hierarchical planners matching flat
planners at roughly 3× lower cost is the kind of result that only becomes
legible once compute is on the axis.

**Learned value functions have largely replaced long rollouts.** TD-MPC and TD-MPC2
plan over a short horizon and bootstrap with a learned terminal value, which
sidesteps compounding error by simply not rolling out far. TD-MPC2 scales this to
a single agent across many tasks and embodiments — currently among the strongest
results in model-based continuous control.

**Planning on frozen pretrained features works.** DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2024)
builds a world model on top of *frozen* DINOv2 features rather than training the
encoder jointly, and achieves zero-shot planning to image goals. This is
significant because it decouples representation learning from dynamics learning —
you can improve either independently.

**Flat planning fails on multi-stage tasks, and the failure is total.** The result
from the hierarchical planning literature is stark: a single-level planner scores
0% on end-to-end pick-and-place from one goal image, versus 70% for the
hierarchical version. Not a tuning gap — a structural inability.

## Open problems

- **Uncertainty is essentially unmodelled in deployed systems.** Without it,
  planners cannot distinguish a genuinely good plan from an exploit of a model
  error. This is, in my reading, the largest gap between current systems and
  reliable ones.

- **CEM's unimodality is unaddressed.** Real tasks have multimodal solutions —
  around the left or the right of an obstacle — and the standard sampler is
  structurally unable to represent them.

- **The horizon is a hyperparameter and a sensitive one.** Too short and the
  planner is myopic; too long and error dominates. Nothing sets it adaptively from
  model confidence, which is the obviously right thing to do.

- **The learned-cost problem.** In goal-conditioned latent planning, "distance in
  embedding space" is a proxy for "progress toward the goal", and it is not a
  metric on task progress. Two states equidistant from the goal in latent space
  may be very differently far in reality.

- **Real-time guarantees are absent.** MPC in classical control comes with
  stability and feasibility guarantees. Sampling-based planning against a learned
  latent model has none, which is a real barrier to deployment anywhere safety
  matters.

2. **Williams et al. (2015), MPPI** — for the path-integral derivation, so you know
