<Abstract>
Thirty-six years of work sits under the phrase "world model", and the systems it
covers have almost nothing in common at the implementation level — a Gaussian
process fitted to a cart-pole, a recurrent VAE dreaming Doom, a Monte Carlo tree
search over an abstract state with no decoder, a diffusion model rendering
playable video at 20 fps. What unifies them is one commitment: **learn what
happens next, then use it to decide.** This page catalogues every major variant,
what each predicts and in what space, where each is actually used, and what each
gives up.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Four axes explain the whole zoo.** What space you predict in (pixels, continuous latent, discrete tokens, abstract value-equivalent), how the model is used (dream-to-train, search, or plan), what trains it (reconstruction, latent prediction, reward-relevance), and whether dynamics are stochastic.
- **The field oscillated between reconstructing and not reconstructing, twice.** Pixels (2015) → latents with decoders (2018) → value-equivalent, no decoder (2019) → tokens (2022) → back to pixels via diffusion (2024). Each swing was a response to a concrete failure of the previous one.
- **MuZero is the sharpest idea here and the least imitated.** It never reconstructs anything. Its latent state is *defined* by predicting reward, value and policy — so it is free to discard everything not decision-relevant. That is a stronger claim than "compress the observation".
- **"World model" now names two different research programmes.** One builds *controllers* (Dreamer, TD-MPC, MuZero — the model exists to act better). The other builds *simulators* (Genie, GameNGen, Cosmos — the model exists to generate). They share a name, a lineage, and increasingly little else.

</KeyPoints>

## The one commitment

Every system on this page learns a function that answers: **given where I am and
what I do, what happens?**

$$
(\text{state},\ \text{action}) \longmapsto \text{next state}
$$

That is it. Everything else — the architecture, the loss, the planner — is a
choice about how to represent "state", how to train the map, and what to do with
it once you have it.

Once you see that, the zoo organises itself. Two models that look nothing alike
are usually making different choices on the same four questions.

## The four questions

<Steps>

1. **What space do you predict in?** Raw pixels are interpretable and expensive and
   full of irrelevant detail. A continuous latent is compact. Discrete tokens let
   you reuse language-model machinery. An abstract state with no decoder can throw
   away everything except what affects decisions.

2. **What do you do with it?** *Dream*: generate synthetic experience and train a
   policy on it. *Search*: expand a tree of futures and pick a branch. *Plan*:
   optimise an action sequence directly against a goal.

3. **What trains it?** Reconstruction (can you rebuild the observation?), latent
   prediction (can you predict your own encoder's output?), or value-equivalence
   (does the model predict reward, value and policy correctly — regardless of
   whether it resembles the world at all?).

4. **Is it stochastic?** Deterministic dynamics are simpler and wrong for anything
   with genuine uncertainty. Stochastic dynamics are necessary and harder to train.

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="The question that separates the two research programmes">
Ask what happens if the model's predictions look *wrong to a human* but the agent
acts well. For a **controller** world model, that is fine — MuZero's latents are
not decodable into game screens and nobody minds. For a **simulator** world model
it is total failure, because the output *is* the product. These are different
goals wearing one name, and confusing them is the most common error in reading
this literature.
</Aside>

## Family 1 — Classical model-based control (1990–2015)

The prehistory, and it is not obsolete. These methods remain the right answer for
low-dimensional systems with expensive data.

| System | Year | Model | Key idea | Where it still wins |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Schmidhuber's RNN world model** | 1990 | recurrent net | first proposal to train a predictive model and backprop through it to a controller | historical; the framing is the ancestor of everything here |
| **Dyna** | 1991 | tabular | interleave real experience with *simulated* experience from a learned model | the template for every dream-to-train method since |
| **PILCO** | 2011 | Gaussian process | propagate model *uncertainty* analytically through the rollout | cart-pole-scale problems with very few trials; still remarkably sample-efficient |
| **Guided Policy Search** | 2013 | local linear | fit simple local models, use them to supervise a global policy | real robots with limited data |
| **E2C** | 2015 | locally linear latent | learn a latent where dynamics are *linear*, so optimal control theory applies | when you want classical control guarantees on pixel inputs |

**PILCO deserves specific attention** because it did something modern methods
mostly abandoned: it modelled its own uncertainty and propagated it. A Gaussian
process gives a posterior over dynamics, and PILCO integrates over that posterior
when evaluating a policy — so it never exploits a region the model is unsure about.

That is precisely the [model exploitation](/world-models/planning-and-mpc/) problem
that deep world models still have. PILCO solved it, at the cost of scaling to
roughly ten state dimensions.

<Aside type="caution" title="The capability that was traded away">
Deep world models scale to pixels and lost calibrated uncertainty. PILCO had
calibrated uncertainty and could not scale. Fifteen years on, this trade has not
been undone — and it is the single clearest thing the modern literature could
recover from the classical one.
</Aside>

## Family 2 — Pixel-space video prediction (2015–2019)

Predict the next *frame*. Interpretable, expensive, and it taught the field what
not to do.

| System | Year | Contribution | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oh et al.** | 2015 | first convincing action-conditional Atari frame prediction | deterministic; blurs under uncertainty |
| **Finn et al.** | 2016 | predict *pixel motion* rather than pixel values, for robot pushing | struggles with novel objects |
| **SVG** | 2018 | learned prior over per-frame latents — genuinely stochastic video | still blurry at long horizons |
| **SimPLe** | 2019 | full model-based Atari: train PPO entirely inside a learned pixel simulator | model errors compound; beaten later by latent methods |

**The lesson these produced.** A deterministic model trained with MSE, faced with
genuine uncertainty, outputs the *mean* of the possible futures — which is a blur,
not a plausible frame. Every subsequent design either models the stochasticity
explicitly (SVG, RSSM) or moves the loss out of pixel space entirely (JEPA), and
both moves trace back to this.

## Family 3 — Latent dynamics, the Dreamer line (2018–2025)

The workhorse family. Encode observations to a compact latent, learn dynamics
there, and train a policy on imagined latent rollouts.

<Figure
  lead="The RSSM keeps two paths, and both are necessary."
  caption="The deterministic path is a GRU carrying information forward reliably — without it, information about distant past states is lost to sampling noise. The stochastic path samples a latent that captures genuine environment uncertainty — without it, the model cannot represent multiple possible futures and collapses to blurry averages. <Tabs>
<TabItem label="World Models (2018)">

**Ha & Schmidhuber.** VAE compresses frames to $z$; an MDN-RNN predicts
$p(z_{t+1}\mid z_t, a_t, h_t)$; a tiny linear controller (867 parameters) is
evolved with CMA-ES on top.

**The famous result**: training the controller *entirely inside the model's
hallucinated dream*, then transferring to the real environment. It worked on
CarRacing and VizDoom.

**The catch, which the paper is honest about**: the agent found and exploited the
dream's flaws. Adding a temperature parameter to make the dream *noisier* — harder,
not easier — was needed to prevent it. That is model exploitation, discovered and
named in the same paper that popularised the term "world model".

**Use it for**: understanding the field's structure. The V-M-C decomposition is the
clearest teaching example. Superseded in practice.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="PlaNet (2019)">

**The RSSM.** Split the latent into a deterministic recurrent path $h_t$ and a
stochastic path $z_t$, and train with a variational bound plus *latent
overshooting* — a multi-step consistency term forcing the model to be accurate over
several steps rather than only one.

**No policy at all.** PlaNet plans with CEM directly in latent space at every
timestep. Pure planning, no learned actor.

**Use it for**: continuous control from pixels when you want planning rather than a
policy, and when re-planning cost is acceptable.

**Weakness**: CEM at every step is slow, and the horizon is limited by compounding
error.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Dreamer V1→V3">

**V1 (2019)** replaced PlaNet's CEM with an actor-critic trained on imagined
rollouts, and — crucially — **backpropagates the value gradient through the learned
dynamics**. Analytic gradients rather than score-function estimates, which is far
lower variance.

**V2 (2020)** switched the stochastic latent from Gaussian to **categorical** (32
categories × 32 variables, straight-through gradients). Discrete latents suit the
sharp transitions in Atari, where a Gaussian must smear probability across states
that cannot occur.

**V3 (2023)** made it work across 150+ tasks *with fixed hyperparameters*. The
enabling trick is **symlog** prediction — regressing a symmetric log transform of
targets — which stabilises training across wildly different reward and observation
scales. First system to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch. Published in
Nature in 2025.

**DayDreamer (2022)** applied Dreamer to physical robots, learning locomotion in
about an hour of real-world interaction — the strongest practical demonstration
that world models buy sample efficiency where it costs real money.

**Use it for**: the default choice for model-based RL from pixels. Robust,
general, well-documented.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="TD-MPC / TD-MPC2">

The value-equivalent branch of planning. Learn a latent dynamics model with **no
decoder**, trained only to predict reward and value — then plan a *short* horizon
(around 5 steps) and bootstrap with the learned value at the end.

**Why this sidesteps compounding error entirely**: you never roll out far enough
for it to matter. The terminal value carries the long-horizon information instead.

TD-MPC2 scaled this to a single 317M-parameter agent covering 80+ tasks across
multiple embodiments and action spaces. Currently among the strongest results in
model-based continuous control.

**Use it for**: continuous control where sample efficiency matters and you want
strong performance without Dreamer's reconstruction cost.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

| System | Year | Adds | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MuZero** | 2019 | value-equivalent latent + MCTS; matches AlphaZero without knowing the rules | discrete-action games and planning problems with a simulator budget |
| **Sampled MuZero** | 2021 | samples actions instead of enumerating — extends MCTS to continuous and very large action spaces | continuous control, large combinatorial action spaces |
| **EfficientZero** | 2021 | self-supervised consistency loss, value prefix, off-policy correction; **human-level Atari from 2 hours of play** | extreme sample efficiency on discrete-action tasks |
| **Stochastic MuZero** | 2022 | chance nodes in the tree, so genuinely stochastic environments are handled correctly | backgammon, 2048, any environment with real randomness |

**Why value-equivalence is a stronger idea than compression.** Every other family
trains the latent to *represent the observation* in some sense. MuZero trains it to
*predict the quantities that matter for deciding*. If the colour of the sky affects
nothing, MuZero's latent is free to discard it entirely — not because a bottleneck
forced it, but because nothing in the objective ever asked about it.

**The cost.** No decoder means no inspectability, no transfer of the model to a new
reward function, and a hard dependence on reward signal — you cannot train MuZero
on reward-free data.

## Family 5 — Token world models (2022–)

Discretise observations with a [VQ-VAE](/encoders/neural-encoders/), then model the
token sequence with a transformer. Everything built for language models transfers.

| System | Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Trajectory Transformer** | 2021 | discretise states, actions and rewards; beam search over the sequence. RL as sequence modelling |
| **IRIS** | 2022 | VQ-VAE tokens + GPT-style dynamics; strong Atari-100k results, ~1.6 hours of play |
| **TWM** | 2023 | Transformer-XL dynamics with 100k-interaction budget |
| **STORM** | 2023 | stochastic transformer world model; better efficiency than IRIS at similar performance |
| **Genie** | 2024 | learns a **latent action space** from unlabelled internet video — no action labels at all |

**Genie is the outlier worth understanding.** Every other system here needs
$(s, a, s')$ triples. Genie infers a discrete latent action from consecutive frames
via an unsupervised action model, then trains a dynamics model conditioned on it.
The result is a controllable, generated 2D world from video that was never labelled
with controls — which unlocks internet-scale video as training data for interactive
models.

<Aside type="tip" title="Why tokenisation keeps reappearing">
Once your world is a token sequence, you inherit everything: transformers,
scaling laws, KV caching, speculative decoding, in-context learning. The same
argument drove [neural audio codecs](/domains/speech-audio/) and
[VQ-VAE](/encoders/neural-encoders/) for images. The cost is quantisation error and
a codebook that can collapse.
</Aside>

## Family 6 — Diffusion and video world models (2024–)

The return to pixels, for a different purpose.

| System | Year | What it is | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **UniPi** | 2023 | text-conditioned video generation as a planner; inverse dynamics extracts actions | controller |
| **DIAMOND** | 2024 | diffusion world model for Atari; visual detail matters for policy quality | controller |
| **GameNGen** | 2024 | diffusion model runs **DOOM at ~20 fps**, interactively, with no game engine | simulator |
| **Oasis** | 2024 | real-time playable Minecraft-like world model | simulator |
| **Cosmos** | 2025 | NVIDIA's world foundation model platform for physical AI and robotics | simulator / data engine |
| **Genie 2 / Genie 3** | 2024 / 2025 | large-scale foundation world models generating playable 3D environments | simulator |

**DIAMOND's finding is the interesting one for controllers.** Its argument is
that latent world models discard visual detail that turns out to matter — a small
sprite that determines a reward, for instance. Diffusion in pixel space preserves
it, and the resulting policies are better on exactly those Atari games.

That is a direct challenge to the "reconstruction wastes capacity" thesis, and it
is *not* resolved. The honest reading: which detail matters is task-dependent, and
neither family knows in advance which it is discarding.

<Aside type="danger" title="What a playable Doom does and does not demonstrate">
GameNGen generating interactive Doom is a genuine achievement in conditional video
generation. It is **not** evidence that the model understands Doom — there is no
explicit game state, no object permanence guarantee, and the paper reports drift
over long horizons. A simulator that looks right and has no persistent state is a
different thing from a model you could plan in. Read these results as graphics
results, which is how their authors largely frame them.
</Aside>

## Family 7 — Joint-embedding world models (2023–2026)

Predict in embedding space; never reconstruct. The [JEPA](/architectures/jepa/)
argument applied to dynamics.

| System | Year | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **I-JEPA / V-JEPA** | 2023 / 2024 | representation learning by latent prediction, images then video — the substrate |
| **DINO-WM** | 2024 | world model on **frozen** DINOv2 features; zero-shot planning to image goals |
| **Navigation World Models** | 2024 | conditional video-latent model for navigation; CVPR 2025 |
| **PLDM** (Sobal et al.) | 2025 | planning with latent dynamics learned from reward-free offline data |
| **V-JEPA 2** | 2025 | action-conditioned predictor; zero-shot robot manipulation from a goal image |
| **HWM** | 2026 | two timescales in one shared latent space; hierarchical planning |

**DINO-WM's contribution is architectural and underrated.** By freezing a
pretrained visual encoder and learning only the dynamics on top, it *decouples*
representation learning from dynamics learning. You can improve either
independently, and you get to reuse an encoder trained on far more data than your
control task could ever provide.

**V-JEPA 2 is the strongest zero-shot result**: pretrained on over a million hours
of internet video, adapted with fewer than 62 hours of *unlabelled* robot
trajectories, then deployed on Franka arms in unseen labs at roughly 65–80%
success on reach, grasp and pick-and-place — with no reward and no task-specific
training.

**HWM's result is the starkest number in the literature**: flat V-JEPA2-AC planning
scores **0%** on end-to-end pick-and-place from a single goal image; the
hierarchical version scores **70%**. See [hierarchical
planning](/world-models/hierarchy/).

## The common formalism

Every system here instantiates:

$$
E_\theta: \mathcal{O} \to \mathcal{Z}, \qquad
P_\phi: \mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{Z}, \qquad
[\,D_\psi: \mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{O}\,],
$$

with the decoder optional. The families differ in **what constrains $\mathcal{Z}$**:

| Family | Constraint on the latent |
|---|---|
| Pixel | $\mathcal{Z} = \mathcal{O}$; no constraint needed |
| Reconstructive latent | $D_\psi(E_\theta(o)) \approx o$ — invertibility |
| Value-equivalent | $\hat r, \hat v, \hat\pi$ correct — **no constraint on resemblance** |
| Joint-embedding | $P_\phi(z_t,a_t) \approx \operatorname{sg}[E_{\bar\theta}(o_{t+1})]$ |
| Token | $z \in \{1..K\}^{n}$ — discrete, from a learned codebook |

## RSSM and the ELBO

PlaNet's model factorises the latent into deterministic $h_t$ and stochastic $s_t$:

$$
h_t = f\big(h_{t-1},\, s_{t-1},\, a_{t-1}\big), \qquad
s_t \sim p\big(s_t \mid h_t\big), \qquad
o_t \sim p\big(o_t \mid h_t, s_t\big).
$$

Training maximises

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t}\Big(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p(o_t\mid h_t,s_t)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \beta\,\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(s_t\mid o_{\leq t}, a_{<t})\,\big\|\,p(s_t\mid h_t)\big)}_{\text{prior matching}}\Big).
$$

**Why both paths.** The deterministic path is not sampled, so information flows
through it without accumulating sampling variance — it can carry a fact from
$t{=}0$ to $t{=}50$ intact. The stochastic path can represent genuine
multimodality. Ablating either measurably degrades performance, which is PlaNet's
core empirical claim.

**Latent overshooting.** Standard training only enforces one-step accuracy. PlaNet
adds a multi-step term:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{overshoot}} = \sum_{t}\sum_{d=1}^{D} \mathrm{KL}\Big(q(s_t \mid o_{\leq t}) \,\Big\|\, p\big(s_t \mid s_{t-d},\, a_{t-d:t}\big)\Big),
$$

requiring the $d$-step prediction to match the posterior. This directly attacks
compounding error by making the model accurate over the horizon it will actually be
rolled out for — a small change with a large effect.

## DreamerV2's categorical latent

Replace the Gaussian $s_t$ with $32$ categorical variables of $32$ classes each,
sampled with straight-through gradients:

$$
s_t \sim \operatorname{Cat}\big(\pi_\theta(h_t)\big), \qquad
\nabla_\theta s_t \approx \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(h_t)\ \ (\text{straight-through}).
$$

**Why discrete helps on Atari.** A Gaussian posterior must place probability mass
continuously. When the true next state is one of a few sharply distinct
possibilities — a life lost or not, a level transition or not — a Gaussian smears
mass across intermediate states that cannot occur. Categorical variables represent
exactly the multimodality present.

This is the same argument that motivates [VQ-VAE](/encoders/neural-encoders/) over
VAE for images, and the same straight-through estimator.

## Dreamer's analytic value gradient

Dreamer's advantage over PlaNet is the objective it optimises. Imagine a rollout
$\{\hat s_\tau\}$ under $\pi_\theta$ and maximise

$$
\max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{p_\phi,\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{\tau=t}^{t+H}\gamma^{\tau - t} V_\lambda(\hat s_\tau)\right],
$$

with $V_\lambda$ a TD($\lambda$) value estimate. Because $p_\phi$ is a
differentiable neural network, the gradient can be taken **through the dynamics**:

$$
\nabla_\theta V \;=\; \frac{\partial V}{\partial \hat s_{\tau+1}}\cdot\frac{\partial p_\phi(\hat s_\tau, a_\tau)}{\partial a_\tau}\cdot\frac{\partial \pi_\theta(\hat s_\tau)}{\partial \theta} .
$$

Compare with the [score-function estimator](/training/reinforcement-learning/) used
in model-free policy gradients, which multiplies $\nabla\log\pi$ by a scalar return
and carries no information about *how* the return would change. **The analytic path
is dramatically lower variance**, and it is the main reason model-based RL is more
sample-efficient — not the imagined data volume, which is the usual explanation.

## MuZero's value equivalence

Three networks:

$$
s^0 = h_\theta(o_1,\dots,o_t), \qquad
(s^{k}, \hat r^{k}) = g_\theta(s^{k-1}, a^{k}), \qquad
(\hat p^{k}, \hat v^{k}) = f_\theta(s^{k}).
$$

Trained by unrolling $K$ steps and matching three observable targets:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{k=0}^{K}\Big[\underbrace{\ell^{r}\big(u_{t+k},\,\hat r^{k}\big)}_{\text{reward}} + \underbrace{\ell^{v}\big(z_{t+k},\,\hat v^{k}\big)}_{\text{value}} + \underbrace{\ell^{p}\big(\pi_{t+k},\,\hat p^{k}\big)}_{\text{policy}}\Big] + c\|\theta\|^2 ,
$$

with $\pi_{t+k}$ the MCTS visit-count distribution and $z_{t+k}$ the bootstrapped
$n$-step return.

**The value-equivalence principle, stated precisely.** Two models $M_1, M_2$ are
value-equivalent with respect to a policy set $\Pi$ and function set $\mathcal{V}$
if

$$
\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{M_1} v = \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{M_2} v \quad \forall \pi\in\Pi,\ v\in\mathcal{V},
$$

where $\mathcal{T}^\pi$ is the Bellman operator. MuZero seeks a model that is
value-equivalent to the real environment — **not one that resembles it**. A model
can be arbitrarily unlike reality internally and still induce identical optimal
behaviour, and searching over value-equivalent models is a strictly easier problem
than searching over accurate ones.

This is the deepest idea in the catalogue, and note what it costs: the latent is
defined *relative to a reward function*, so it does not transfer to a new task.

## MCTS with a learned model

Selection uses pUCT:

$$
a^{k} = \arg\max_{a}\left[ Q(s,a) + P(s,a)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_b N(s,b)}}{1+N(s,a)}\left(c_1 + \log\frac{\sum_b N(s,b) + c_2 + 1}{c_2}\right)\right].
$$

The first term exploits; the second explores, weighted by the prior $P$ and decayed
as the visit count grows. Because $g_\theta$ is a network rather than a simulator,
each expansion is a forward pass — so tree depth trades directly against wall-clock
in a way it does not for a real simulator.

**Stochastic MuZero** adds *chance nodes*: after an action node, an
afterstate leads to a chance node whose children are sampled outcomes with learned
probabilities. Without this, MuZero in a stochastic environment learns an
expectation model, and planning through expectations is wrong — the mean of the
outcomes is not an outcome.

## Diffusion world models

DIAMOND models $p(o_{t+1}\mid o_{\leq t}, a_{\leq t})$ with a
[diffusion](/architectures/diffusion/) process conditioned on history:

$$
o_{t+1} = \operatorname{Denoise}_\theta\big(\epsilon,\ o_{t-L:t},\ a_{t-L:t}\big).
$$

**Why diffusion rather than a deterministic decoder.** A deterministic model
trained with MSE outputs the conditional mean, which for a multimodal future is a
blur that is not a valid frame. Diffusion samples from the conditional
distribution, so it produces a *particular* plausible future. The 2015-era blurring
problem, solved properly.

**The cost is sampling.** Even few-step samplers make each imagined timestep far
more expensive than an RSSM's single forward pass, which caps rollout length and
therefore planning horizon.

## The JEPA world model

$$
\mathcal{L} = \big\| P_\phi\big(E_\theta(o_t),\, a_t\big) - \operatorname{sg}\big[E_{\bar\theta}(o_{t+1})\big]\big\|_1 ,
\qquad \bar\theta \leftarrow \lambda\bar\theta + (1-\lambda)\theta .
$$

Planning is goal-conditioned with no reward at all:

$$
a^\star_{1:H} = \arg\min_{a_{1:H}} d\big(\hat z_{t+H},\, z_g\big), \qquad z_g = E_\theta(o_g).
$$

**Where this sits between the other families.** Reconstruction sets a floor on
$I(Z;X)$ and wastes capacity on irrelevant detail. Value-equivalence sets a floor
on decision-relevance but requires reward. **Latent prediction requires neither** —
it is trainable on reward-free video and still learns dynamics. That is why it is
the family that scales to internet data.

The price is that collapse must be prevented architecturally, with no guarantee.
See [JEPA](/architectures/jepa/).

## Where the field is, as of 2026

**The controller/simulator split is now the main structural fact.** Genie 3,
GameNGen and Cosmos are generative video systems that happen to be interactive.
V-JEPA 2, TD-MPC2 and HWM are control systems that happen to predict. Both call
themselves world models; the benchmarks, the failure modes, and the research
communities have largely separated.

**Zero-shot planning from goal images works, at moderate reliability.** Roughly
65–80% on real manipulation in unseen environments with no task-specific training.
Real, and not deployable.

**Hierarchy is the settled answer to long horizons.** Flat planners fail totally
(0%) where hierarchical ones succeed (70%), at ~3× lower planning cost, and the
gain holds across several different underlying world models — which suggests it is
the planning structure, not the representation, doing the work.

**Reconstruct-versus-predict is genuinely unresolved.** DIAMOND argues visual
detail matters and demonstrates it on specific Atari games. V-JEPA argues detail
wastes capacity and demonstrates it on transfer. Both are right about their
evidence. Nobody can say in advance which detail your task needs.

## Open problems, across all families

- **Uncertainty is unmodelled.** PILCO had it in 2011 and nothing scalable has it
  now. Without it, planners cannot distinguish a good plan from an exploit of a
  model error — the single largest gap between current systems and reliable ones.

- **No transfer between world models.** Two models of the same environment,
  differently trained, have incomparable latent spaces. This blocks modular reuse
  and forces hierarchical systems to train levels jointly.

- **Evaluation is weak and inconsistent.** Success rate on a handful of tabletop
  tasks, with benchmark suites differing enough between papers that cross-paper
  comparison is unreliable. Be sceptical of leaderboard claims in this subfield
  specifically.

- **Long-horizon consistency.** Generative world models drift — objects vanish,
  layouts change. There is no persistent state, so nothing enforces object
  permanence.

- **Compositional generalisation is absent.** Every system plans within the
  distribution of dynamics it saw. Reasoning about a novel object with novel
  physics remains out of reach for all of them.
