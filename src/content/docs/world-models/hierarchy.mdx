<Abstract>
Long-horizon planning fails for two compounding reasons: the search space grows
exponentially in the horizon, and prediction error grows geometrically along a
rollout. Hierarchy attacks both with the same move — plan coarsely over long
spans, finely over short ones, and never let either exponent see the full
horizon. The idea is thirty years old; what changed recently is doing it entirely
inside a learned latent space, with no hand-designed skills.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Hierarchy fixes two problems with one mechanism.** Search cost drops from $A^H$ to roughly $M^{H/c} + (H/c)A^c$, and the error bound applies with exponent $c$ or $H/c$ instead of $H$. Both improvements come from the same decomposition.
- **The shared latent space is the load-bearing idea in the modern version.** If both levels predict in the *same* space, a state predicted by the high level is directly usable as a subgoal for the low level. No skill vocabulary, no translation layer, no task-specific reward.
- **The gain is not incremental.** A flat planner scoring 0% and a hierarchical one scoring 70% on the same task is not a tuning difference — it says the flat formulation cannot represent the problem.
- **Hierarchy assumes the task decomposes.** It buys tractability by assuming a good plan can be written as a sequence of reachable intermediate states. When that assumption fails, or when the right subgoal spacing varies within an episode, you get nothing.

</KeyPoints>

## Planning a journey

You are in London and need to get to a specific meeting room in Tokyo. You do not
plan this as a sequence of footsteps.

You plan: *get to Heathrow, fly to Haneda, get across the city, find the
building*. Four steps. Then, when you are actually walking to the station, you
plan the footsteps — but only those, and only then.

Two things are happening. You planned at a **coarse** resolution over the whole
journey, and you plan at a **fine** resolution only over the next small piece. You
never once considered the sequence of all footsteps from London to Tokyo, and if
you tried, you would fail — there are too many, and your prediction of where your
foot lands in hour nine is worthless.

## Why the flat version fails

Two separate failures, both fatal, and they compound.

**The search explodes.** With ten plausible actions at each step, a twenty-step
plan has $10^{20}$ possibilities. Doubling the horizon squares the space. No amount
of sampling makes a dent in that once the horizon is long enough.

**The imagination drifts.** Every predicted step feeds the next, so error
multiplies. Ten steps is usually fine; a hundred is usually fiction. Scoring a
plan by where you *think* you'll end up is meaningless if the ending is invented.

Hierarchy fixes both at once, and that dual fix is why it keeps reappearing across
thirty years of otherwise unrelated literatures.

## What "coarse" means when there are no words

The hard part is that a machine has no vocabulary of steps. It cannot plan *"get
to the airport"* unless something gives it the concept.

The classical answer was to hand-design a library of skills, or learn one. That
works and it is fragile — the skills are task-specific and someone has to decide
what they are.

**The modern answer is to skip skills entirely.** Train two predictors that share
one internal representation: a slow one that predicts where you'll be in eight
steps, and a fast one that predicts the next step. Because they speak the same
language, the slow model's prediction is directly usable as a target for the fast
model. The "subgoal" is just a point in latent space — nobody named it, nobody
designed it.

## The pieces

<Steps>

1. **Two predictors, one space.** A low-level model $P^{\text{lo}}(z, a)$ stepping
   one primitive action, and a high-level model $P^{\text{hi}}(z, m)$ stepping $c$
   primitive steps at once. Crucially both operate on the *same* $z$.

2. **An action encoder.** $\Omega: a_{t:t+c} \mapsto m_t$ compresses a chunk of
   $c$ primitive actions into a single latent macro-action. This is what makes the
   high-level search space $M$ rather than $A^c$.

3. **High-level search.** Plan over macro-actions to reach the goal embedding.
   Only $H/c$ steps, so both the search and the error compounding are over the
   short exponent.

4. **Subgoal extraction.** Take the *first* predicted state $\hat z_{t+c}$ from
   the high-level plan. That is the subgoal. Nothing was designed or named.

5. **Low-level search.** Plan $c$ primitive actions to minimise $d(\hat z_{t+c},
   z)$. Execute the first one.

6. **Re-plan.** Both levels, from the newly observed state. The receding-horizon
   logic from [MPC](/world-models/planning-and-mpc/) applies at both levels.

</Steps>

## How this differs from hierarchical RL

The classical literature — options, FeUdal, HIRO — is also hierarchical, and the
difference is worth being precise about because the words overlap.

| | Hierarchical RL | Hierarchical latent planning |
|---|---|---|
| Low level is | a learned policy (a *skill*) | a search procedure |
| Subgoals are | learned embeddings or designed states | states predicted by the high-level model |
| Needs reward | yes, per level | no |
| Task transfer | retrain or relabel skills | swap the goal image |
| Levels communicate via | a learned interface | a shared latent space directly |

**Options (Sutton, Precup & Singh, 1999)** established the formal framework:
temporally extended actions with an initiation set, a policy, and a termination
condition, turning the MDP into a semi-MDP. Everything since is a way of learning
options rather than specifying them.

**FeUdal Networks (2017)** had a Manager emitting directional goals in a learned
latent space and a Worker rewarded for moving in that direction. The direction-not-
position choice was deliberate and is the paper's most cited idea.

**HIRO (2018)** used raw state observations as subgoals, which made the levels
trivially compatible but only works when the state space is low-dimensional and
interpretable. Its real contribution was off-policy correction — relabelling old
high-level transitions when the low-level policy changes underneath them.

**Director (2022)** planned in the latent space of a learned world model, which is
the closest classical ancestor of the current approach.

<Aside type="tip" title="The through-line">
Every one of these is answering "what should the subgoal *be*?". Hand-designed
states, learned directions, raw observations, learned codes. The latent-world-model
answer is the least designed of them: **the subgoal is whatever the long-horizon
model predicts**, because that is by construction both reachable and on the way.
</Aside>

## The setup

Two predictors sharing an encoder $E_\theta$ and latent space $\mathcal{Z}$:

$$
P^{\text{lo}}_\phi: \mathcal{Z} \times \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{Z}, \qquad
P^{\text{hi}}_\psi: \mathcal{Z} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{Z},
$$

with macro-actions produced by a learned action encoder $\Omega_\xi: \mathcal{A}^c
\to \mathcal{M}$. Both are trained by latent prediction:

$$
\mathcal{L}^{\text{lo}} = \big\| P^{\text{lo}}_\phi(z_t, a_t) - \operatorname{sg}[E_{\bar\theta}(o_{t+1})] \big\|_1,
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}^{\text{hi}} = \big\| P^{\text{hi}}_\psi\big(z_t, \Omega_\xi(a_{t:t+c})\big) - \operatorname{sg}[E_{\bar\theta}(o_{t+c})] \big\|_1 .
$$

**The shared space is a constraint, not a convenience.** Because both losses
target embeddings from the same encoder $E_{\bar\theta}$, the outputs of both
predictors live in one space by construction. Train them with separate encoders
and the handoff in step 4 above becomes meaningless — you would be handing the low
level a point in a space it does not inhabit.

## Planning

$$
m^{\star}_{1:H/c} = \arg\min_{m_{1:H/c}} \; d\big(\hat z^{\text{hi}}_{H},\, z_g\big), \qquad \hat z^{\text{hi}}_{k+1} = P^{\text{hi}}_\psi(\hat z^{\text{hi}}_{k}, m_k),
$$

$$
z_{\text{sub}} = P^{\text{hi}}_\psi\big(z_t,\, m^{\star}_1\big),
$$

$$
a^{\star}_{1:c} = \arg\min_{a_{1:c}} \; d\big(\hat z^{\text{lo}}_{c},\, z_{\text{sub}}\big), \qquad \hat z^{\text{lo}}_{k+1} = P^{\text{lo}}_\phi(\hat z^{\text{lo}}_{k}, a_k).
$$

Execute $a^\star_1$, observe, repeat. No reward appears anywhere; the only
task-specific input is $z_g = E_\theta(o_g)$.

## The complexity argument

**Flat.** Search space $A^H$; error bound $O(\epsilon L^H)$ for Lipschitz constant
$L > 1$.

**Hierarchical.** The high level searches $M^{H/c}$; the low level searches $A^c$
once per segment, and there are $H/c$ segments:

$$
\underbrace{M^{H/c}}_{\text{high}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{H}{c}\, A^{c}}_{\text{low}} .
$$

Minimising over $c$ (treating $M \approx A$ for a rough comparison) gives an
optimum near $c \approx \sqrt{H}$, where both terms are $O(A^{\sqrt{H}})$ — the
exponent drops from $H$ to $\sqrt{H}$.

With $H = 32$, $A = 10$, $c = 8$: flat is $10^{32}$; hierarchical is $M^4 + 4
\times 10^8$. Even with a generous macro-action space the second is many orders of
magnitude smaller, and — the part that matters — each factor is individually
within sampling range, which $10^{32}$ never is.

**Error.** The low level compounds over $c$ steps, the high level over $H/c$
steps:

$$
\text{err} \;=\; O\!\big(\epsilon_{\text{lo}} L^{c}\big) \;+\; O\!\big(\epsilon_{\text{hi}} L^{H/c}\big).
$$

Neither exponent is $H$. This is the second, independent benefit, and it is why
hierarchy helps even when compute is not the constraint.

<Aside type="caution" title="Where the argument leaks">
The bound treats $\epsilon_{\text{hi}}$ as comparable to $\epsilon_{\text{lo}}$,
and it isn't: predicting $c$ steps ahead in one shot is a harder problem than
predicting one step, so $\epsilon_{\text{hi}} > \epsilon_{\text{lo}}$. The
hierarchy trades a *smaller exponent* for a *larger base*. It wins when $L^{H}$
dominates, which is the long-horizon regime — and it can lose at short horizons,
which is exactly what the empirical results show.
</Aside>

## Choosing the stride

The optimum balances the two error terms. Differentiating

$$
g(c) = \epsilon_{\text{lo}} L^{c} + \epsilon_{\text{hi}} L^{H/c}
$$

and setting $g'(c) = 0$ gives an implicit condition

$$
\epsilon_{\text{lo}} L^{c} \;=\; \epsilon_{\text{hi}} \frac{H}{c^2} L^{H/c},
$$

which has no closed form and — more to the point — depends on $\epsilon_{\text{lo}},
\epsilon_{\text{hi}}, L$, none of which are known. In practice $c$ is a
hyperparameter chosen by sweep, and results are sensitive to it.

This is the field's most obvious unaddressed problem. Humans vary planning
granularity continuously — coarse crossing a room, fine threading a needle — and
nothing here does.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**The empirical case is unusually clear-cut.** On end-to-end pick-and-place from a
single goal image, a flat V-JEPA2-AC planner scores 0%; the hierarchical version
scores 70%. The flat planner succeeds only when a human manually decomposes the
task into greedy subgoals — which is to say, only when a human supplies the
hierarchy by hand. A 0-to-70 jump is not a tuning result; it says the flat
formulation cannot represent the problem class.

**Compute, not just accuracy.** Hierarchical planning matches or exceeds flat
success at roughly 3× lower planning cost, and up to 4× in some settings. Two
independent wins from one change is rare, and it is the strongest argument for the
approach.

**Model-agnostic, which is the surprising part.** The hierarchical wrapper improves
several different latent world models — V-JEPA2-AC, DINO-WM, PLDM — without
modification. That suggests the gain comes from the planning structure rather than
from any particular representation, which is a stronger claim than a
single-model result would support.

**Two levels, not more.** Everything deployed is two-level. Three-plus-level
hierarchies are natural on paper and have not produced convincing results, which
is itself worth explaining and unexplained.

## Open problems

- **The stride is hand-set and sensitive.** No method adapts $c$ to the task, to
  the current state, or to model confidence. This is the clearest gap.

- **Tasks that do not decompose get nothing.** Hierarchy assumes a good plan is a
  sequence of reachable intermediate states. Where intermediate states are not
  meaningful, or where the useful spacing varies within an episode, the machinery
  adds cost and no benefit.

- **Subgoal reachability is unverified.** The high-level model predicts a state;
  nothing checks the low level can actually get there in $c$ steps. If it cannot,
  the low level chases an impossible target and wastes the segment. HIRO's
  off-policy correction addressed a version of this in the RL setting; the
  planning setting largely does not.

- **Deeper hierarchies don't work yet**, for reasons nobody has articulated
  clearly.

- **The uncertainty problem is inherited and worsened.** The high-level model is
  less accurate by construction, and neither level represents its own confidence,
  so the planner cannot tell a good subgoal from a hallucinated one.

2. **Vezhnevets et al. (2017), FeUdal** — for the directional-goal argument.
