<Abstract>
A graph neural network passes messages along edges: each node repeatedly gathers
information from its neighbours and updates itself. That single mechanism covers
almost every GNN ever published — and it comes with a proven ceiling. Standard
message passing cannot distinguish any two graphs that the Weisfeiler-Lehman
colour refinement test cannot distinguish, which rules out counting triangles and
telling some molecules apart.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Message passing is the whole framework.** Aggregate from neighbours, combine with your own state, repeat. GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT and GIN differ only in the aggregate and combine functions.
- **The aggregator must be permutation-invariant, and that constrains expressivity.** Neighbours have no canonical order, so you need a set function. Mean and max lose information that sum retains — which is exactly why GIN uses sum.
- **Expressivity is capped by the 1-WL test.** No amount of width, depth or training lets standard message passing separate graphs 1-WL cannot. Cycle counting and many molecular distinctions fall outside it.
- **Depth hurts, for two different reasons.** Oversmoothing makes all node representations converge as layers stack; oversquashing crushes exponentially many distant nodes through a fixed-width channel. Most useful GNNs are 2–4 layers.

</KeyPoints>

<Tier level="intuition" />

## Why images and text don't cover this

A sentence is a sequence — every token has a position, and "third from the left"
means something. An image is a grid — every pixel has coordinates.

A social network has neither. A user has friends, and there is no first friend, no
canonical order, no fixed count. A molecule is atoms bonded to atoms; rotate your
drawing and it is the same molecule.

**Graphs have structure without coordinates**, and that is what needs a different
architecture. Anything you compute must give the same answer if the nodes are
relabelled.

## Message passing

The algorithm is simple enough to state completely.

Every node starts with a feature vector. Then, repeatedly:

1. Each node collects the vectors of its neighbours.
2. It combines them into one summary — but the summary must not depend on the
   *order* they arrived in, so it must be something like a sum, mean or max.
3. It updates itself from its old state plus that summary.

After one round, each node knows about its immediate neighbours. After two, about
neighbours-of-neighbours. **After $k$ rounds, each node's representation depends on
everything within $k$ hops.**

<Aside type="tip" title="Depth means radius, not capacity">
In a CNN, more layers mean more abstraction. In a GNN, more layers mean a larger
*neighbourhood* — you are literally choosing how far information travels. That
reframing explains why GNNs are shallow: three layers on a small-world graph
already reaches most of it.
</Aside>

## The ceiling

Here is the surprising part, and it is a theorem rather than an observation.

Standard message passing **cannot distinguish** certain pairs of structurally
different graphs. Consider two graphs: one is a single six-node ring; the other is
two separate three-node triangles. Every node in both has exactly two neighbours.
Every neighbourhood looks identical at every round.

The messages are the same. The representations are the same. **No GNN of this type
can tell them apart**, however wide, however deep, however trained.

This exactly matches the limit of a 1960s graph-isomorphism heuristic — the
Weisfeiler-Lehman colour refinement test — and that correspondence is the field's
central theoretical result. It has practical consequences: counting triangles,
detecting rings in molecules, and several other chemically meaningful properties
sit outside what message passing can compute.

## Why deeper isn't better

Two distinct problems, often conflated.

**Oversmoothing.** Each round mixes a node with its neighbours. Do this enough
times and everything converges to the same value — like repeatedly averaging a
picture with its blurred self until it is uniformly grey. Node representations
become indistinguishable and classification collapses.

**Oversquashing.** To see a node $k$ hops away, information must funnel through
intermediate nodes. The number of nodes at distance $k$ grows exponentially; the
vector carrying them is fixed-width. Exponentially much information is compressed
into constant space, and most of it is destroyed.

These pull in the same direction — keep it shallow — but they are different
mechanisms, and the fixes differ.

## The four canonical variants

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="GCN">

Symmetric-normalised mean over the neighbourhood including self-loops:

$$
H^{(l+1)} = \sigma\!\left(\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\, \tilde D^{-1/2} H^{(l)} W^{(l)}\right).
$$

The $\tilde D^{-1/2}$ on both sides is the detail worth understanding. Plain
$\tilde D^{-1}\tilde A$ (row normalisation) is a random walk; the symmetric version
keeps the operator's eigenvalues in $[-1,1]$, which keeps repeated application
stable. Without it, activations explode or vanish with depth.

Simple, fast, and the mean aggregation is exactly what limits its expressivity.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="GraphSAGE">

Two contributions that mattered more than the architecture:

**Neighbour sampling.** Sample a fixed number of neighbours rather than using all
of them. This bounds the computation per node, which is what makes GNNs work on
graphs with high-degree hubs — without it, one celebrity account with ten million
followers dominates the batch.

**Inductive learning.** Learn an aggregation *function* rather than per-node
embeddings, so the model generalises to nodes never seen during training. GCN as
originally formulated is transductive — the whole graph must be present at training
time — which is unusable for a graph that grows.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="GAT">

Learn attention weights over neighbours instead of fixing them:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\big(\text{LeakyReLU}(a^{\top}[Wh_i \,\|\, Wh_j])\big)}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\exp\big(\text{LeakyReLU}(a^{\top}[Wh_i \,\|\, Wh_k])\big)} .
$$

The graph supplies the mask; attention supplies the weights. This is exactly a
transformer restricted to attend only along edges — which is the observation that
later motivated graph transformers.

Better than uniform averaging when neighbours differ in relevance, and it does not
raise the expressivity ceiling: attention weights are still a function of the
neighbourhood multiset.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="GIN">

Designed backwards from the theory. Xu et al. asked what aggregator would be
*maximally* expressive, and derived:

$$
h_v^{(l+1)} = \operatorname{MLP}^{(l)}\!\left((1+\epsilon)\,h_v^{(l)} + \sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(v)} h_u^{(l)}\right).
$$

**Sum, not mean or max.** Sum is injective on multisets (given suitable features);
mean cannot distinguish $\{a,b\}$ from $\{a,a,b,b\}$, and max cannot distinguish
$\{a,b\}$ from $\{a,a,b\}$. The MLP then approximates an arbitrary injective
function of that sum.

GIN is provably as powerful as the 1-WL test — the ceiling for message passing —
and no more.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Oversmoothing versus oversquashing

| | Oversmoothing | Oversquashing |
|---|---|---|
| Symptom | all nodes converge to the same vector | distant information never arrives |
| Cause | repeated averaging is a low-pass filter | exponential neighbourhood, fixed-width channel |
| Shows up on | homophilous graphs, deep stacks | tasks needing long-range dependencies |
| Fixes | residual connections, PairNorm, jumping knowledge | graph rewiring, virtual nodes, graph transformers |

They are frequently conflated because both say "don't go deep". The distinction
matters when choosing a fix: residual connections help oversmoothing and do nothing
for oversquashing, because the bottleneck is bandwidth, not signal decay.

<Aside type="caution" title="Check homophily before reaching for a GNN">
GNNs assume connected nodes are similar — *homophily*. On heterophilous graphs
(fraud networks, where fraudsters connect to legitimate accounts precisely to blend
in), neighbourhood averaging actively destroys the signal. A plain MLP on node
features frequently beats a GCN there, and this is a commonly-skipped baseline.
</Aside>

## The message passing framework

Gilmer et al.'s formulation, which subsumes essentially every GNN:

$$
m_v^{(l+1)} = \bigoplus_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} M_l\big(h_v^{(l)},\, h_u^{(l)},\, e_{uv}\big),
\qquad
h_v^{(l+1)} = U_l\big(h_v^{(l)},\, m_v^{(l+1)}\big),
$$

with $\bigoplus$ a permutation-invariant aggregator, $M_l$ the message function,
and $U_l$ the update function. For graph-level tasks a final readout pools all
nodes:

$$
\hat y = R\big(\{h_v^{(L)} : v \in V\}\big).
$$

**Permutation equivariance** is the defining property:

$$
f(PAP^{\top},\, PX) = P\,f(A, X)
$$

for any permutation matrix $P$. Relabel the nodes and the outputs permute
identically. This is the graph analogue of a CNN's translation equivariance, and it
is why the aggregator must be a set function.

## The Weisfeiler-Lehman bound

The 1-WL colour refinement algorithm:

$$
c_v^{(0)} = \text{initial label}, \qquad
c_v^{(l+1)} = \operatorname{HASH}\!\Big(c_v^{(l)},\ \{\!\{\, c_u^{(l)} : u \in \mathcal{N}(v) \,\}\!\}\Big),
$$

where $\{\!\{\cdot\}\!\}$ denotes a multiset. If two graphs' colour multisets differ
at any round, they are non-isomorphic. If they never differ, the test cannot
decide.

**Xu et al.'s theorem.** Any message-passing GNN maps two graphs to the same
representation whenever 1-WL assigns them the same colouring. So:

$$
\text{expressivity(MPNN)} \;\leq\; \text{expressivity(1-WL)} .
$$

The proof is a straightforward induction: if $c_u^{(l)} = c_{u'}^{(l)}$ for
corresponding nodes at layer $l$, then the aggregated multisets are equal, so the
updates are equal at layer $l+1$.

**Achieving the bound requires injectivity.** GIN attains it because sum over a
multiset of countable features is injective, and by universal approximation an MLP
can represent the required injective map. Mean fails:

$$
\operatorname{mean}(\{a, b\}) = \operatorname{mean}(\{a,a,b,b\}),
$$

so any mean-aggregating GNN is *strictly* weaker than 1-WL — this is the formal
reason GCN and GAT sit below GIN.

**What lies outside.** 1-WL cannot count cycles or triangles, cannot distinguish
regular graphs of the same degree, and cannot compute clustering coefficients.
Since these are chemically and socially meaningful, the bound is a practical
limitation rather than a curiosity. Higher-order $k$-WL variants are strictly more
powerful and cost $O(n^k)$, which is why they are rare in practice.

## Oversmoothing, formally

For a GCN, propagation applies $\hat A = \tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A \tilde D^{-1/2}$
repeatedly. Its eigenvalues satisfy $\lambda_i \in (-1, 1]$ with $\lambda_1 = 1$.
Writing $H^{(0)}$ in the eigenbasis $\{u_i\}$:

$$
\hat A^{L} H^{(0)} = \sum_i \lambda_i^{L}\,\langle u_i, H^{(0)}\rangle\, u_i \;\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}\; \langle u_1, H^{(0)}\rangle\, u_1 .
$$

Every component except the top eigenvector decays geometrically. The limit depends
on $H^{(0)}$ only through its projection onto $u_1$ — which for the normalised
adjacency is determined by node degrees. **All node representations converge to a
function of degree alone**, and everything that distinguished them is gone.

The rate is governed by the spectral gap $1 - |\lambda_2|$: a large gap means fast
smoothing, so well-connected graphs oversmooth in fewer layers.

**Why residual connections help.** With $H^{(l+1)} = \hat A H^{(l)} W + H^{(l)}$,
the effective operator is $\hat A W + I$, whose spectrum is shifted away from the
contraction — the identity term preserves a component of the original signal at
every layer. Exactly the same mechanism as in
[backpropagation](/foundations/backpropagation/).

## Oversquashing, formally

Alon & Yahav's argument. The number of nodes within $k$ hops grows roughly as

$$
|\mathcal{N}_k(v)| \sim \bar d^{\,k}
$$

for average degree $\bar d$. All of that information must pass through node $v$'s
representation, of fixed size $h$. So the information per source node scales as

$$
\frac{h}{\bar d^{\,k}},
$$

decaying exponentially in $k$. A task requiring a specific fact from 5 hops away on
a degree-10 graph is trying to route one signal out of $10^5$ through a
fixed-width channel.

The formal measure is the Jacobian sensitivity:

$$
\left\|\frac{\partial h_v^{(L)}}{\partial h_u^{(0)}}\right\|
$$

for $u$ at distance $L$ from $v$. Bounds show this decays exponentially in the
distance, controlled by the graph's **Ricci curvature** at the bottleneck edges —
negatively curved edges are the ones that squash. This is why rewiring methods
target exactly those edges.

**Note the contrast with oversmoothing.** Oversmoothing is signal *decay*;
oversquashing is *bandwidth*. Adding residual connections fixes the first and not
the second, because a residual does not widen the channel.

## Graph transformers

Attend over all node pairs rather than only along edges, injecting graph structure
through the attention bias instead of the mask:

$$
A_{ij} = \frac{(h_i W_Q)(h_j W_K)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}} + b_{\phi(i,j)} + c_{\text{edge}(i,j)},
$$

with $\phi(i,j)$ the shortest-path distance and $b$ a learned per-distance bias.
Graphormer adds a *centrality* encoding to node features as well.

**What this buys.** Every node reaches every other in one layer, so oversquashing
disappears — there is no bottleneck to squash through. And because shortest-path
distance is a stronger signal than the WL colouring, Graphormer provably exceeds
1-WL expressivity.

**What it costs.** $O(n^2)$ attention, so it is limited to small graphs —
molecules, mostly, where $n$ is tens of atoms. It is not an option for a
million-node social network, which is why message passing persists.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Graph transformers dominate molecular property prediction; message passing
dominates large-scale graphs.** The split is entirely about $n$. For molecules,
quadratic attention is affordable and the expressivity gain is real. For web-scale
graphs it is not, and sampled message passing remains the only practical option.

**AlphaFold is the field's landmark application**, and worth being precise about:
it is not a plain GNN. Its Evoformer operates on a pair representation with
triangle updates enforcing geometric consistency — closer to a graph transformer
with domain-specific structure than to a message-passing network. The lesson
generally drawn is that heavy domain structure beats generic architectures on
scientific problems.

**Positional encodings partially lift the WL ceiling.** Laplacian eigenvectors,
random-walk encodings, and shortest-path features all inject information message
passing cannot derive, raising expressivity without $k$-WL's cost. This is the most
active area and none of the schemes is clearly best.

**Benchmarks were bad and are improving.** Dwivedi et al. showed that the standard
small citation datasets could not distinguish architectures — differences fell
inside seed variance. Many pre-2020 GNN comparisons should be read with that in
mind. Larger, better-designed benchmarks have since replaced them.

## Open problems

- **Expressivity beyond 1-WL is expensive.** $k$-WL costs $O(n^k)$. Positional
  encodings are cheap heuristics without clean guarantees. There is no known
  method that is both provably more expressive and scalable.

- **Heterophily is under-served.** Most architectures assume connected nodes are
  similar. Fraud detection, adversarial networks, and many biological graphs
  violate this, and specialised methods do not transfer well between them.

- **Scaling is a systems problem.** Neighbour explosion makes minibatching genuinely
  hard — sampling introduces variance, and full-batch training does not fit. Neither
  answer is satisfactory.

- **Dynamic graphs are unsolved.** Most methods assume a static graph. Real graphs
  change continuously, and recomputing embeddings on every edge insertion is
  infeasible.

- **No foundation model for graphs.** Unlike text or images, graphs from different
  domains share no common vocabulary — a molecule and a social network have nothing
  transferable. Whether a general graph pretraining objective exists is genuinely
  open.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Xu et al. (2018), GIN** — the WL connection. The most important theoretical
   result here and the paper is readable.
2. **Alon & Yahav (2020)** — oversquashing, with the bottleneck experiment that
   makes it concrete.
3. **Hamilton et al. (2017), GraphSAGE** — for sampling and inductive learning,
   which is what makes GNNs deployable.
4. **Dwivedi et al. (2020)** — read it before trusting any GNN benchmark comparison.
