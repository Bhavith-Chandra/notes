<Abstract>
Speech recognition has a structural problem text does not: one second of audio is
a hundred frames and perhaps three characters, and nobody labelled which frames
belong to which character. CTC solved that alignment problem in 2006 by summing
over all possible alignments — and the same idea, applied in reverse through
neural codecs, is why text-to-speech is now a language modelling task.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **Alignment is the defining difficulty.** Audio frames vastly outnumber output tokens and the correspondence is unlabelled. CTC introduces a blank symbol and marginalises over every alignment consistent with the transcript, computed efficiently by dynamic programming.
- **CTC assumes conditional independence between outputs; RNN-T does not.** That single difference is why RNN-T dominates streaming production ASR — it has an internal language model, CTC needs an external one.
- **Neural codecs turned audio into tokens, and that changed generation.** Once a second of speech is 75 discrete tokens, generating audio becomes next-token prediction. VALL-E clones a voice from three seconds of reference audio on that basis.
- **Weak supervision beat clever self-supervision for ASR.** Whisper trained on 680k hours of noisy web audio-transcript pairs and generalises better across domains than models with lower benchmark WER. Scale and diversity beat pretraining sophistication here.

</KeyPoints>

## The alignment problem

You have a one-second recording of someone saying "cat". At a typical frame rate
that is about a hundred feature frames, and three characters of output.

Which frames are the "c"? Where does "a" begin? Nobody labelled it, and labelling
it by hand for thousands of hours is out of the question.

Before 2006 this was handled by iterative forced alignment inside an HMM pipeline —
guess an alignment, train, re-align, repeat. It worked and it was elaborate.

**CTC's answer is to refuse to choose.** Instead of picking one alignment, sum the
probability over *all* alignments that produce the right transcript. Add a blank
symbol meaning "no output here", allow repeats, and define a collapsing rule:
merge repeated characters, then drop blanks. `c-aa-t` and `cc-a-tt` both collapse
to `cat`.

There are exponentially many valid alignments, and dynamic programming sums them in
polynomial time — the same trick as the forward algorithm for HMMs.

## What CTC gives up

CTC makes one strong assumption: **given the audio, each output is independent of
the others.**

This is clearly false. After "cat sat on the ma" the next character is
overwhelmingly "t", and CTC's model structure cannot express that — it looks only
at the audio at each frame.

In practice this shows up as phonetically plausible nonsense: "recognise speech"
transcribed as "wreck a nice beach". Every frame was decoded reasonably; nothing
enforced that the sequence be a sentence.

The standard fix is to bolt on an external language model at decoding time.
**RNN-T's fix is structural**: feed previous outputs back into the model, so it
carries its own language model internally. That is why RNN-T, not CTC, runs on your
phone.

<Aside type="tip" title="Why streaming forces the architecture">
An offline system can attend over the whole utterance. A live captioning system
must emit words as you speak, so it can only look backwards. This rules out
bidirectional encoders and full attention-based encoder–decoders, and it is the
main reason RNN-T dominates production ASR while Whisper-style models dominate
offline transcription.
</Aside>

## Audio as tokens

The other half of this page is generation, and one idea unlocked it.

A **neural codec** compresses audio into a sequence of discrete tokens — around 75
tokens per second — from which a decoder reconstructs near-original quality. This
is [VQ-VAE](/encoders/neural-encoders/) applied to waveforms, with a residual
refinement so that successive codebooks encode progressively finer detail.

Once audio is a token sequence, generating it is next-token prediction. Everything
built for language models transfers. VALL-E's demonstration was striking: given
three seconds of someone's voice as a prompt, continue in that voice saying
arbitrary new text — voice cloning as in-context learning.

## The three ASR architectures

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="CTC">

Encoder only. Per-frame distribution over characters plus blank, trained by summing
over alignments.

**Fast** — one forward pass, all frames decoded in parallel. **Streamable** with a
causal encoder. **Weakest** language modelling, because of the independence
assumption.

Still widely used as an auxiliary loss even in systems that decode differently: it
provides a strong per-frame training signal that stabilises the encoder.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="RNN-T">

Adds a **prediction network** — an autoregressive model over the output so far —
and a **joint network** combining it with the encoder at each frame.

The output distribution is now conditioned on previous outputs, so the model has an
internal language model. It remains streamable, because at each step it either
emits a token or advances to the next frame, and both decisions depend only on past
audio.

**The dominant production architecture for streaming ASR.** More complex to train
— the lattice is two-dimensional rather than one — and worth it.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Attention encoder–decoder">

A standard [encoder–decoder](/encoders/encoder-decoder/): encode the whole
utterance, decode text with cross-attention. Whisper is this.

No alignment machinery at all — attention learns the correspondence implicitly.
Best offline accuracy, and **not streamable**, since the encoder needs the full
utterance.

A known failure mode: because the decoder is a strong language model, it can
"hallucinate" fluent text during silence or noise, generating a plausible sentence
with no acoustic support. CTC and RNN-T are structurally less prone to this.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Conformer">

The encoder, not the loss — usable with any of the above.

Self-attention captures long-range dependencies; convolution captures local
patterns like formant transitions. Speech needs both, and interleaving them beats
either alone. The Conformer block sandwiches a convolution module between two
feed-forward halves with attention in the middle.

**The default speech encoder**, and one of the clearest cases where a
domain-specific architectural prior genuinely pays.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Self-supervised speech

Labelled speech is expensive; raw audio is abundant. The same logic as elsewhere in
[self-supervised learning](/training/self-supervision/).

**wav2vec 2.0** masks spans of latent speech representations and solves a
contrastive task: identify the true quantised latent among distractors. Its result
was striking — competitive ASR from **ten minutes** of labelled data plus large-scale
pretraining.

**HuBERT** replaces the contrastive objective with masked prediction of cluster
assignments. Cluster the features (initially MFCCs, then the model's own
representations), then predict the cluster ID of masked frames — BERT for speech,
with the clustering supplying the vocabulary. Iterating the clustering with better
representations is the key trick.

**Whisper** went the other way: no self-supervision, just 680,000 hours of weakly
supervised web audio-transcript pairs. Its benchmark WER on clean read speech is
not state of the art; its *robustness* across accents, noise, and domains is far
better, and it does multilingual recognition and translation in one model.

<Aside type="caution" title="What Whisper's result actually shows">
It is easy to read this as "scale beats method". The more precise claim is that
**training distribution diversity beats benchmark optimisation**. Models tuned on
LibriSpeech achieve better LibriSpeech WER and degrade on anything else. Whisper's
messy web-scale data covers the variation real audio contains. Which you want
depends on whether your deployment resembles your benchmark.
</Aside>

## The CTC objective

Given input $X$ of length $T$ and target $\mathbf{y}$ of length $U \leq T$, define
the extended alphabet $\mathcal{A}' = \mathcal{A}\cup\{\varnothing\}$ and the
collapsing map $\mathcal{B}: \mathcal{A}'^{T}\to\mathcal{A}^{\leq T}$ that merges
repeats then removes blanks.

$$
p(\mathbf{y}\mid X) = \sum_{\pi \in \mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})} \prod_{t=1}^{T} p\big(\pi_t \mid X\big), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}} = -\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid X).
$$

**The conditional independence assumption is visible in that product** — each
$\pi_t$ depends on $X$ alone, never on $\pi_{<t}$. Everything CTC gives up follows
from it.

**The forward-backward recursion.** Let $\mathbf{y}'$ be $\mathbf{y}$ with blanks
inserted between every symbol and at both ends, so $|\mathbf{y}'| = 2U+1$. Define
$\alpha_t(s)$ as the total probability of all paths reaching position $s$ of
$\mathbf{y}'$ by frame $t$:

$$
\alpha_t(s) = p\big(\mathbf{y}'_s \mid X_t\big)\cdot
\begin{cases}
\alpha_{t-1}(s) + \alpha_{t-1}(s-1) & \text{if } \mathbf{y}'_s = \varnothing \text{ or } \mathbf{y}'_s = \mathbf{y}'_{s-2}\\[4pt]
\alpha_{t-1}(s) + \alpha_{t-1}(s-1) + \alpha_{t-1}(s-2) & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

The three-term case allows skipping a blank between *distinct* symbols; the
two-term case forbids skipping when it would merge a genuine double letter. That
condition is the entire reason the blank exists.

Cost is $O(TU)$ rather than exponential, and the whole recursion is differentiable,
so it trains by ordinary backpropagation. Final probability:

$$
p(\mathbf{y}\mid X) = \alpha_T(2U+1) + \alpha_T(2U).
$$

## RNN-T

The lattice becomes two-dimensional: a path moves right (consume a frame) or up
(emit a token). Define

$$
p(k \mid t, u) = \operatorname{softmax}\big(J(f_t,\, g_u)\big),
$$

with $f_t$ the encoder output at frame $t$, $g_u$ the prediction network's state
after $u$ emitted tokens, and $J$ the joint network. The alphabet includes blank,
which advances $t$.

$$
\alpha(t,u) = \alpha(t-1,u)\,p(\varnothing \mid t-1, u) \;+\; \alpha(t,u-1)\,p(y_u \mid t, u-1).
$$

**The key difference from CTC** is the dependence on $u$ — the distribution is
conditioned on how many tokens have already been emitted, and through $g_u$ on
*which* ones. That is an internal language model, and it is why RNN-T does not need
an external one. Cost is $O(TU)$ in both time and memory, which is a real training
burden at long utterances.

## Neural codecs

Residual vector quantisation. A single [VQ-VAE](/encoders/neural-encoders/)
codebook of size $K$ gives $\log_2 K$ bits per frame — far too few for audio. RVQ
stacks $N_q$ quantisers, each encoding the *residual* left by the previous:

$$
r_0 = z, \qquad q_i = \operatorname{VQ}_i(r_{i-1}), \qquad r_i = r_{i-1} - q_i, \qquad \hat z = \sum_{i=1}^{N_q} q_i .
$$

Bitrate is

$$
B = f_{\text{frame}} \cdot N_q \cdot \log_2 K \quad \text{bits/s}.
$$

For $f_{\text{frame}} = 75$ Hz, $N_q = 8$, $K = 1024$: $75 \times 8 \times 10 = 6$
kbps — roughly 250× compression from 16-bit 48 kHz audio, at quality competitive
with classical codecs at much higher rates.

**Quantiser dropout** during training makes the codec *scalable*: randomly use only
the first $n \leq N_q$ quantisers, so a single trained model supports a range of
bitrates by truncating the stack. Elegant, and it is why one EnCodec checkpoint
serves multiple operating points.

The training objective combines reconstruction in time and frequency with
adversarial and feature-matching terms:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_t\|x - \hat x\|_1 + \lambda_f\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{spec}} + \lambda_g\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{GAN}} + \lambda_{\text{feat}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{feat}} + \lambda_q\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{VQ}} .
$$

**The adversarial term is not optional.** $\ell_1$ or spectral losses alone produce
audible artefacts — the same blurring problem VAEs have with images, transposed to
audio. The discriminator supplies the perceptual criterion that reconstruction
losses lack.

## TTS as language modelling

Once audio is tokens, text-to-speech is sequence-to-sequence over discrete symbols.
VALL-E models

$$
p\big(c_{1:T}^{(1:N_q)} \mid \mathbf{x},\ \tilde c_{1:T'}^{(1:N_q)}\big),
$$

with $\mathbf{x}$ the phoneme sequence and $\tilde c$ codec tokens from a short
reference recording.

The two-stage structure follows from RVQ's hierarchy. The first quantiser carries
most of the semantic content, so it is modelled **autoregressively**:

$$
p\big(c_{1:T}^{(1)}\big) = \prod_{t} p\big(c_t^{(1)} \mid c_{<t}^{(1)},\, \mathbf{x},\, \tilde c\big).
$$

Quantisers $2\ldots N_q$ carry acoustic detail and are generated
**non-autoregressively**, conditioned on the first — which is far faster and loses
little, because the residuals are much less structured.

**Why three seconds of reference suffices.** The reference tokens are simply a
prefix in the context. Continuing in that voice is in-context learning, exactly as
a language model continues a style from a few sentences. No speaker embedding, no
fine-tuning — which is both the impressive part and the reason voice cloning
became trivially accessible.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**ASR is close to solved for clean speech in high-resource languages, and not for
the rest.** Accented speech, spontaneous conversation, overlapping speakers, code
switching, and low-resource languages all remain substantially worse, and the gap
between benchmark WER and field performance is large.

**Audio language models unified generation.** Once speech, music and sound effects
are all token sequences, one architecture handles all of them. AudioLM established
the semantic-plus-acoustic token hierarchy that most systems still follow.

**End-to-end speech-to-speech is displacing the pipeline.** The classical route —
ASR, then a text model, then TTS — loses prosody, emotion and timing at every
boundary, and adds latency. Models operating directly on audio tokens throughout
preserve those and respond faster, which matters for conversational systems.

**Voice cloning from seconds of audio is a solved capability and an unsolved
problem.** The technical achievement is real; the misuse potential is obvious.
Watermarking and detection are active and not reliable.

## Open problems

- **Speaker separation in real conditions.** The cocktail party problem is
  substantially improved and not solved. Overlapping speech remains hard.

- **Long-form consistency.** Generating minutes of coherent audio with stable voice
  and prosody drifts. Same class of problem as long-horizon anything.

- **Low-resource languages.** Most of the world's languages have almost no
  transcribed audio. Self-supervised pretraining helps and does not close the gap.

- **Evaluation is expensive and subjective.** Mean Opinion Score requires human
  listeners. Automatic metrics correlate poorly with perceived quality, so progress
  in synthesis is measured slowly and unreliably.

- **Detecting synthetic speech.** As quality improves, distinguishing real from
  generated becomes harder. This is a security problem with no robust solution.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Graves et al. (2006), CTC** — the forward-backward derivation. It is the same
   dynamic program as HMM training and worth working through once.
2. **Gulati et al. (2020), Conformer** — the ablations on convolution-plus-attention
   are a clean argument for domain priors.
3. **Baevski et al. (2020), wav2vec 2.0** — for the ten-minutes-of-labels result.
4. **Zeghidour et al. (2021), SoundStream** — residual vector quantisation and
   quantiser dropout, which underpin everything in audio generation since.
