<Abstract>
For three years the forecasting literature published increasingly elaborate
transformer variants, each beating the last. Then a 2022 paper showed a **single
linear layer** matched or beat all of them on the standard benchmarks. That
result is worth studying not as a curiosity but as a case study in how a
subfield can optimise itself into a corner — and in what a good baseline is
actually for.
</Abstract>

<KeyPoints>

- **DLinear embarrassed three years of work.** One linear layer mapping the input window directly to the forecast window matched elaborate transformers on the standard long-horizon benchmarks. Those benchmarks are dominated by trend and seasonality, which a linear map captures perfectly.
- **The lesson is about baselines, not about transformers.** Nobody had checked a trivially simple model. When a linear layer wins, either the task is easy or the benchmark is uninformative — and here it was both.
- **Patching fixed the real problem.** Treating individual timesteps as tokens is wasteful: one point carries almost no information, and attention over thousands of them is mostly noise. PatchTST groups timesteps into patches and beats the linear baseline properly.
- **Classical methods remain hard to beat.** The M4 winner was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and an RNN, and in M5 simple statistical baselines beat most sophisticated entries. On small, noisy, short series, statistical methods often win.

</KeyPoints>

## What makes forecasting different

Predicting the next word and predicting next month's sales look similar and are
not.

**The data is thin.** A language model trains on trillions of tokens. A weekly
sales series with five years of history has 260 points. There is very little to
learn from.

**The signal is mostly structure.** Most series are a trend, plus a seasonal
pattern, plus noise. Once you have captured those three, there is often nothing
left — and all three are simple.

**Extrapolation is required, not interpolation.** A classifier sees test data drawn
from the training distribution. A forecaster must predict a future that is
*definitionally* outside its training range, and the world may have changed.

**Uncertainty is the product.** "Sales will be 1,000" is much less useful than
"between 800 and 1,200 with 90% confidence", because the decision downstream —
how much inventory to hold — depends on the spread.

## The linear model result

Between 2020 and 2022 a stream of papers proposed transformers for long-horizon
forecasting, each reporting improvements over the last.

Zeng et al. then tried the simplest thing anyone could: **one linear layer** mapping
the input window straight to the output window. No attention, no encoder, no
decoder. Optionally decompose into trend and seasonal components first and use two
linear layers.

It matched or beat every transformer on the standard benchmarks.

<Aside type="danger" title="What went wrong, and it generalises">
Nobody had run the trivial baseline. Three years of comparisons were made against
each other rather than against a floor — so the field measured relative progress
within a set of methods that were all, it turned out, above the floor by very
little.

The transferable lesson: **your baseline should be embarrassingly simple, and you
should run it before anything else.** If it wins, you have learned something
important about the task. This failure mode is not specific to forecasting.
</Aside>

## Why the linear model does so well

Not because linearity is right. Because those benchmarks are easy.

Electricity, traffic and weather series are dominated by strong daily and weekly
periodicity plus a slow trend. A linear map from a long input window to the output
window can represent exactly that — a weighted average over the window with weights
learned to pick out the periodic structure.

Attention adds the capacity to model *irregular, content-dependent* dependencies.
If the series contains none, the capacity is unused and its parameters are pure
variance on a dataset of a few thousand points.

## The two ideas in PatchTST

<Steps>

1. **Patching.** Group $P$ consecutive timesteps into one token. Sequence length
   falls by $P$, attention cost by $P^2$, and each token now represents a local
   *shape* rather than a single value.

2. **Channel independence.** Process each variate with the same shared model rather
   than mixing them in the embedding. Counter-intuitive — surely cross-series
   information helps — but mixing early lets one noisy series contaminate the
   others, and sharing weights across variates multiplies the effective training
   data.

</Steps>

Together these beat DLinear properly, which restored the transformer position on a
sounder footing than the earlier papers had.

## The classical baselines you should run

| Method | When it wins |
|---|---|
| **Naive / seasonal naive** | the floor. Always run it. Surprisingly hard to beat on noisy series |
| **ETS (exponential smoothing)** | short series with clear trend and seasonality |
| **ARIMA** | stationary series with autocorrelation structure |
| **Theta** | won M3; still competitive and nearly parameter-free |
| **Prophet** | business series with holidays and changepoints; interpretable, not especially accurate |
| **Gradient boosting on lag features** | dominant in M5 and in most Kaggle forecasting competitions |

That last row deserves emphasis. For tabular-style forecasting with covariates,
**gradient-boosted trees on engineered lag and calendar features** remain extremely
strong, and they were the backbone of most top M5 entries.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the M competitions established">
M4's winner was a *hybrid* — exponential smoothing for the structure, an RNN for
the residual — not a pure neural model. In M5, simple statistical benchmarks beat a
large fraction of sophisticated submissions. The consistent finding across these
competitions is that **combining methods beats any single method**, and that
complexity is not rewarded on its own.
</Aside>

## The problem

Given history $\mathbf{y}_{1:T} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M}$ for $M$ variates,
predict $\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}$. Point forecasting minimises

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\big\|\hat y_{T+h} - y_{T+h}\big\|^2 ,
$$

but the useful output is usually a *distribution*. DeepAR parameterises a
likelihood — Gaussian for real-valued data, negative binomial for counts — and
maximises

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t}\log p\big(y_t \mid \theta(h_t)\big),
$$

with $h_t$ an RNN state. Sampling forward through the recursion yields prediction
intervals rather than a single line.

**Why negative binomial for counts.** Demand data is non-negative, integer, and
over-dispersed — the variance exceeds the mean, which a Poisson cannot represent.
The negative binomial has a separate dispersion parameter and can. Using a Gaussian
on count data produces negative forecasts and badly calibrated intervals, and this
is a common error.

## DLinear

The model that started the argument, in full:

$$
\hat y_{T+1:T+H} = W\, y_{T-L+1:T} + b, \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times L}.
$$

That is the entire architecture. The decomposition variant splits first:

$$
y^{\text{trend}} = \operatorname{AvgPool}_k(y), \qquad y^{\text{seas}} = y - y^{\text{trend}},
$$

$$
\hat y = W_t\, y^{\text{trend}} + W_s\, y^{\text{seas}} .
$$

**Why this suffices on the standard benchmarks.** If a series is exactly periodic
with period $p$ and $L \geq p$, then setting

$$
W_{h,\,l} = \mathbb{1}\{\,l \equiv (T + h) \bmod p\,\}
$$

reproduces the seasonal pattern exactly. A linear map can *perfectly* express
seasonal repetition given a long enough window. Add a slowly varying trend, which
is also linear over a short horizon, and you have captured most of what the ETTh,
Electricity and Traffic datasets contain.

The transformers were not failing to model something hard. There was little hard
structure to model.

## Patching, quantified

Embedding a patch of $P$ timesteps:

$$
x_i = W_p\, y_{(i-1)P+1\,:\,iP} + \text{pos}_i, \qquad W_p \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times P},
$$

giving $N = \lceil L/P \rceil$ tokens instead of $L$. Attention cost:

$$
O(L^2 d) \;\longrightarrow\; O\!\left(\frac{L^2}{P^2}\,d\right).
$$

For $L = 512$, $P = 16$: a 256-fold reduction, which is what makes long input
windows affordable — and long windows are exactly what a forecaster needs to see
multiple seasonal cycles.

**Channel independence, formally.** Rather than $f: \mathbb{R}^{L\times M} \to
\mathbb{R}^{H\times M}$, learn $f: \mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{H}$ applied to each
variate:

$$
\hat y^{(m)} = f_\theta\big(y^{(m)}\big), \qquad m = 1,\dots,M,
$$

with $\theta$ **shared**. Two consequences: the parameter count no longer grows
with $M$, and the effective training set is $M$ times larger. Cross-series
information is lost, and on the standard benchmarks that loss is smaller than the
variance reduction gained — which is a statement about those datasets, not a
general law.

## Scale and evaluation

Series live on wildly different scales, so raw error is meaningless across a
dataset. Two standard fixes.

**Instance normalisation.** Normalise each input window by its own mean and
variance, forecast, then invert:

$$
\tilde y = \frac{y - \mu_{\text{win}}}{\sigma_{\text{win}}}, \qquad \hat y = \sigma_{\text{win}}\,\hat{\tilde y} + \mu_{\text{win}} .
$$

This also handles distribution shift between train and test, which is endemic in
forecasting. Note that DLinear's strong results depend partly on this — it is doing
real work.

**Scaled error metrics.** MASE scales by the in-sample naive forecast error:

$$
\text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h}|y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h}|}{\frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|y_t - y_{t-m}|},
$$

with $m$ the seasonal period. **MASE < 1 means you beat seasonal naive**, which is
the only comparison that means anything across heterogeneous series.

Avoid MAPE. It is undefined at zero, unbounded when actuals are small, and
asymmetric — it penalises over-forecasting more than under-forecasting, which
silently biases any model tuned against it.

For probabilistic forecasts use the **pinball loss** at quantile $q$:

$$
L_q(y,\hat y) = \max\big(q(y - \hat y),\ (q-1)(y - \hat y)\big),
$$

which is minimised exactly at the true $q$-quantile — a proper scoring rule, so it
cannot be gamed by mis-stating your uncertainty.

## Where this stands, as of 2026

**Forecasting foundation models are real and their advantage is zero-shot, not
peak accuracy.** Chronos tokenises series values and trains a language model on
them; TimesFM is a decoder-only model pretrained on a large time-series corpus.
Both forecast unseen series with no training at all, and both are typically
matched or beaten by a well-tuned model fitted to your specific series. **The value
is in not having to fit anything**, which matters enormously when you have ten
thousand series and no time.

**Chronos's approach is worth noting for what it implies.** Quantising real values
into a fixed vocabulary and running a standard language model works — which
suggests much of forecasting is pattern completion over discretised shapes rather
than anything numerically special.

**The benchmark problem is acknowledged but not fixed.** The standard long-horizon
datasets remain in use despite the DLinear result showing they cannot discriminate
between methods. New benchmarks exist and adoption is slow.

**Gradient boosting still wins on tabular-style forecasting.** For demand
forecasting with rich covariates — promotions, prices, calendars — LightGBM on
engineered features remains the practical default, and this has not changed since
M5.

## Open problems

- **Cold start.** A new product has no history. Hierarchical and cross-series
  methods help; nothing works well.

- **Distribution shift is the norm.** A pandemic, a price change, a competitor
  entering — all break the assumption that the future resembles the past. Detecting
  a regime change from a short window is close to impossible.

- **Hierarchical coherence.** Forecasts at product, category and region level should
  sum consistently. Reconciliation methods exist and reconciling *probabilistic*
  forecasts coherently is unsolved.

- **Interpretability is genuinely demanded here.** Forecasts drive decisions people
  must justify. "The model said so" is often not acceptable, which keeps simple
  methods in production regardless of accuracy.

- **Evaluation windows are too short.** Reporting on one test split conflates model
  quality with which period you happened to hold out. Rolling-origin evaluation is
  correct and under-used.

## What to read next, in order

1. **Zeng et al. (2022), DLinear** — read it as methodology criticism. Short, and
   the lesson about baselines transfers everywhere.
2. **Nie et al. (2022), PatchTST** — patching and channel independence, and the
   proper response to the criticism.
3. **Makridakis et al. (2020), M4** — for what a large, honest empirical comparison
   looks like, and the finding that hybrids beat pure methods.
4. **Ansari et al. (2024), Chronos** — for the tokenise-and-language-model approach
   and what it implies about the problem.
