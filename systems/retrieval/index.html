Retrieval and RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation looks like a solved problem — embed documents, search by cosine similarity, paste the results into the prompt. In practice most RAG systems fail quietly: they retrieve plausible-looking passages that do not contain the answer, and the model produces a fluent response anyway. Almost every real improvement comes from fixing retrieval, not from a better generator.

What you should walk away with

  • Chunking is the highest-leverage decision and gets the least attention. If the answer spans a chunk boundary, no retriever can find it, and no amount of reranking or prompting recovers it.
  • Dense and sparse retrieval fail on different queries. Embeddings handle paraphrase and miss exact identifiers; BM25 does the reverse. Hybrid search is not a hedge — it covers two genuinely disjoint failure sets.
  • Retrieve broadly, then rerank. A bi-encoder scores query and document independently, which is fast and lossy. A cross-encoder reads them together, which is accurate and slow. Use the first for recall, the second for precision.
  • Position in the prompt changes whether the model uses a passage. Models attend most to the beginning and end of their context and reliably under-use the middle — so retrieval order is part of the system, not a cosmetic detail.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Why not just fine-tune it in

Section titled “Why not just fine-tune it in”

The instinct when a model does not know your documents is to train it on them. This is usually the wrong tool, for three reasons worth stating plainly.

Knowledge does not stick reliably. Fine-tuning is excellent at changing behaviour and unreliable at installing facts — the model may reproduce a fact in the training format and fail to use it in conversation.

Updates are expensive. A changed document means retraining. Retrieval means replacing a row.

There is no citation. A fine-tuned model cannot tell you where an answer came from. A retrieval system hands you the passage, which for most real applications is the entire point.

So: if you want the model to know something, retrieve it. If you want the model to behave differently, fine-tune it.

The pipeline in one paragraph

Section titled “The pipeline in one paragraph”

Split your documents into chunks. Convert each chunk into a vector that captures its meaning. When a question arrives, convert it into a vector too, find the chunks whose vectors are closest, paste them into the prompt, and ask the model to answer using them.

Every step in that sentence is a place where the system can silently fail, and the rest of this page is about which failures matter.

The failure that defines the field

Section titled “The failure that defines the field”

A user asks a question. The system retrieves three passages that are topically related but do not contain the answer. The model, given three relevant-looking passages and a question, writes a confident answer anyway — synthesising something plausible from adjacent facts.

The output looks good. It has citations. It is wrong.

Why this is worse than a plain hallucination

An unaided model that does not know something often hedges. A RAG system supplies context that looks authoritative, which both suppresses the hedging and gives the user documents to point at. Retrieval failures produce more confident wrong answers than no retrieval at all, which is why measuring retrieval quality separately from answer quality is not optional.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The pipeline, and where each stage fails

Section titled “The pipeline, and where each stage fails”

Chunking

Section titled “Chunking”

The decision that determines the ceiling on everything downstream.

  1. Fixed-size — split every N tokens with overlap. Simple, and it cuts sentences and tables in half.

  2. Recursive structural — split on paragraph, then sentence, then word, taking the largest unit that fits. The sane default.

  3. Semantic — embed sentences and split where consecutive similarity drops. Better boundaries, more expensive, and it needs tuning.

  4. Document-aware — split on the document’s own structure: markdown headings, HTML sections, function definitions in code. Usually the best option when the structure exists, and routinely skipped because it needs per-format work.

Two adjustments that pay for themselves:

  • Overlap (10–20%) so a boundary does not orphan a sentence.
  • Contextual headers — prepend the document title and section path to each chunk before embedding. A chunk reading “The limit is 40 hours” is useless; the same chunk prefixed with “Employment Policy › Overtime” is retrievable.

They fail on disjoint sets of queries, which is why hybrid retrieval is standard rather than a compromise. Dense embeddings map a rare identifier to whatever region of the space its subword pieces suggest — which is nowhere useful. BM25 finds it because it is a literal string match.

Fusion is usually Reciprocal Rank Fusion, which combines rankings rather than scores and so needs no score normalisation between two systems whose scores are not comparable.

Reranking

Section titled “Reranking”

Retrieval and reranking use fundamentally different architectures, and the difference is the whole reason for the two-stage design.

Bi-encoder (retrieval). Query and document are embedded separately. Document vectors can be computed offline and indexed, so search is an approximate nearest-neighbour lookup over millions of items in milliseconds. The cost is that the model never sees query and document together, so it cannot reason about their interaction.

Cross-encoder (reranking). Query and document are concatenated and run through a transformer together, with full attention between them. Far more accurate, and it requires one forward pass per pair — impossible over a whole corpus, fine over 50 candidates.

ColBERT sits between them: encode separately into per-token vectors, then score by summing each query token’s maximum similarity over document tokens. Late interaction retains some cross-attention benefit while keeping documents pre-computable. More accurate than a bi-encoder, more storage-hungry.

The retrieve-wide-then-rerank rule

Retrieve 50–100 candidates cheaply, rerank to 3–5 accurately. This is almost always better than retrieving 5 directly, and the reason is asymmetric: the reranker can only fix ordering within what retrieval returned. Recall at stage one is a hard ceiling on the whole system.

Lost in the middle

Section titled “Lost in the middle”

Models do not attend uniformly across their context. Liu et al. found a pronounced U-shape: information at the start or end of the context is used reliably; information in the middle is often ignored, even when it is exactly the answer and the context window is nowhere near full.

Two direct consequences for system design:

  • Order retrieved passages so the highest-scoring ones sit at the edges — best first, second-best last, weakest buried in the middle.
  • More context is not better. Stuffing 20 passages in because the window allows it measurably degrades accuracy relative to 5 well-chosen ones.

This is InfoNCE with documents as the contrastive set. Two practical points that determine whether a retriever is good:

Hard negatives dominate quality. Random negatives are trivially separable and teach almost nothing. DPR’s central finding was that mining negatives from BM25’s top results — documents that look relevant and are not — improves retrieval far more than architectural changes.

In-batch negatives make batch size a quality parameter. Each batch supplies n−1n-1n−1 free negatives, so larger batches mean a harder and more informative contrastive task.

The key property is that RRF uses only ranks, never scores. BM25 scores are unbounded sums; cosine similarities live in [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1]. Combining them numerically requires a normalisation nobody can justify. Ranks are directly comparable.

The constant kkk damps the influence of the very top positions — without it, a single system’s first result would dominate any consensus among the others.

Approximate nearest neighbours

Section titled “Approximate nearest neighbours”

Exact search over NNN vectors is O(Nd)O(Nd)O(Nd) per query — unusable at scale. HNSW builds a multi-layer proximity graph: upper layers are sparse, giving long-range hops; lower layers are dense, giving local refinement. Search descends greedily from the top.

Expected complexity is O(log⁡N)O(\log N)O(logN), with a recall/latency knob (efSearch).

The trade you are making is silent. ANN returns approximate neighbours — recall of 95–99% is typical and considered fine. That means 1–5% of the time the true best passage is simply not in your candidate set, and nothing downstream can tell. When debugging a retrieval failure, check exact search on the failing query before blaming the embedding model.

Why cross-encoders win

Section titled “Why cross-encoders win”

A bi-encoder computes s(q,d)=⟨Eq(q),Ed(d)⟩s(q,d) = \langle E_q(q), E_d(d)\rangles(q,d)=⟨Eq​(q),Ed​(d)⟩. The document representation is fixed before the query is known, so it must compress the document into a single vector adequate for every possible query.

A cross-encoder computes s(q,d)=f([q;d])s(q,d) = f([q; d])s(q,d)=f([q;d]) with full attention across the pair, so it can condition the document’s representation on the query. That is strictly more expressive: the bi-encoder’s scoring function is a bilinear form in fixed embeddings, and the cross-encoder’s is an arbitrary function of the pair.

The cost is combinatorial. Bi-encoder: NNN document encodings offline, one query encoding online. Cross-encoder: NNN forward passes online. Hence the split.

with per-token embeddings. The max⁡\maxmax over document tokens is a soft term-matching operation, and because document tokens are still encoded independently they can be indexed offline. Storage grows by roughly the token count per document, which is the price.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Long context did not kill RAG, and the reason is cost, not capability. Models with million-token windows can in principle hold your corpus. In practice attention cost, KV-cache memory, and the lost-in-the-middle effect all mean that retrieving 5 relevant chunks beats stuffing 500 — and it is orders of magnitude cheaper per query. The two are complements: better retrieval feeds better long-context reasoning.

Hybrid retrieval with reranking is the settled default. Dense-only systems underperform on exact identifiers and rare terminology often enough that pure vector search is now considered a beginner’s configuration.

Embedding model choice matters less than people think, above a threshold. MTEB leaderboard differences of a point or two rarely survive contact with a specific domain. Chunking strategy and reranking produce larger gains than swapping a top-20 embedding model for a top-5 one.

Agentic retrieval is the active direction. Rather than one retrieval pass, the model decides whether to retrieve, issues multiple reformulated queries, reads results, and retrieves again. Self-RAG formalised the “should I retrieve, and is this passage actually relevant” decision as learned reflection tokens. It costs more per query and handles multi-hop questions that single-pass RAG cannot.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Evaluation is genuinely hard. End-to-end answer quality conflates retrieval and generation failures. Measuring retrieval alone needs relevance labels, which are expensive and subjective. Most deployed systems are not measured properly.

  • Multi-hop questions defeat single-pass retrieval. “Which of our vendors in the EU has a contract expiring before the new regulation takes effect” requires chaining. One embedding lookup cannot do it.

  • Negation and temporal qualifiers are poorly handled. Embeddings of “contracts that include an indemnity clause” and “contracts that exclude an indemnity clause” are close together. This is a known and unsolved weakness of the bi-encoder formulation.

  • No principled chunking method. It remains hand-tuned per corpus, and it is the highest-leverage parameter in the system.

  • Freshness and invalidation. When a document changes, its chunks, embeddings, and any cached answers all go stale. Most systems handle this by re-indexing everything on a schedule and hoping.
