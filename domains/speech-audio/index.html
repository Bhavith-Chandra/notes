Speech and Audio

Speech recognition has a structural problem text does not: one second of audio is a hundred frames and perhaps three characters, and nobody labelled which frames belong to which character. CTC solved that alignment problem in 2006 by summing over all possible alignments — and the same idea, applied in reverse through neural codecs, is why text-to-speech is now a language modelling task.

What you should walk away with

  • Alignment is the defining difficulty. Audio frames vastly outnumber output tokens and the correspondence is unlabelled. CTC introduces a blank symbol and marginalises over every alignment consistent with the transcript, computed efficiently by dynamic programming.
  • CTC assumes conditional independence between outputs; RNN-T does not. That single difference is why RNN-T dominates streaming production ASR — it has an internal language model, CTC needs an external one.
  • Neural codecs turned audio into tokens, and that changed generation. Once a second of speech is 75 discrete tokens, generating audio becomes next-token prediction. VALL-E clones a voice from three seconds of reference audio on that basis.
  • Weak supervision beat clever self-supervision for ASR. Whisper trained on 680k hours of noisy web audio-transcript pairs and generalises better across domains than models with lower benchmark WER. Scale and diversity beat pretraining sophistication here.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The alignment problem

Section titled “The alignment problem”

You have a one-second recording of someone saying “cat”. At a typical frame rate that is about a hundred feature frames, and three characters of output.

Which frames are the “c”? Where does “a” begin? Nobody labelled it, and labelling it by hand for thousands of hours is out of the question.

Before 2006 this was handled by iterative forced alignment inside an HMM pipeline — guess an alignment, train, re-align, repeat. It worked and it was elaborate.

CTC’s answer is to refuse to choose. Instead of picking one alignment, sum the probability over all alignments that produce the right transcript. Add a blank symbol meaning “no output here”, allow repeats, and define a collapsing rule: merge repeated characters, then drop blanks. c-aa-t and cc-a-tt both collapse to cat.

There are exponentially many valid alignments, and dynamic programming sums them in polynomial time — the same trick as the forward algorithm for HMMs.

What CTC gives up

Section titled “What CTC gives up”

CTC makes one strong assumption: given the audio, each output is independent of the others.

This is clearly false. After “cat sat on the ma” the next character is overwhelmingly “t”, and CTC’s model structure cannot express that — it looks only at the audio at each frame.

In practice this shows up as phonetically plausible nonsense: “recognise speech” transcribed as “wreck a nice beach”. Every frame was decoded reasonably; nothing enforced that the sequence be a sentence.

The standard fix is to bolt on an external language model at decoding time. RNN-T’s fix is structural: feed previous outputs back into the model, so it carries its own language model internally. That is why RNN-T, not CTC, runs on your phone.

Why streaming forces the architecture

An offline system can attend over the whole utterance. A live captioning system must emit words as you speak, so it can only look backwards. This rules out bidirectional encoders and full attention-based encoder–decoders, and it is the main reason RNN-T dominates production ASR while Whisper-style models dominate offline transcription.

Audio as tokens

Section titled “Audio as tokens”

The other half of this page is generation, and one idea unlocked it.

A neural codec compresses audio into a sequence of discrete tokens — around 75 tokens per second — from which a decoder reconstructs near-original quality. This is VQ-VAE applied to waveforms, with a residual refinement so that successive codebooks encode progressively finer detail.

Once audio is a token sequence, generating it is next-token prediction. Everything built for language models transfers. VALL-E’s demonstration was striking: given three seconds of someone’s voice as a prompt, continue in that voice saying arbitrary new text — voice cloning as in-context learning.

The three ASR architectures

Section titled “The three ASR architectures”
  • CTC
  • RNN-T
  • Attention encoder–decoder
  • Conformer

Encoder only. Per-frame distribution over characters plus blank, trained by summing over alignments.

Fast — one forward pass, all frames decoded in parallel. Streamable with a causal encoder. Weakest language modelling, because of the independence assumption.

Still widely used as an auxiliary loss even in systems that decode differently: it provides a strong per-frame training signal that stabilises the encoder.

Adds a prediction network — an autoregressive model over the output so far — and a joint network combining it with the encoder at each frame.

The output distribution is now conditioned on previous outputs, so the model has an internal language model. It remains streamable, because at each step it either emits a token or advances to the next frame, and both decisions depend only on past audio.

The dominant production architecture for streaming ASR. More complex to train — the lattice is two-dimensional rather than one — and worth it.

A standard encoder–decoder: encode the whole utterance, decode text with cross-attention. Whisper is this.

No alignment machinery at all — attention learns the correspondence implicitly. Best offline accuracy, and not streamable, since the encoder needs the full utterance.

A known failure mode: because the decoder is a strong language model, it can “hallucinate” fluent text during silence or noise, generating a plausible sentence with no acoustic support. CTC and RNN-T are structurally less prone to this.

The encoder, not the loss — usable with any of the above.

Self-attention captures long-range dependencies; convolution captures local patterns like formant transitions. Speech needs both, and interleaving them beats either alone. The Conformer block sandwiches a convolution module between two feed-forward halves with attention in the middle.

The default speech encoder, and one of the clearest cases where a domain-specific architectural prior genuinely pays.

Self-supervised speech

Section titled “Self-supervised speech”

Labelled speech is expensive; raw audio is abundant. The same logic as elsewhere in self-supervised learning.

wav2vec 2.0 masks spans of latent speech representations and solves a contrastive task: identify the true quantised latent among distractors. Its result was striking — competitive ASR from ten minutes of labelled data plus large-scale pretraining.

HuBERT replaces the contrastive objective with masked prediction of cluster assignments. Cluster the features (initially MFCCs, then the model’s own representations), then predict the cluster ID of masked frames — BERT for speech, with the clustering supplying the vocabulary. Iterating the clustering with better representations is the key trick.

Whisper went the other way: no self-supervision, just 680,000 hours of weakly supervised web audio-transcript pairs. Its benchmark WER on clean read speech is not state of the art; its robustness across accents, noise, and domains is far better, and it does multilingual recognition and translation in one model.

What Whisper's result actually shows

It is easy to read this as “scale beats method”. The more precise claim is that training distribution diversity beats benchmark optimisation. Models tuned on LibriSpeech achieve better LibriSpeech WER and degrade on anything else. Whisper’s messy web-scale data covers the variation real audio contains. Which you want depends on whether your deployment resembles your benchmark.

RNN-T

Section titled “RNN-T”

The lattice becomes two-dimensional: a path moves right (consume a frame) or up (emit a token). Define

p(k∣t,u)=softmax⁡(J(ft, gu)),p(k \mid t, u) = \operatorname{softmax}\big(J(f_t,\, g_u)\big),p(k∣t,u)=softmax(J(ft​,gu​)),

with ftf_tft​ the encoder output at frame ttt, gug_ugu​ the prediction network’s state after uuu emitted tokens, and JJJ the joint network. The alphabet includes blank, which advances ttt.

α(t,u)=α(t−1,u) p(∅∣t−1,u)  +  α(t,u−1) p(yu∣t,u−1).\alpha(t,u) = \alpha(t-1,u)\,p(\varnothing \mid t-1, u) \;+\; \alpha(t,u-1)\,p(y_u \mid t, u-1).α(t,u)=α(t−1,u)p(∅∣t−1,u)+α(t,u−1)p(yu​∣t,u−1).

The key difference from CTC is the dependence on uuu — the distribution is conditioned on how many tokens have already been emitted, and through gug_ugu​ on which ones. That is an internal language model, and it is why RNN-T does not need an external one. Cost is O(TU)O(TU)O(TU) in both time and memory, which is a real training burden at long utterances.

The adversarial term is not optional. ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ or spectral losses alone produce audible artefacts — the same blurring problem VAEs have with images, transposed to audio. The discriminator supplies the perceptual criterion that reconstruction losses lack.

TTS as language modelling

Section titled “TTS as language modelling”

Once audio is tokens, text-to-speech is sequence-to-sequence over discrete symbols. VALL-E models

p(c1:T(1:Nq)∣x, c~1:T′(1:Nq)),p\big(c_{1:T}^{(1:N_q)} \mid \mathbf{x},\ \tilde c_{1:T'}^{(1:N_q)}\big),p(c1:T(1:Nq​)​∣x, c~1:T′(1:Nq​)​),

with x\mathbf{x}x the phoneme sequence and c~\tilde cc~ codec tokens from a short reference recording.

The two-stage structure follows from RVQ’s hierarchy. The first quantiser carries most of the semantic content, so it is modelled autoregressively:

p(c1:T(1))=∏tp(ct(1)∣c<t(1), x, c~).p\big(c_{1:T}^{(1)}\big) = \prod_{t} p\big(c_t^{(1)} \mid c_{<t}^{(1)},\, \mathbf{x},\, \tilde c\big).p(c1:T(1)​)=t∏​p(ct(1)​∣c<t(1)​,x,c~).

Quantisers 2…Nq2\ldots N_q2…Nq​ carry acoustic detail and are generated non-autoregressively, conditioned on the first — which is far faster and loses little, because the residuals are much less structured.

Why three seconds of reference suffices. The reference tokens are simply a prefix in the context. Continuing in that voice is in-context learning, exactly as a language model continues a style from a few sentences. No speaker embedding, no fine-tuning — which is both the impressive part and the reason voice cloning became trivially accessible.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

ASR is close to solved for clean speech in high-resource languages, and not for the rest. Accented speech, spontaneous conversation, overlapping speakers, code switching, and low-resource languages all remain substantially worse, and the gap between benchmark WER and field performance is large.

Audio language models unified generation. Once speech, music and sound effects are all token sequences, one architecture handles all of them. AudioLM established the semantic-plus-acoustic token hierarchy that most systems still follow.

End-to-end speech-to-speech is displacing the pipeline. The classical route — ASR, then a text model, then TTS — loses prosody, emotion and timing at every boundary, and adds latency. Models operating directly on audio tokens throughout preserve those and respond faster, which matters for conversational systems.

Voice cloning from seconds of audio is a solved capability and an unsolved problem. The technical achievement is real; the misuse potential is obvious. Watermarking and detection are active and not reliable.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Speaker separation in real conditions. The cocktail party problem is substantially improved and not solved. Overlapping speech remains hard.

  • Long-form consistency. Generating minutes of coherent audio with stable voice and prosody drifts. Same class of problem as long-horizon anything.

  • Low-resource languages. Most of the world’s languages have almost no transcribed audio. Self-supervised pretraining helps and does not close the gap.

  • Evaluation is expensive and subjective. Mean Opinion Score requires human listeners. Automatic metrics correlate poorly with perceived quality, so progress in synthesis is measured slowly and unreliably.

  • Detecting synthetic speech. As quality improves, distinguishing real from generated becomes harder. This is a security problem with no robust solution.
