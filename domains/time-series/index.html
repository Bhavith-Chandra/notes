Time Series Forecasting

For three years the forecasting literature published increasingly elaborate transformer variants, each beating the last. Then a 2022 paper showed a single linear layer matched or beat all of them on the standard benchmarks. That result is worth studying not as a curiosity but as a case study in how a subfield can optimise itself into a corner — and in what a good baseline is actually for.

What you should walk away with

  • DLinear embarrassed three years of work. One linear layer mapping the input window directly to the forecast window matched elaborate transformers on the standard long-horizon benchmarks. Those benchmarks are dominated by trend and seasonality, which a linear map captures perfectly.
  • The lesson is about baselines, not about transformers. Nobody had checked a trivially simple model. When a linear layer wins, either the task is easy or the benchmark is uninformative — and here it was both.
  • Patching fixed the real problem. Treating individual timesteps as tokens is wasteful: one point carries almost no information, and attention over thousands of them is mostly noise. PatchTST groups timesteps into patches and beats the linear baseline properly.
  • Classical methods remain hard to beat. The M4 winner was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and an RNN, and in M5 simple statistical baselines beat most sophisticated entries. On small, noisy, short series, statistical methods often win.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

What makes forecasting different

Section titled “What makes forecasting different”

Predicting the next word and predicting next month’s sales look similar and are not.

The data is thin. A language model trains on trillions of tokens. A weekly sales series with five years of history has 260 points. There is very little to learn from.

The signal is mostly structure. Most series are a trend, plus a seasonal pattern, plus noise. Once you have captured those three, there is often nothing left — and all three are simple.

Extrapolation is required, not interpolation. A classifier sees test data drawn from the training distribution. A forecaster must predict a future that is definitionally outside its training range, and the world may have changed.

Uncertainty is the product. “Sales will be 1,000” is much less useful than “between 800 and 1,200 with 90% confidence”, because the decision downstream — how much inventory to hold — depends on the spread.

The linear model result

Section titled “The linear model result”

Between 2020 and 2022 a stream of papers proposed transformers for long-horizon forecasting, each reporting improvements over the last.

Zeng et al. then tried the simplest thing anyone could: one linear layer mapping the input window straight to the output window. No attention, no encoder, no decoder. Optionally decompose into trend and seasonal components first and use two linear layers.

It matched or beat every transformer on the standard benchmarks.

What went wrong, and it generalises

Nobody had run the trivial baseline. Three years of comparisons were made against each other rather than against a floor — so the field measured relative progress within a set of methods that were all, it turned out, above the floor by very little.

The transferable lesson: your baseline should be embarrassingly simple, and you should run it before anything else. If it wins, you have learned something important about the task. This failure mode is not specific to forecasting.

Why the linear model does so well

Section titled “Why the linear model does so well”

Not because linearity is right. Because those benchmarks are easy.

Electricity, traffic and weather series are dominated by strong daily and weekly periodicity plus a slow trend. A linear map from a long input window to the output window can represent exactly that — a weighted average over the window with weights learned to pick out the periodic structure.

Attention adds the capacity to model irregular, content-dependent dependencies. If the series contains none, the capacity is unused and its parameters are pure variance on a dataset of a few thousand points.

The two ideas in PatchTST

Section titled “The two ideas in PatchTST”

  1. Patching. Group PPP consecutive timesteps into one token. Sequence length falls by PPP, attention cost by P2P^2P2, and each token now represents a local shape rather than a single value.

  2. Channel independence. Process each variate with the same shared model rather than mixing them in the embedding. Counter-intuitive — surely cross-series information helps — but mixing early lets one noisy series contaminate the others, and sharing weights across variates multiplies the effective training data.

Together these beat DLinear properly, which restored the transformer position on a sounder footing than the earlier papers had.

The classical baselines you should run

That last row deserves emphasis. For tabular-style forecasting with covariates, gradient-boosted trees on engineered lag and calendar features remain extremely strong, and they were the backbone of most top M5 entries.

What the M competitions established

M4’s winner was a hybrid — exponential smoothing for the structure, an RNN for the residual — not a pure neural model. In M5, simple statistical benchmarks beat a large fraction of sophisticated submissions. The consistent finding across these competitions is that combining methods beats any single method, and that complexity is not rewarded on its own.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

The problem

Section titled “The problem”

Given history y1:T∈RT×M\mathbf{y}_{1:T} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times M}y1:T​∈RT×M for MMM variates, predict yT+1:T+H\mathbf{y}_{T+1:T+H}yT+1:T+H​. Point forecasting minimises

L=1H∑h=1H∥y^T+h−yT+h∥2,\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\big\|\hat y_{T+h} - y_{T+h}\big\|^2 ,L=H1​h=1∑H​​y^​T+h​−yT+h​​2,

but the useful output is usually a distribution. DeepAR parameterises a likelihood — Gaussian for real-valued data, negative binomial for counts — and maximises

L=∑tlog⁡p(yt∣θ(ht)),\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t}\log p\big(y_t \mid \theta(h_t)\big),L=t∑​logp(yt​∣θ(ht​)),

with hth_tht​ an RNN state. Sampling forward through the recursion yields prediction intervals rather than a single line.

Why negative binomial for counts. Demand data is non-negative, integer, and over-dispersed — the variance exceeds the mean, which a Poisson cannot represent. The negative binomial has a separate dispersion parameter and can. Using a Gaussian on count data produces negative forecasts and badly calibrated intervals, and this is a common error.

DLinear

Section titled “DLinear”

The model that started the argument, in full:

y^T+1:T+H=W yT−L+1:T+b,W∈RH×L.\hat y_{T+1:T+H} = W\, y_{T-L+1:T} + b, \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times L}.y^​T+1:T+H​=WyT−L+1:T​+b,W∈RH×L.

That is the entire architecture. The decomposition variant splits first:

ytrend=AvgPool⁡k(y),yseas=y−ytrend,y^{\text{trend}} = \operatorname{AvgPool}_k(y), \qquad y^{\text{seas}} = y - y^{\text{trend}},ytrend=AvgPoolk​(y),yseas=y−ytrend, y^=Wt ytrend+Ws yseas.\hat y = W_t\, y^{\text{trend}} + W_s\, y^{\text{seas}} .y^​=Wt​ytrend+Ws​yseas.

Why this suffices on the standard benchmarks. If a series is exactly periodic with period ppp and L≥pL \geq pL≥p, then setting

Wh, l=1{ l≡(T+h) mod p }W_{h,\,l} = \mathbb{1}\{\,l \equiv (T + h) \bmod p\,\}Wh,l​=1{l≡(T+h)modp}

reproduces the seasonal pattern exactly. A linear map can perfectly express seasonal repetition given a long enough window. Add a slowly varying trend, which is also linear over a short horizon, and you have captured most of what the ETTh, Electricity and Traffic datasets contain.

The transformers were not failing to model something hard. There was little hard structure to model.

Patching, quantified

Section titled “Patching, quantified”

Embedding a patch of PPP timesteps:

xi=Wp y(i−1)P+1 : iP+posi,Wp∈Rd×P,x_i = W_p\, y_{(i-1)P+1\,:\,iP} + \text{pos}_i, \qquad W_p \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times P},xi​=Wp​y(i−1)P+1:iP​+posi​,Wp​∈Rd×P,

giving N=⌈L/P⌉N = \lceil L/P \rceilN=⌈L/P⌉ tokens instead of LLL. Attention cost:

O(L2d)  ⟶  O ⁣(L2P2 d).O(L^2 d) \;\longrightarrow\; O\!\left(\frac{L^2}{P^2}\,d\right).O(L2d)⟶O(P2L2​d).

For L=512L = 512L=512, P=16P = 16P=16: a 256-fold reduction, which is what makes long input windows affordable — and long windows are exactly what a forecaster needs to see multiple seasonal cycles.

Channel independence, formally. Rather than f:RL×M→RH×Mf: \mathbb{R}^{L\times M} \to \mathbb{R}^{H\times M}f:RL×M→RH×M, learn f:RL→RHf: \mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{H}f:RL→RH applied to each variate:

y^(m)=fθ(y(m)),m=1,…,M,\hat y^{(m)} = f_\theta\big(y^{(m)}\big), \qquad m = 1,\dots,M,y^​(m)=fθ​(y(m)),m=1,…,M,

with θ\thetaθ shared. Two consequences: the parameter count no longer grows with MMM, and the effective training set is MMM times larger. Cross-series information is lost, and on the standard benchmarks that loss is smaller than the variance reduction gained — which is a statement about those datasets, not a general law.

Scale and evaluation

Section titled “Scale and evaluation”

Series live on wildly different scales, so raw error is meaningless across a dataset. Two standard fixes.

Instance normalisation. Normalise each input window by its own mean and variance, forecast, then invert:

y~=y−μwinσwin,y^=σwin y~^+μwin.\tilde y = \frac{y - \mu_{\text{win}}}{\sigma_{\text{win}}}, \qquad \hat y = \sigma_{\text{win}}\,\hat{\tilde y} + \mu_{\text{win}} .y~​=σwin​y−μwin​​,y^​=σwin​y~​^​+μwin​.

This also handles distribution shift between train and test, which is endemic in forecasting. Note that DLinear’s strong results depend partly on this — it is doing real work.

Scaled error metrics. MASE scales by the in-sample naive forecast error:

MASE=1H∑h∣yT+h−y^T+h∣1T−m∑t=m+1T∣yt−yt−m∣,\text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h}|y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h}|}{\frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|y_t - y_{t-m}|},MASE=T−m1​∑t=m+1T​∣yt​−yt−m​∣H1​∑h​∣yT+h​−y^​T+h​∣​,

with mmm the seasonal period. MASE < 1 means you beat seasonal naive, which is the only comparison that means anything across heterogeneous series.

Avoid MAPE. It is undefined at zero, unbounded when actuals are small, and asymmetric — it penalises over-forecasting more than under-forecasting, which silently biases any model tuned against it.

For probabilistic forecasts use the pinball loss at quantile qqq:

Lq(y,y^)=max⁡(q(y−y^), (q−1)(y−y^)),L_q(y,\hat y) = \max\big(q(y - \hat y),\ (q-1)(y - \hat y)\big),Lq​(y,y^​)=max(q(y−y^​), (q−1)(y−y^​)),

which is minimised exactly at the true qqq-quantile — a proper scoring rule, so it cannot be gamed by mis-stating your uncertainty.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Forecasting foundation models are real and their advantage is zero-shot, not peak accuracy. Chronos tokenises series values and trains a language model on them; TimesFM is a decoder-only model pretrained on a large time-series corpus. Both forecast unseen series with no training at all, and both are typically matched or beaten by a well-tuned model fitted to your specific series. The value is in not having to fit anything, which matters enormously when you have ten thousand series and no time.

Chronos’s approach is worth noting for what it implies. Quantising real values into a fixed vocabulary and running a standard language model works — which suggests much of forecasting is pattern completion over discretised shapes rather than anything numerically special.

The benchmark problem is acknowledged but not fixed. The standard long-horizon datasets remain in use despite the DLinear result showing they cannot discriminate between methods. New benchmarks exist and adoption is slow.

Gradient boosting still wins on tabular-style forecasting. For demand forecasting with rich covariates — promotions, prices, calendars — LightGBM on engineered features remains the practical default, and this has not changed since M5.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Cold start. A new product has no history. Hierarchical and cross-series methods help; nothing works well.

  • Distribution shift is the norm. A pandemic, a price change, a competitor entering — all break the assumption that the future resembles the past. Detecting a regime change from a short window is close to impossible.

  • Hierarchical coherence. Forecasts at product, category and region level should sum consistently. Reconciliation methods exist and reconciling probabilistic forecasts coherently is unsolved.

  • Interpretability is genuinely demanded here. Forecasts drive decisions people must justify. “The model said so” is often not acceptable, which keeps simple methods in production regardless of accuracy.

  • Evaluation windows are too short. Reporting on one test split conflates model quality with which period you happened to hold out. Rolling-origin evaluation is correct and under-used.
