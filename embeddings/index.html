Embeddings

An embedding is a map from things to vectors such that geometry means something. That is the entire idea, and everything difficult about embeddings comes from one question the definition leaves open: means what, exactly? A vector space where nearby means “appears in similar contexts” is a different object from one where nearby means “answers this question”, and confusing the two is the most common and most expensive mistake in applied embedding work.

What you should walk away with

  • An embedding is defined by its training objective, not by its architecture. Two models with identical weights-shapes trained on different objectives produce spaces where “similar” means different things. Ask what the objective made close, not what the model is.
  • Similarity is not one thing. Topical similarity, semantic equivalence, question-answer relevance and stylistic similarity are four different relations. Most retrieval failures are a mismatch between the relation you need and the relation the model was trained on.
  • Static and contextual embeddings answer different questions. word2vec gives one vector per word type; a transformer gives one per token occurrence. The first is a lexicon, the second is a reading.
  • Cosine similarity is a convention, not a law. Whether it is the right metric depends on how the model was trained. Using cosine on a model trained with unnormalised dot products silently changes the ranking.
  • The benchmark you optimise decides what you get. MTEB has meaningfully improved the field and is now heavily gamed; a top-of-leaderboard model can be worse than a mid-table one on your actual data.

The idea, and the trap inside it

Section titled “The idea, and the trap inside it”

Suppose you want a computer to know that cat and kitten are related. You could write that down. You would then need to write down every other such fact, forever.

The alternative: assign every word a list of numbers, and arrange those numbers so that related words end up near each other. Now relatedness is a distance computation rather than a lookup, and it generalises to pairs you never considered.

That is an embedding. It works remarkably well and it hides a question that most practical difficulty flows from: near in what sense?

The question that decides everything

Consider four pairs of texts:

  • “How do I reset my password?” / “Password reset instructions” — the same topic.
  • “How do I reset my password?” / “Click Settings, then Security, then Reset” — question and its answer, which share almost no vocabulary.
  • “The film was terrible” / “The film was wonderful” — near-identical form, opposite meaning.
  • “The film was terrible” / “Ce film était horrible” — same meaning, different language.

There is no single embedding space in which all four pairs have the “right” similarity, because the four notions conflict. A model trained on question-answer pairs will place the second pair close; a model trained on paraphrase will place the third pair close because they differ by one word, which is exactly wrong for sentiment.

The similarity your embedding computes is the similarity its training objective rewarded. Nothing else. Choosing an embedding model is choosing which of these relations you want, and that choice cannot be deferred to a leaderboard.

The four questions

Section titled “The four questions”

Any embedding — of words, sentences, images, users, molecules — is characterised by the answers to four questions. Get these straight and most of the field organises itself.

The map

Section titled “The map”

  1. Static word embeddings — one vector per word type, learned from co-occurrence statistics. word2vec, GloVe, fastText. Largely superseded for downstream use, but they are where the geometry was first understood, and the analysis is still the clearest available. See Word Embeddings.

  2. Contextual token embeddings — one vector per occurrence, produced by a transformer. The same word gets different vectors in different sentences. These are the internal representations discussed in Representations and Latent Spaces.

  3. Text embeddings — a single vector for a sentence, passage or document, produced by a model trained specifically for that. SBERT through E5, GTE, BGE and the current LLM-based embedders. This is what people usually mean by “embeddings” in 2026. See Text Embeddings.

  4. Multimodal embeddings — a shared space for images and text, trained on paired data. CLIP and its descendants. See Multimodal Models.

  5. Entity and categorical embeddings — learned vectors for users, products, categories, nodes. See Feature Encoding and Graph Neural Networks.

Where the operational problems live

Section titled “Where the operational problems live”

The theory is comfortable. The operational side is where projects fail, and it gets its own page — Vector Search and Production Embeddings — because the failure modes are specific and repeatable: choosing a dimension without measuring the recall cost, discovering that re-embedding a corpus takes a week, indexes that lose recall silently, and the fact that embeddings of private text are substantially invertible.

Reading path

Section titled “Reading path”

If you want the geometry and the historical understanding, start with Word Embeddings. If you are choosing a model for a retrieval system today, start with Text Embeddings. If you already have a model and need it to work at scale, go to Vector Search.

Adjacent: Neural Encoders for the architectures that produce embeddings, Latent Spaces for the geometry of the spaces themselves, Retrieval for the system that consumes them, and Self-Supervised Learning for the contrastive machinery that trains them.
