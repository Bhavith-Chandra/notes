Compute Budgeting

“How much compute do I need?” has an arithmetic answer, and the arithmetic is not hard. Three numbers — parameters, tokens, and the utilisation you can realistically achieve — determine GPU-hours, and GPU-hours times a rental rate determines dollars. A fourth number, memory, determines whether the job runs at all. This page gives you the formulas, their derivations, the places where they lie, and several fully worked examples you can adapt.

What you should walk away with

  • Training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens. The factor 6 is 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward, and it is derivable in two lines rather than remembered.
  • Memory is a different budget from compute, and it is usually what stops you first. Full fine-tuning with Adam in mixed precision costs about 16 bytes per parameter before you have stored a single activation. A 7B model needs ~112 GB of optimizer-side state — more than any single GPU has.
  • Model FLOPs utilisation (MFU) is the honest efficiency number. Well-tuned large-scale training reaches 35–55%. Plan with 40%, not with peak. Using peak makes every estimate wrong by a factor of two to three.
  • Chinchilla’s ~20 tokens per parameter is a training-cost optimum, not a deployment optimum. If you will serve the model heavily, the total-cost optimum is a smaller model trained on far more tokens — which is why released models are systematically “over-trained” relative to Chinchilla.
  • For inference sizing, the KV cache is the number that surprises people. At long context and moderate batch it can exceed the weights, and it grows linearly in both batch and sequence length.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The whole calculation, on one page

Section titled “The whole calculation, on one page”

Before any derivation, here is the shape of the answer.

  1. Count the work. Training a model with NNN parameters on DDD tokens costs about 6ND6ND6ND floating-point operations. That single formula does most of the job.

  2. Divide by what your hardware actually delivers. Not its peak. Take peak dense FLOP/s for your precision and multiply by a realistic utilisation — 40% is a defensible planning figure for a well-engineered run, and 20–30% is common for a first attempt.

  3. That gives you GPU-seconds. Divide by the number of GPUs for wall-clock, multiply by the hourly rate for dollars.

  4. Separately, check that it fits. Compute tells you how long; memory tells you whether it runs. Memory is the constraint that stops most projects, and it is the one people check last.

A concrete instance, carried through: a 7B model on 1 trillion tokens is 6×7×109×1012=4.2×10226 \times 7\times10^9 \times 10^{12} = 4.2 \times 10^{22}6×7×109×1012=4.2×1022 FLOPs. On 64 H100s at 40% MFU that is 4.2×1022/(64×990×1012×0.4)4.2\times10^{22} / (64 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.4)4.2×1022/(64×990×1012×0.4) ≈ 1.66 million seconds ≈ 19 days, and at roughly 3.50perGPU−hour,about∗∗3.50 per GPU-hour, about **3.50perGPU−hour,about∗∗102,000**.

Every number in that sentence is one you can compute yourself in a minute. The rest of this page explains where each comes from and where it goes wrong.

Why the factor is 6

Section titled “Why the factor is 6”

This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because the same reasoning tells you how to modify it.

Consider one parameter in one matrix multiplication, and one token flowing through it.

  • Forward: the parameter is multiplied by an input and the result accumulated. One multiply, one add → 2 FLOPs.
  • Backward, gradient with respect to the input: the parameter is used again in a multiply-accumulate → 2 FLOPs.
  • Backward, gradient with respect to the parameter: another multiply-accumulate → 2 FLOPs.

Total 6 FLOPs per parameter per token. Multiply by NNN parameters and DDD tokens and you have C≈6NDC \approx 6NDC≈6ND.

The immediate corollaries

Because the derivation is structural, the modifications follow directly:

  • Forward only (inference prefill, or evaluating a frozen model): 2ND2ND2ND, one third the cost.
  • Frozen backbone with a small trainable head (linear probing, LoRA): the frozen parameters cost 2 FLOPs each for the forward and 2 more for passing gradient through them, but not the third 2 for their own gradient. So roughly 4NDfrozen+6NDtrainable4ND_{\text{frozen}} + 6ND_{\text{trainable}}4NDfrozen​+6NDtrainable​, and since the trainable fraction is tiny, the saving is about a third — not the 100× that “we only train 0.1% of parameters” suggests. LoRA saves memory, not compute. This is the single most common misconception about parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
  • Gradient checkpointing recomputes the forward pass during the backward, adding roughly 2ND2ND2ND: total 8ND8ND8ND, a 33% compute increase to buy a large memory reduction.

Compute and memory are different budgets

Section titled “Compute and memory are different budgets”

People conflate these constantly, so state it plainly.

Compute determines how long a job takes. It scales with parameters × tokens and is spread across however many GPUs you have. Throw more GPUs at it and it finishes sooner.

Memory determines whether the job runs at all. It scales with parameters × bytes-per-parameter, plus activations. Throwing more GPUs at it only helps if you use a parallelism strategy that actually shards the state — which is exactly what ZeRO and FSDP are for.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

FLOPs, with the attention correction

Section titled “FLOPs, with the attention correction”

The 6ND6ND6ND estimate counts the parameter-weighted matrix multiplications. It omits the attention score computation, which involves no parameters but real work.

Per token, per layer, self-attention over a context of length sss costs about 4sd4sd4sd forward FLOPs — 2sd2sd2sd to form the scores QK⊤QK^\topQK⊤ against sss previous positions, and 2sd2sd2sd to apply them to VVV, where ddd is the model width. Applying the same 3× factor for the backward pass and summing over LLL layers and DDD tokens:

C≈6ND+12LsdD.C \approx 6ND + 12LsdD.C≈6ND+12LsdD.

To see when the second term matters, take the ratio, and use the fact that a standard transformer has roughly N≈12Ld2N \approx 12Ld^2N≈12Ld2 parameters:

12LsdD6ND=2LsdN≈2Lsd12Ld2=s6d.\frac{12LsdD}{6ND} = \frac{2Ls d}{N} \approx \frac{2Lsd}{12Ld^{2}} = \frac{s}{6d}.6ND12LsdD​=N2Lsd​≈12Ld22Lsd​=6ds​.

So the attention correction is about s/6ds / 6ds/6d. For a 70B model with d=8192d = 8192d=8192 at s=4096s = 4096s=4096 that is 8% — noticeable, ignorable for planning. At s=128,000s = 128{,}000s=128,000 it is 2.6×, so attention costs more than everything else combined and 6ND6ND6ND is badly wrong. Causal masking halves both terms of the attention count in practice, so treat this as an upper bound.

When 6ND stops being trustworthy

Four regimes where the shortcut breaks:

  • Long context. As above. Past roughly s≈6ds \approx 6ds≈6d, attention FLOPs rival parameter FLOPs, then exceed them.
  • Mixture of experts. NNN must be the active parameters per token, not the total. A model with 400B total and 30B active costs 6×30B×D6 \times 30\text{B} \times D6×30B×D for compute, but needs memory for all 400B. See Mixture of Experts.
  • Small models. Embedding and output-projection FLOPs, and the non-matmul work, are a larger fraction. 6ND6ND6ND can underestimate by 20% or more below ~1B parameters.
  • Gradient checkpointing. Add roughly a third, as derived above.

Memory accounting, item by item

Section titled “Memory accounting, item by item”

Four categories, and you must budget all four.

1. Parameters. N×bwN \times b_wN×bw​ where bwb_wbw​ is bytes per weight. BF16: 2.

2. Gradients. Same shape as parameters. Usually BF16 (2 bytes), sometimes accumulated in FP32 (4).

3. Optimizer state. The recipe that dominates practice is Adam with an FP32 master copy: 4 bytes for the master weights, 4 for the first moment, 4 for the second. 12 bytes per parameter.

Adding it up for standard mixed-precision Adam: 2+2+12=162 + 2 + 12 = 162+2+12=16 bytes per parameter. This is the ZeRO paper’s accounting and it is the number to carry around.

4. Activations. Everything saved during the forward pass for use in the backward. Unlike the first three, this scales with batch size and sequence length, not just with the model. Korthikanti et al. give, for a standard transformer layer without any recomputation:

Mact=s b h L(34+5 a sh) bytes,M_{\text{act}} = s\,b\,h\,L\left(34 + 5\,\frac{a\,s}{h}\right) \text{ bytes},Mact​=sbhL(34+5has​) bytes,

with sss sequence length, bbb microbatch, hhh hidden size, LLL layers, aaa attention heads. The 343434 term is linear in sequence length; the 5as/h5as/h5as/h term is quadratic, and comes from materialising attention scores.

Why the quadratic term is mostly gone now

That 5as/h5as/h5as/h term assumed attention scores are written to memory. FlashAttention does not write them, so in any modern stack the quadratic term largely disappears and activation memory is approximately linear in sss. Keep the formula for intuition about why long-context training used to be impossible, but do not use it to size a 2026 training run — measure instead.

MFU: the number that makes estimates honest

Section titled “MFU: the number that makes estimates honest”

Model FLOPs Utilisation, introduced with PaLM, is

MFU=model FLOPs per second actually achievedpeak FLOP/s of the hardware,\text{MFU} = \frac{\text{model FLOPs per second actually achieved}}{\text{peak FLOP/s of the hardware}},MFU=peak FLOP/s of the hardwaremodel FLOPs per second actually achieved​,

where “model FLOPs” means the 6ND6ND6ND work the model logically requires — not counting recomputation. (The variant that does count recomputation is hardware FLOPs utilisation, HFU, and it is always the larger number. Papers are not always clear about which they report; if a figure looks surprisingly good, check.)

SettingTypical MFU
Large-scale pretraining, well-tuned, dense transformer40–55%
Same, first attempt without tuning15–30%
Long-context traininglower — attention is less efficient
MoE trainingtypically lower — routing and expert imbalance
Fine-tuning small models on few GPUsoften 20–35%
Inference, batch-1 decode~1% and this is expected, not a failure

Plan with 40%, report what you measure

The single most common estimation error is dividing by peak FLOPs. It makes every timeline optimistic by a factor of two to three and it is the reason “we’ll train it over the weekend” becomes a fortnight. Use 40% for planning a dense pretraining run on good hardware, 25% if you have not tuned anything, and then measure your actual MFU in the first hour and re-plan.

The wall-clock and cost formulas

Section titled “The wall-clock and cost formulas”

Put it together. With nnn accelerators of peak throughput XXX FLOP/s at utilisation uuu:

T=6NDn X u,Cost=T⋅n⋅p,T = \frac{6ND}{n \, X \, u}, \qquad \text{Cost} = T \cdot n \cdot p,T=nXu6ND​,Cost=T⋅n⋅p,

with ppp the price per accelerator-hour (and TTT converted to hours).

Note that cost is independent of nnn: T⋅n=6ND/(Xu)T \cdot n = 6ND/(Xu)T⋅n=6ND/(Xu). Doubling the cluster halves the time at the same price — in the ideal case. In practice uuu falls as nnn grows, because communication grows and stragglers appear, so scaling out costs more in total. That degradation is the real subject of Distributed Training.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

Chinchilla, stated properly

Section titled “Chinchilla, stated properly”

Hoffmann et al. fit the loss as a function of parameters and tokens:

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ,L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},L(N,D)=E+NαA​+DβB​,

with fitted values around A≈406.4A \approx 406.4A≈406.4, B≈410.7B \approx 410.7B≈410.7, E≈1.69E \approx 1.69E≈1.69, α≈0.34\alpha \approx 0.34α≈0.34, β≈0.28\beta \approx 0.28β≈0.28.

Minimise LLL subject to the compute constraint C=6NDC = 6NDC=6ND. Forming the Lagrangian and setting derivatives to zero gives

Nopt∝Ca,Dopt∝Cb,N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{b},Nopt​∝Ca,Dopt​∝Cb,

with a=β/(α+β)≈0.46a = \beta/(\alpha+\beta) \approx 0.46a=β/(α+β)≈0.46 and b=α/(α+β)≈0.54b = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta) \approx 0.54b=α/(α+β)≈0.54. Because a≈b≈0.5a \approx b \approx 0.5a≈b≈0.5, the practical rule is that parameters and tokens should scale roughly together, giving the famous D≈20ND \approx 20ND≈20N.

Three things Chinchilla is not

  1. Not a deployment optimum. It minimises the loss achievable for a fixed training budget. It says nothing about serving cost, and serving cost scales with NNN forever.

  2. Not exactly 20. The ratio depends on the fitted exponents, which depend on the architecture, the data and the tokenizer. It has been re-estimated several times with somewhat different results, and a re-analysis found the original fit’s confidence intervals were understated. Treat 20 as an order-of-magnitude anchor.

  3. Not applicable to a fixed target quality. If you need a specific capability rather than the best loss for a budget, the constrained-optimisation framing does not apply.

Inference-aware sizing

Section titled “Inference-aware sizing”

Sardana et al. make the correction that matters commercially. If you will serve DinfD_{\text{inf}}Dinf​ tokens over the model’s lifetime, total cost is

Ctotal=6NDtrain⏟training+2NDinf⏟inference,C_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{6ND_{\text{train}}}_{\text{training}} + \underbrace{2ND_{\text{inf}}}_{\text{inference}},Ctotal​=training6NDtrain​​​+inference2NDinf​​​,

and minimising this subject to a fixed target loss gives a smaller NNN trained on more tokens than Chinchilla prescribes.

The intuition is simple: training compute is paid once and scales as 6ND6ND6ND; inference compute is paid per served token and scales as 2N2N2N per token, forever. Once DinfD_{\text{inf}}Dinf​ is large, the 2NDinf2ND_{\text{inf}}2NDinf​ term dominates everything and shrinking NNN is worth almost any amount of extra training.

This is why every released open-weights model is “over-trained” relative to Chinchilla — Llama-3-8B saw roughly 15 trillion tokens against a Chinchilla prescription of about 160 billion, nearly 100× more. That was not a mistake. It was the correct answer to a different, better-posed question.

KV cache, the inference memory formula

Section titled “KV cache, the inference memory formula”

For autoregressive decoding you cache keys and values for every past position:

MKV=2⋅L⋅nkv⋅dhead⋅s⋅b⋅bbytes,M_{\text{KV}} = 2 \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot s \cdot b \cdot b_{\text{bytes}},MKV​=2⋅L⋅nkv​⋅dhead​⋅s⋅b⋅bbytes​,

with the leading 2 for keys and values, nkvn_{\text{kv}}nkv​ the number of key-value heads (equal to the number of query heads under plain MHA, far fewer under GQA), sss the sequence length and bbb the batch.

Worked: Llama-3-70B has L=80L = 80L=80, 8 KV heads, dhead=128d_{\text{head}} = 128dhead​=128. In FP16 at s=8192s = 8192s=8192 and b=32b = 32b=32:

2×80×8×128×8192×32×2=85.9 GB.2 \times 80 \times 8 \times 128 \times 8192 \times 32 \times 2 = 85.9 \text{ GB}.2×80×8×128×8192×32×2=85.9 GB.

The weights are 140 GB. The cache is 86 GB and grows linearly in both bbb and sss — double the batch or double the context and it exceeds the model. This is the constraint that PagedAttention and GQA exist to manage, and it is why context length is priced.

Without GQA — 64 KV heads instead of 8 — the same configuration would need 687 GB. GQA is not a minor efficiency tweak; it is what makes long-context serving economically possible at all.

Worked examples

Goal: adapt a 7B model on 50 million tokens of domain data.

Compute. Frozen base costs ~4 FLOPs per parameter per token, adapters negligible: ≈4×7×109×5×107=1.4×1018\approx 4 \times 7\times10^9 \times 5\times10^7 = 1.4\times10^{18}≈4×7×109×5×107=1.4×1018 FLOPs.

Hardware. One H100 at ~30% MFU: 990×1012×0.3=2.97×1014990\times10^{12} \times 0.3 = 2.97\times10^{14}990×1012×0.3=2.97×1014 FLOP/s.

Time. 1.4×1018/2.97×1014≈4,7001.4\times10^{18} / 2.97\times10^{14} \approx 4{,}7001.4×1018/2.97×1014≈4,700 s ≈ 1.3 hours.

Memory. 4-bit base ≈ 3.5 GB, adapters and their optimizer state ≈ 0.2 GB, activations at modest batch and 2k context a few GB. Comfortable on a 24 GB consumer card; trivial on an 80 GB H100.

Cost. Under $10. This is the regime where you should stop optimising and just run it — and where renting is obviously correct.

Goal: full-parameter fine-tune, 7B, 50M tokens.

Compute. 6×7×109×5×107=2.1×10186 \times 7\times10^9 \times 5\times10^7 = 2.1\times10^{18}6×7×109×5×107=2.1×1018 FLOPs — only 1.5× the LoRA run.

Memory. This is the actual problem. Mixed-precision Adam: 16×7×109=11216 \times 7\times10^9 = 11216×7×109=112 GB of persistent state. Plus activations. Does not fit on one 80 GB GPU.

Options, in increasing order of disruption: 8-bit Adam (~70 GB, fits but leaves almost nothing for activations); gradient checkpointing to cut activations at 33% more compute; ZeRO-2 or FSDP across 2–4 GPUs to shard optimizer state and gradients; CPU offload of optimizer state, which works but is slow.

Realistic plan. 4× H100 with FSDP. Compute time on 4 GPUs at 35% MFU: 2.1×1018/(4×990×1012×0.35)≈1,5002.1\times10^{18} / (4 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.35) \approx 1{,}5002.1×1018/(4×990×1012×0.35)≈1,500 s. Call it an hour with data loading and checkpointing. About $15.

The lesson. Full fine-tuning is barely more compute than LoRA and vastly more memory. If you have the memory, the compute argument for LoRA is weak; if you do not, LoRA is not an optimisation but an enabler.

Goal: pretrain 7B from scratch.

Tokens. Chinchilla-optimal: 20×7×109=14020 \times 7\times10^9 = 14020×7×109=140 billion. But per the inference-aware argument, if you intend to serve it, train longer — 1–2 trillion is the modern practice.

Compute at 1T tokens. 6×7×109×1012=4.2×10226 \times 7\times10^9 \times 10^{12} = 4.2\times10^{22}6×7×109×1012=4.2×1022 FLOPs.

Time on 64 H100s at 40% MFU. 4.2×1022/(64×990×1012×0.4)≈1.66×1064.2\times10^{22} / (64 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.4) \approx 1.66\times10^{6}4.2×1022/(64×990×1012×0.4)≈1.66×106 s ≈ 19.2 days.

Cost. 64 GPUs × 461 hours × 3.50/GPU−hour≈∗∗3.50/GPU-hour ≈ **3.50/GPU−hour≈∗∗103,000** at neocloud rates; roughly $200k–300k on a hyperscaler.

Sanity check the other way. At 140B tokens (strict Chinchilla) it is 2.7 days and ~$14,000 — and you get a noticeably weaker model. That gap is the price of the inference-aware correction, and for anything you plan to deploy it is worth paying.

Goal: 70B parameters, 15 trillion tokens — roughly the Llama-3-70B recipe.

Compute. 6×7×1010×1.5×1013=6.3×10246 \times 7\times10^{10} \times 1.5\times10^{13} = 6.3\times10^{24}6×7×1010×1.5×1013=6.3×1024 FLOPs.

Time on 1,024 H100s at 45% MFU. 6.3×1024/(1024×990×1012×0.45)≈1.38×1076.3\times10^{24} / (1024 \times 990\times10^{12} \times 0.45) \approx 1.38\times10^{7}6.3×1024/(1024×990×1012×0.45)≈1.38×107 s ≈ 160 days.

Cost. 1,024 GPUs × 3,836 hours × 3.00/GPU−hour≈∗∗3.00/GPU-hour ≈ **3.00/GPU−hour≈∗∗11.8 million** in compute alone. Add data acquisition and cleaning, failed runs — plan for two to three times the ideal figure — staff, and storage.

Memory. 70B × 16 bytes = 1.12 TB of optimizer state. Requires sharding across at least 16–32 GPUs before considering activations, so tensor + pipeline + data parallelism together, not a single strategy.

The honest framing. This is the scale where the compute estimate becomes the easy part. Node failures at 1,024 GPUs are a weekly event, checkpoint-and-restart overhead is material, and the difference between a good run and a bad one is measured in months of wall clock.
Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Scaling-law fits are less settled than the citation count suggests. The Chinchilla coefficients have been re-derived by independent groups with meaningfully different exponents, and a reproduction found the original paper’s uncertainty intervals were too narrow. The qualitative conclusion — parameters and data should scale together, and GPT-3 was badly under-trained — is robust. The specific number 20 is not.

Compute-optimal has been largely abandoned in favour of inference-optimal. Every major open-weights release trains far past Chinchilla, for the reason given above. The interesting open question is where the returns actually stop: models continue improving at 100× the Chinchilla token count, and no one has published a convincing account of the asymptote.

MFU has stopped improving. Reported figures for large dense transformer pretraining have sat in the 40–55% band across several hardware generations. Faster chips have not translated into better utilisation, because communication and memory have not kept pace with arithmetic. This is the same memory-wall story from the accelerators page viewed from the software side.

MoE complicates every formula here. With sparse activation, compute scales with active parameters and memory with total parameters, which decouples two quantities that all the classical scaling work treats as one. Scaling laws for MoE exist but are less mature, and the effective-parameter-count question does not have a settled answer.

Reasoning models moved the budget to inference. When a model spends thousands of tokens of chain-of-thought per query, the 2NDinf2ND_{\text{inf}}2NDinf​ term grows by whatever the average trace length is. The whole training/inference balance shifts, and the inference-aware scaling analyses have not caught up.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  1. Predicting downstream capability from loss. Scaling laws predict validation loss well. They predict whether a model can do a task poorly. Emergence claims are contested — much of the apparent discontinuity comes from discontinuous metrics — but the mapping from loss to usefulness remains largely empirical.

  2. Data-constrained scaling. High-quality text is finite. What the right substitution rate is between repeated data, synthetic data and more parameters is an active and unresolved question.

  3. Scaling laws for post-training. RLHF, distillation and reasoning training have no compute-optimal theory comparable to Chinchilla’s, despite consuming a large and growing share of budgets.

  4. The utilisation gap. Roughly half of purchased FLOPs are lost. No one has a principled account of how much of that is irreducible.

  5. Honest total cost of ownership. Published costs are almost always the successful run only. Failed runs, data pipeline compute, evaluation and the human time to babysit a 160-day job routinely double or triple the figure, and nobody reports them.

Practical guidance

Section titled “Practical guidance”
  • Compute the estimate before you provision. Five minutes of arithmetic routinely reveals that a plan is off by an order of magnitude, in either direction.
  • Check memory first, compute second. Compute makes a job slow. Memory makes it impossible.
  • Measure MFU in the first hour of any long run. If it is below 20%, stop and fix it — you will save more than any subsequent optimisation.
  • Budget 2–3× the ideal figure. Failed runs, restarts, hyperparameter searches and data pipeline compute are not optional extras; they are most of the difference between the estimate and the invoice.
  • For anything under a few thousand GPU-hours, rent. The break-even against owning hardware sits far above where most projects live — see Choosing Hardware.
  • Do not size a serving deployment on weights alone. Add the KV cache at your actual target batch and context, and it will often be the larger number.
