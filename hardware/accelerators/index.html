Accelerators and the Memory Hierarchy

Almost everyone shopping for AI hardware compares the wrong number. Peak FLOPs is the figure on the box, but the overwhelming majority of deep-learning kernels finish long before the arithmetic units are saturated, because they are waiting on memory. This page builds the mental model that makes hardware decisions predictable: a small number of bytes-per-second and FLOPs-per-second figures, one ratio that combines them, and a set of honest caveats about how vendors report all three.

What you should walk away with

  • Bandwidth, not FLOPs, is the scarce resource. An H100 can do about 990 dense BF16 TFLOP/s and read about 3.35 TB/s. That is roughly 300 FLOPs per byte. Any kernel doing less arithmetic per byte than that is memory-bound, and most are.
  • Arithmetic intensity is the one number to compute. FLOPs divided by bytes moved. Compare it to the machine’s ratio and you know immediately whether more compute or more bandwidth would help.
  • Capacity decides what you can run; bandwidth decides how fast. These are different constraints and people routinely conflate them. 141 GB of HBM3e does not make an H200 faster at training — it makes larger batches and longer contexts possible, which then makes it faster.
  • Vendor spec sheets are not comparable as printed. NVIDIA’s H100 table prints only 2:4-sparse numbers; AMD’s platform brochures print 8-GPU sparse aggregates; GeForce cards halve their tensor throughput under the FP32 accumulation that PyTorch actually uses. Halve, divide, and check the footnote before comparing anything.
  • Lower precision is a bandwidth optimisation that happens to also be a compute optimisation. FP8 weights move half the bytes of BF16 and, on hardware with an FP8 path, run twice as fast. The second effect gets the attention; the first is usually the one that matters.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Start with a kitchen

Section titled “Start with a kitchen”

A GPU is a kitchen with an enormous number of cooks and one narrow doorway to the pantry.

The cooks are the arithmetic units. There are tens of thousands of them and they are extremely fast. The pantry is high-bandwidth memory (HBM), which holds your model weights and activations. The doorway is the memory bus.

Now the question that decides everything: for each trip to the pantry, how much cooking gets done?

If a cook fetches one ingredient and performs one operation with it, the kitchen is limited by the doorway. Adding cooks changes nothing. If a cook fetches one ingredient and performs three hundred operations with it, the kitchen is limited by the cooks, and a wider doorway changes nothing.

Deep learning contains both kinds of work, and knowing which kind you are doing is most of what hardware intuition is.

The one ratio

Section titled “The one ratio”

Take any piece of work. Count the floating-point operations it performs. Count the bytes it must move from HBM. Divide.

That is arithmetic intensity, and it has units of FLOPs per byte.

Every machine has its own ratio: peak FLOP/s divided by peak bytes/s. For an H100 in BF16 that is roughly 989 × 10¹² ÷ 3.35 × 10¹² ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte.

Now compare:

  • Your kernel’s intensity is above the machine’s ratio → you are compute-bound. Buy more FLOPs, or use lower precision to get more FLOPs.
  • Your kernel’s intensity is below it → you are memory-bound. Buy more bandwidth, or restructure the computation to move fewer bytes. Adding FLOPs does nothing at all.

The number that surprises people

Generating one token from a 70B model at batch size 1 has an arithmetic intensity of about 2 FLOPs per byte. The machine wants 295. You are using roughly 0.7% of the GPU’s arithmetic capability, and no amount of extra compute will help.

This is not a bug in your code. It is the structure of the problem, and it is why inference is a separate engineering discipline from training.

Why matrix multiplication is special

Section titled “Why matrix multiplication is special”

Deep learning runs on GPUs for one structural reason: matrix multiplication has arithmetic intensity that grows with size.

Multiply two n×nn \times nn×n matrices. You move 3n23n^23n2 elements and perform 2n32n^32n3 operations. Intensity is 2n3/(3n2⋅bytes)∝n2n^3 / (3n^2 \cdot \text{bytes}) \propto n2n3/(3n2⋅bytes)∝n. Make the matrices bigger and each byte does more work.

Contrast an elementwise operation — adding a bias, applying GELU. You read an element, do one or two operations, write it back. Intensity is a small constant, independent of size, and always far below the machine’s ratio. Those kernels are always memory-bound, which is why compilers work so hard to fuse them into the matmul that produced their input.

What this predicts, correctly

Three things follow immediately, and all three are true in practice:

  1. Bigger batches are more efficient — until you run out of memory, because they raise intensity.
  2. Small models on big GPUs waste the GPU — the matrices are too small to reach the machine’s ratio.
  3. Attention was slow for reasons that had nothing to do with FLOPs — the n2n^2n2 score matrix was being written to and read from HBM. FlashAttention did not reduce the arithmetic; it kept the tiles on chip. Same FLOPs, several times faster.
Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

What is physically on the die

Section titled “What is physically on the die”

An NVIDIA datacenter GPU is a grid of streaming multiprocessors (SMs) — 132 on an H100 SXM. Each SM contains:

  • CUDA cores — general-purpose FP32/INT32 lanes. These do the elementwise work, the reductions, the indexing.
  • Tensor cores — fixed-function units that consume small matrix tiles and emit a matrix product. This is where essentially all the advertised FLOPs come from. On an H100, tensor cores provide roughly 15× the BF16 throughput of the CUDA cores.
  • Shared memory / L1 — 228 KB per SM, software-managed. The staging area for tiling.
  • Register file — 256 KB per SM, the fastest storage on the chip.

Threads are grouped into warps of 32 that execute in lockstep. Warps are grouped into thread blocks which are resident on one SM and can share its shared memory. This is why tile sizes in high-performance kernels are always multiples of awkward numbers: they are chosen so that a tile fits in shared memory and the warps map cleanly onto the tensor-core instruction shape.

The practical consequence for model design

Tensor cores operate on fixed tile shapes, so dimensions that are not multiples of those shapes leave lanes idle. Anthony et al. quantify this: hidden sizes and vocabulary sizes divisible by 64 (better, 128) can be substantially faster than nearby values that are not. Choosing a hidden size of 4096 rather than 4000 is not superstition — it is alignment with the hardware’s tile geometry.

Reading the roofline

Section titled “Reading the roofline”

The roofline model, from Williams, Waterman and Patterson, plots achievable performance against arithmetic intensity. It is two straight lines on a log-log plot, and it is the single most useful diagram in performance engineering.

Precision, and what each format buys

Numeric format changes three things at once: bytes moved, arithmetic throughput, and numerical behaviour. Conflating them causes most of the confusion.

Why BF16 beat FP16 despite being less precise

BF16 has fewer mantissa bits than FP16 — 7 against 10 — so it represents each number less precisely. It won anyway, because it keeps FP32’s 8 exponent bits and therefore FP32’s dynamic range.

Training failures at low precision are overwhelmingly range failures: a gradient underflows to zero or an activation overflows to infinity. FP16 training needs loss scaling — multiplying the loss by a large constant to drag gradients into representable range — and that machinery is fiddly and can diverge. BF16 needs none of it, because anything that fits in FP32’s range fits in BF16’s.

Precision you can average away over a batch. Range you cannot. That is the whole argument, and it generalises: the FP8 pair splits along exactly the same line, with E5M2 (more range) reserved for gradients and E4M3 (more precision) for forward values.

The interconnect tiers

Section titled “The interconnect tiers”

A single GPU is rarely the unit of interest. What matters is how quickly GPUs can talk, and the bandwidth falls off a cliff at each boundary.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

Arithmetic intensity, precisely

Section titled “Arithmetic intensity, precisely”

For a kernel that performs WWW floating-point operations and moves QQQ bytes between HBM and the chip, the arithmetic intensity is

I=WQ.I = \frac{W}{Q}.I=QW​.

Let π\piπ be the machine’s peak throughput in FLOP/s and β\betaβ its peak memory bandwidth in bytes/s. Attainable performance is

P=min⁡(π,  β⋅I).P = \min(\pi,\; \beta \cdot I).P=min(π,β⋅I).

The machine balance is I⋆=π/βI^\star = \pi / \betaI⋆=π/β, the intensity at which the two terms are equal. Below it you are bandwidth-limited; above it, compute-limited.

What the roofline deliberately ignores

This model assumes bandwidth and compute are each independently saturable and perfectly overlapped. Reality intrudes in at least four ways:

  • Latency, not just bandwidth. With too few concurrent memory requests in flight you cannot reach β\betaβ at all, regardless of intensity. Occupancy exists to hide latency.
  • Caches. QQQ is HBM traffic, so a kernel that hits in L2 has a much better effective intensity than a naive count suggests. This is exactly the lever tiling pulls.
  • Power and clocks. Sustained heavy tensor-core work throttles clocks below the boost frequency the peak number assumes. Achieving 100% of a printed peak is not merely difficult; it is often thermally impossible.
  • Kernel launch and synchronisation. Small operations spend more time being scheduled than executing.

The roofline is a bound and a diagnostic, not a prediction. Its value is that when you measure far below the roof, it tells you which roof you are under.

A worked intensity calculation

Section titled “A worked intensity calculation”

Take a linear layer Y=XWY = XWY=XW with X∈RB×dX \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d}X∈RB×d and W∈Rd×dW \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}W∈Rd×d, in BF16 (2 bytes).

FLOPs: 2Bd22Bd^22Bd2 — one multiply and one add per element of the inner product.

Bytes: read XXX (2Bd2Bd2Bd), read WWW (2d22d^22d2), write YYY (2Bd2Bd2Bd), so Q=4Bd+2d2Q = 4Bd + 2d^2Q=4Bd+2d2.

I=2Bd24Bd+2d2=Bd2B+d.I = \frac{2Bd^2}{4Bd + 2d^2} = \frac{Bd}{2B + d}.I=4Bd+2d22Bd2​=2B+dBd​.

Two regimes fall straight out.

Large batch, B≫dB \gg dB≫d: the ddd term in the denominator vanishes and I→d/2I \to d/2I→d/2. With d=4096d = 4096d=4096 that is 2048 FLOPs/byte, comfortably compute-bound. The weight read is amortised across the whole batch.

Batch of one, B=1B = 1B=1: I=d/(2+d)→1I = d/(2 + d) \to 1I=d/(2+d)→1. You read the entire weight matrix to perform a single vector-matrix product. One FLOP per byte, against a machine that wants 295.

That ratio — d/2d/2d/2 against 111 — is the entire quantitative case for batching, and the reason a serving system’s throughput and its latency pull in opposite directions.

Where the FLOPs come from

Section titled “Where the FLOPs come from”

A tensor core computes a small matrix product per instruction. On Hopper the relevant instruction family operates on tiles of shape 64×N×1664 \times N \times 1664×N×16 per warpgroup. Peak throughput is

π=(SMs)×(tensor cores per SM)×(FLOPs per core-cycle)×fclock.\pi = (\text{SMs}) \times (\text{tensor cores per SM}) \times (\text{FLOPs per core-cycle}) \times f_{\text{clock}}.π=(SMs)×(tensor cores per SM)×(FLOPs per core-cycle)×fclock​.

For H100 SXM: 132 SMs × 4 tensor cores × 1024 BF16 FLOPs per cycle × 1.755 GHz ≈ 949 TFLOP/s, which lands within a couple of percent of the published 989 — the gap is clock-boost accounting.

The point of doing this arithmetic once is that it makes the structure visible: peak FLOPs is a product of four numbers, and vendors improve it by increasing any of them. A generational “2× FLOPs” claim may be twice the tensor cores, or the same cores at half the precision, and those two have very different implications for your workload.

Reading a spec sheet without being fooled

Five traps, each of which has produced widely repeated wrong numbers:

  1. Sparse vs dense. NVIDIA’s datacenter tables often print 2:4-structured-sparse figures; dense is exactly half. NVIDIA’s H100 page prints only sparse, while the A100 page prints both — so a naive H100/A100 comparison overstates the gap by 2×.
  2. Per-package vs per-GPU. A GB200 superchip contains two B200 GPUs. Superchip tables must be halved before comparing to a single GPU.
  3. Platform vs chip. AMD’s MI355X platform brochure headlines an 8-GPU sparse aggregate. Divide by 8, then by 2.
  4. Accumulation precision on consumer cards. GeForce parts run FP16 tensor ops with FP32 accumulation at exactly half rate. PyTorch and cuBLAS default to FP32 accumulation, so an RTX 4090 delivers roughly 165 dense TFLOP/s in practice, not the 330 implied by the FP16-accumulate figure.
  5. SKU divergence under one name. “B200” denotes both the HGX part (180 GB, 7.7 TB/s, 1000 W) and the GB200-rack part (186 GB, 8 TB/s, 1200 W). The widely quoted “192 GB” appears in no current NVIDIA datasheet.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

HBM is the binding constraint on the whole industry. Capacity and bandwidth per package have grown far more slowly than transistor budgets, and HBM supply — not fab capacity for the logic die — has been the practical limiter on accelerator volume. HBM4 arrives with Rubin and MI455X at roughly 22–23 TB/s per package, a real step, but memory has been losing ground to compute for two decades and one generation does not reverse that.

The unit of purchase has shifted from chip to rack. GB200 NVL72 puts 72 GPUs in a single NVLink domain at 130 TB/s aggregate. The product is not a faster GPU; it is a larger region within which the node boundary does not exist. That changes which parallelism strategies are viable — see Distributed Training — and it is the main reason rack-scale systems command the premium they do.

Precision is still descending. FP8 training is now routine; FP4 pretraining is being reported and the hardware paths exist on Blackwell and MI355X. Each step halves bytes moved and doubles nominal throughput, which is the only lever that has been reliably available. How far it goes is unresolved: the error introduced by block-scaled 4-bit formats is not uniformly benign across architectures, and the reported successes involve careful mixed-precision recipes rather than a flat format switch.

Alternatives are real but narrow. TPUs are genuinely competitive on throughput-per-dollar for workloads that fit the XLA compilation model, and Google’s pod-scale optical interconnect is a distinct architectural bet. Trainium’s cost position is strong. But CUDA’s software depth remains the dominant practical fact, and “it benchmarks well” and “your existing code runs on it” are different claims.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  1. The memory wall. Compute per package has grown far faster than bandwidth per package for twenty years. Processing-in-memory and 3D-stacked logic are proposed answers; neither has shipped at scale for training.

  2. Numerical stability at 4 bits and below. Block scaling, stochastic rounding and mixed-precision recipes make it work, but there is no theory that predicts which architectures and which optimisers tolerate a given format. Practice is empirical.

  3. Utilisation. Well-tuned large-scale pretraining reaches roughly 35–55% model FLOPs utilisation. The remaining half is lost to communication, bubbles, memory stalls and imperfect kernels. Recovering it is worth as much as a hardware generation and is far less well funded.

  4. Power and siting. A GB200 NVL72 rack draws well over 100 kW and requires liquid cooling. Grid interconnect timelines, not chip supply, increasingly gate deployment — which makes performance per watt a business constraint, not an environmental footnote.

  5. Benchmarks that mean anything. MLPerf is the serious attempt, but peak-FLOPs marketing continues to drive purchasing. There is no widely accepted way to compare accelerators on a workload you actually care about without running it.

Practical guidance

Section titled “Practical guidance”
  • Compute arithmetic intensity before buying anything. It takes five minutes and tells you whether bandwidth or FLOPs is your constraint. Buying the wrong one is expensive and common.
  • Size on memory first. Ask what fits, then ask how fast. A workload that does not fit runs at zero.
  • Profile before optimising. Nsight Compute or PyTorch’s profiler will tell you your achieved bandwidth and occupancy. Guessing which kernel is slow has a poor hit rate.
  • Halve every sparse number you see, and check the footnote. If a comparison looks too favourable, it usually involves two different footnote conventions.
  • Treat rental pricing as the real currency. As of August 2026, per-GPU-hour on-demand rates cluster around 3.3–4.0forH100,3.3–4.0 for H100, 3.3–4.0forH100,4.5–6.0 for H200 and $6.7–8.6 for B200 across the neoclouds, with hyperscalers roughly 1.5–3× higher. Divide by throughput to compare properly — see Compute Budgeting.
