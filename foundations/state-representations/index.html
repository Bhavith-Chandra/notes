State Representations

A state is whatever a system carries forward in time to decide what to do next. That leaves an enormous amount unspecified, and the choices are not interchangeable — a belief state, a successor representation and a value-equivalent latent answer different questions and fail in different ways. This page catalogues every kind, states precisely what each one is sufficient for, and gives the one criterion that unifies them: a representation is adequate exactly when discarding what it discards costs you nothing you needed.

What you should walk away with

  • Sufficiency is always relative to a question. There is no “correct” state. A representation is sufficient for prediction, or for a fixed reward, or for a family of rewards, or for optimal control — and these are genuinely different, strictly ordered requirements.
  • Markov is the property everything is chasing. A state is Markov if the future is independent of the past given it. Every representation here is an attempt to construct something Markov from observations that are not.
  • The most abstract representation that preserves what you need is the best one. Extra detail is not free: it costs sample complexity, invites model exploitation, and does not generalise. This is the whole argument for value-equivalence over reconstruction.
  • Predictive and successor representations are the two genuinely different ideas here. Both define state by what it predicts rather than what it contains — PSRs by future observations, successor representations by future state occupancy. Both make certain transfers trivial that are otherwise hard.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

What a state is for

Section titled “What a state is for”

You are playing a game. At each moment you must decide what to do. What do you need to remember?

Not everything you have ever seen — that would be unmanageable and mostly irrelevant. Not just the current screen either, because a single frame does not tell you whether the ball is moving up or down.

You need exactly enough that nothing else from the past would change your decision. That is the whole specification, and it has a name.

The Markov property

Section titled “The Markov property”

A state is Markov if, given it, the future is independent of the past. Knowing how you got here adds nothing.

P(future∣state,past)=P(future∣state)P(\text{future} \mid \text{state}, \text{past}) = P(\text{future} \mid \text{state})P(future∣state,past)=P(future∣state)

This is the property everything on this page is trying to construct. Raw observations usually are not Markov — one video frame does not contain velocity, one sensor reading does not contain the robot’s hidden joint temperature — so you have to build something that is.

The two failure directions

Too little state and you cannot act well, because the information you needed is gone and no amount of computation recovers it. Too much state and you need vastly more data to learn anything, because you are learning over a space where most distinctions do not matter. Almost every design choice here is a position on that axis.

Sufficient for what?

Section titled “Sufficient for what?”

Here is the idea that organises the entire catalogue, and it is worth slowing down for.

“Sufficient” is not a property of a representation. It is a property of a representation and a question together. Consider four increasingly demanding questions:

  1. Can I predict the next observation? The hardest requirement — you must retain everything that affects anything you might see, including irrelevant detail.

  2. Can I predict reward under one reward function? Much weaker. You can discard everything that does not affect this reward.

  3. Can I predict reward under any reward in a family? In between. You keep what any member of the family might need.

  4. Can I act optimally? Weakest of all, in a specific sense: two states with the same optimal action and value can be merged even if they look completely different and predict different futures.

These are strictly ordered. Anything sufficient for (1) is sufficient for the rest. The reverse fails — and that failure is not a defect, it is the point. A representation sufficient only for (4) can be enormously more compact, and compactness is what buys sample efficiency.

The families

Section titled “The families”

Raw observation. Use oto_tot​ directly. Simple, and almost never Markov — a single frame has no velocity, a single reading has no trend.

Frame stacking. Concatenate the last kkk observations. The standard fix in Atari (DQN used k=4k=4k=4), and it works because velocity is recoverable from consecutive frames. Fails when the required memory exceeds kkk, and the state grows linearly in kkk.

Hand-engineered features. A domain expert writes down what matters: joint angles, ball position, inventory counts. Compact, interpretable, and it caps you at what the expert thought of. Still the right answer for well-understood low-dimensional systems, and this is under-appreciated — a robot with accurate proprioception rarely needs a learned encoder.

The principled answer for partial observability. Maintain a distribution over the true hidden state:

bt(s)=P(st=s∣o1:t, a1:t−1).b_t(s) = P\big(s_t = s \mid o_{1:t},\, a_{1:t-1}\big).bt​(s)=P(st​=s∣o1:t​,a1:t−1​).

Updated by Bayes’ rule as observations arrive. The belief state is provably Markov and provably sufficient — a POMDP over observations becomes a fully observed MDP over beliefs.

Why this is not the end of the story. The belief is a probability distribution, so for ∣S∣|S|∣S∣ discrete states it lives on an (∣S∣−1)(|S|-1)(∣S∣−1)-simplex — continuous, and high-dimensional the moment ∣S∣|S|∣S∣ is large. Planning in belief space is PSPACE-hard in general. It is the correct object and rarely a tractable one.

Modern latent-state models are best read as learned approximations to belief states: an RSSM’s stochastic latent is doing the job of btb_tbt​, learned rather than computed.

A genuinely different idea, and underrated.

Instead of “what is the hidden state?”, ask “what will I observe if I do things?” A test is a sequence of actions and the observations they would produce. The predictive state is the vector of probabilities of a set of core tests succeeding.

pt=[ P(t1∣ht), P(t2∣ht), …, P(tk∣ht) ].p_t = \big[\,P(t_1 \mid h_t),\ P(t_2\mid h_t),\ \dots,\ P(t_k\mid h_t)\,\big].pt​=[P(t1​∣ht​), P(t2​∣ht​), …, P(tk​∣ht​)].

Why this matters. A PSR is grounded entirely in observable quantities. There is no hidden state to posit, no model of unobservables to get wrong, and the representation is verifiable against data by construction. Littman et al. showed PSRs are at least as compact as POMDPs and sometimes exponentially more so.

Why they stayed niche. Discovering the core tests is hard, learning algorithms were fragile, and deep latent models arrived and worked well enough empirically. The idea is better than its adoption, and it is worth knowing because it reappears: a JEPA world model defines state by what it predicts, which is the same move.

Dayan’s 1993 idea, and one of the most elegant here.

Separate where you will go from what it is worth. The successor representation stores expected discounted future occupancy:

M(s,s′)=E[∑t=0∞γt 1{st=s′} ∣ s0=s].M(s, s') = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,\mathbb{1}\{s_t = s'\}\ \Big|\ s_0 = s\right].M(s,s′)=E[t=0∑∞​γt1{st​=s′} ​ s0​=s].

Then for any reward vector rrr:

V(s)=∑s′M(s,s′) r(s′)=[Mr](s).V(s) = \sum_{s'} M(s,s')\,r(s') = \big[Mr\big](s).V(s)=s′∑​M(s,s′)r(s′)=[Mr](s).

The payoff is immediate. Change the reward, and you recompute the value with a single matrix-vector product — no re-learning of dynamics at all. The SR captures the environment’s structure independent of what you want from it.

Successor features generalise this to continuous spaces: replace state indicators with features ϕ(s)\phi(s)ϕ(s) and assume r(s)=ϕ(s)⊤wr(s) = \phi(s)^\top wr(s)=ϕ(s)⊤w. Then ψ(s)=E[∑γtϕ(st)]\psi(s) = \mathbb{E}[\sum \gamma^t \phi(s_t)]ψ(s)=E[∑γtϕ(st​)] and Q(s,a)=ψ(s,a)⊤wQ(s,a) = \psi(s,a)^\top wQ(s,a)=ψ(s,a)⊤w — transfer across any reward that is linear in your features.

It is also a genuine model of biology. Successor-representation-like coding has been proposed for hippocampal place cells, which is unusually strong convergence between an RL construct and neuroscience.

Merge states that behave identically. Two states are bisimilar if they have the same immediate reward and transition to bisimilar states with the same probabilities. It is the correct notion of “these are the same situation” for control.

Exact bisimulation is too brittle — a floating-point difference breaks it — so Ferns et al. defined a bisimulation metric:

d(si,sj)=max⁡a[ ∣rsia−rsja∣+γ W1(Psia, Psja; d)],d(s_i, s_j) = \max_a\Big[\,\big|r_{s_i}^a - r_{s_j}^a\big| + \gamma\,W_1\big(P^a_{s_i},\, P^a_{s_j};\, d\big)\Big],d(si​,sj​)=amax​[​rsi​a​−rsj​a​​+γW1​(Psi​a​,Psj​a​;d)],

with W1W_1W1​ the Wasserstein distance under ddd itself — a fixed point, computed by iteration.

DBC (Zhang et al. 2020) made this practical: learn an encoder whose latent distances approximate the bisimulation metric. The result is a representation that provably ignores task-irrelevant visual variation — background clutter, distractor objects, lighting — because those do not affect reward or transitions.

This is the theoretically cleanest answer to “what should a latent state contain?” and the practical difficulty is that estimating the Wasserstein term from samples is noisy.

The most aggressive abstraction. Do not require the latent to represent the observation at all — require only that it predicts reward, value and policy correctly.

Grimm et al. formalised it: two models are value-equivalent with respect to a policy set Π\PiΠ and value set V\mathcal{V}V if they induce identical Bellman updates:

Tm1πv=Tm2πv∀ π∈Π, v∈V.\mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m_1} v = \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m_2} v \quad \forall\, \pi\in\Pi,\ v\in\mathcal{V}.Tm1​π​v=Tm2​π​v∀π∈Π, v∈V.

The key theorem: the space of value-equivalent models shrinks as Π\PiΠ and V\mathcal{V}V grow, and searching over value-equivalent models is strictly easier than searching over accurate ones. You are not approximating the world; you are approximating its decision-relevant shadow.

MuZero is the flagship. Its latent has no decoder and cannot be turned back into a game screen — see the world model catalogue.

The cost: the representation is defined relative to a reward, so it does not transfer to a new task, and it cannot be learned from reward-free data.

Represent the scene as a set of objects, each with its own vector, rather than one monolithic embedding.

Slot Attention is the standard mechanism: KKK “slots” compete via an attention softmax over slots rather than over inputs, so each input feature is assigned to one slot, and slots iteratively refine to bind to distinct objects.

Why anyone wants this. Compositional generalisation. If a scene is a set of object vectors, a model can in principle handle three cubes having only seen two, because the representation composes. A monolithic embedding of “two cubes” has no structure that extends.

Why it has not taken over. It works on synthetic scenes with clean object boundaries and degrades on natural images, where “object” is not well-defined (is a person’s arm an object?). The binding problem — which features belong to which object — is not solved in general.

How the modern learned versions are trained

Section titled “How the modern learned versions are trained”

The families above are what to represent. These are how to get there from pixels.

MethodSignalWhat it produces
Reconstruction (PlaNet, Dreamer)rebuild the observationretains everything, including irrelevant detail
Contrastive (CURL)augmented views of the same state agreeinvariance to whatever you augmented
Self-predictive (SPR)predict your own future latentsdynamics-aware, no decoder, no negatives
Bisimulation (DBC)latent distance ≈ behavioural distanceprovably discards task-irrelevant variation
Value-equivalent (MuZero)predict r, v, πmaximally compact, reward-specific
Augmentation only (DrQ)none — just augment the inputoften matches the above

The DrQ result, which the field partly ignored

Kostrikov et al. showed that simply applying random crops and shifts to the input — with no representation-learning objective at all — matched or beat CURL and several contemporaries on the standard pixel-control benchmarks.

The implication is uncomfortable and worth stating: a substantial part of what was attributed to clever representation learning was regularisation. Run the augmentation-only baseline before believing a representation-learning result. This is the same lesson as DLinear in forecasting, in a different subfield.

Sufficiency, formally

Section titled “Sufficiency, formally”

Let ϕ:O∗→Z\phi: \mathcal{O}^* \to \mathcal{Z}ϕ:O∗→Z map histories to a representation. Define increasingly weak sufficiency conditions.

Observation-sufficient (predictive). For all histories h,h′h, h'h,h′:

ϕ(h)=ϕ(h′)  ⟹  P(ot+1:∞∣h, at:∞)=P(ot+1:∞∣h′, at:∞).\phi(h) = \phi(h') \implies P\big(o_{t+1:\infty}\mid h,\, a_{t:\infty}\big) = P\big(o_{t+1:\infty}\mid h',\, a_{t:\infty}\big).ϕ(h)=ϕ(h′)⟹P(ot+1:∞​∣h,at:∞​)=P(ot+1:∞​∣h′,at:∞​).

Reward-sufficient (model-irrelevance abstraction).

ϕ(s)=ϕ(s′)  ⟹  R(s,a)=R(s′,a) ∧ ∑s′′∈ϕ−1(z) ⁣ ⁣P(s′′∣s,a)= ⁣ ⁣∑s′′∈ϕ−1(z) ⁣ ⁣P(s′′∣s′,a)  ∀a,z.\phi(s) = \phi(s') \implies R(s,a) = R(s',a)\ \wedge\ \sum_{s''\in\phi^{-1}(z)}\!\!P(s''\mid s,a) = \!\!\sum_{s''\in\phi^{-1}(z)}\!\!P(s''\mid s',a)\ \ \forall a, z.ϕ(s)=ϕ(s′)⟹R(s,a)=R(s′,a) ∧ s′′∈ϕ−1(z)∑​P(s′′∣s,a)=s′′∈ϕ−1(z)∑​P(s′′∣s′,a)  ∀a,z.

QπQ^\piQπ-irrelevance. ϕ(s)=ϕ(s′)  ⟹  Qπ(s,a)=Qπ(s′,a)\phi(s)=\phi(s') \implies Q^\pi(s,a) = Q^{\pi}(s',a)ϕ(s)=ϕ(s′)⟹Qπ(s,a)=Qπ(s′,a) for all π,a\pi, aπ,a.

a∗a^*a∗-irrelevance. ϕ(s)=ϕ(s′)  ⟹  arg⁡max⁡aQ∗(s,a)=arg⁡max⁡aQ∗(s′,a)\phi(s)=\phi(s') \implies \arg\max_a Q^*(s,a) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s',a)ϕ(s)=ϕ(s′)⟹argmaxa​Q∗(s,a)=argmaxa​Q∗(s′,a).

Li, Walsh & Littman’s hierarchy:

observation⊂model-irrelevance⊂Qπ⊂Q∗⊂a∗.\text{observation} \subset \text{model-irrelevance} \subset Q^\pi \subset Q^* \subset a^* .observation⊂model-irrelevance⊂Qπ⊂Q∗⊂a∗.

Read the containments carefully. Each is a set of abstractions, and the larger sets contain coarser abstractions. So a∗a^*a∗-irrelevance permits the most aggressive merging — and their theorem is that value iteration converges to the optimal policy under model-irrelevance and Q∗Q^*Q∗-irrelevance abstractions, but not necessarily under a∗a^*a∗-irrelevance, because merging states with the same optimal action can break the Bellman backup. Aggressiveness has a limit and it is a precise one.

Belief states

Section titled “Belief states”

For a POMDP (S,A,O,T,O,R,γ)(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},T,O,R,\gamma)(S,A,O,T,O,R,γ), the belief update is

b′(s′)=O(o∣s′,a)∑sT(s′∣s,a) b(s)P(o∣b,a),P(o∣b,a)=∑s′O(o∣s′,a)∑sT(s′∣s,a)b(s).b'(s') = \frac{O(o\mid s',a)\sum_{s} T(s'\mid s,a)\,b(s)}{P(o\mid b,a)}, \qquad P(o\mid b,a) = \sum_{s'}O(o\mid s',a)\sum_s T(s'\mid s,a)b(s).b′(s′)=P(o∣b,a)O(o∣s′,a)∑s​T(s′∣s,a)b(s)​,P(o∣b,a)=s′∑​O(o∣s′,a)s∑​T(s′∣s,a)b(s).

The sufficiency theorem. btb_tbt​ is a sufficient statistic of the history for predicting the future — no additional information from o1:to_{1:t}o1:t​ improves prediction. So the POMDP over S\mathcal{S}S is exactly an MDP over the belief simplex Δ(S)\Delta(\mathcal{S})Δ(S).

And the catch. Δ(S)\Delta(\mathcal{S})Δ(S) is (∣S∣−1)(|S|-1)(∣S∣−1)-dimensional and continuous. The optimal value function over beliefs is piecewise linear and convex — representable as a set of α\alphaα-vectors:

V∗(b)=max⁡α∈Γ α⊤b,V^{*}(b) = \max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\ \alpha^{\top} b ,V∗(b)=α∈Γmax​ α⊤b,

but ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ grows doubly exponentially in the horizon in the worst case. Exact POMDP planning is PSPACE-complete for finite horizons and undecidable for infinite ones. This intractability is why the field learns approximate latent states instead, and an RSSM’s stochastic path is precisely a learned, compressed stand-in for btb_tbt​.

Predictive state representations

Section titled “Predictive state representations”

A test is t=a1o1a2o2⋯akokt = a_1o_1a_2o_2\cdots a_ko_kt=a1​o1​a2​o2​⋯ak​ok​. Its prediction given history hhh is

p(t∣h)=P(o1⋯ok∣h, a1⋯ak).p(t \mid h) = P\big(o_1\cdots o_k \mid h,\, a_1\cdots a_k\big).p(t∣h)=P(o1​⋯ok​∣h,a1​⋯ak​).

The system dynamics matrix DDD has rows indexed by histories, columns by tests, entries p(t∣h)p(t\mid h)p(t∣h). A set of tests Q={q1,…,qk}Q = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}Q={q1​,…,qk​} is a core set if its columns span the column space of DDD; then every test’s prediction is a function of the core predictions:

p(t∣h)=ft(p(q1∣h),…,p(qk∣h)),p(t \mid h) = f_t\big(p(q_1\mid h),\dots,p(q_k\mid h)\big),p(t∣h)=ft​(p(q1​∣h),…,p(qk​∣h)),

linear in the case of linear PSRs. The predictive state is p(h)=[p(q1∣h),…,p(qk∣h)]⊤p(h) = [p(q_1\mid h),\dots,p(q_k\mid h)]^\topp(h)=[p(q1​∣h),…,p(qk​∣h)]⊤, and it is updated in closed form after each action-observation pair:

pi(hao)=maoqi⊤ p(h)mao⊤ p(h).p_i(hao) = \frac{m_{aoq_i}^{\top}\,p(h)}{m_{ao}^{\top}\,p(h)} .pi​(hao)=mao⊤​p(h)maoqi​⊤​p(h)​.

The compactness result: the number of core tests equals rank⁡(D)\operatorname{rank}(D)rank(D), which is at most ∣S∣|S|∣S∣ and can be far smaller. So a PSR is never larger than the equivalent POMDP and is sometimes exponentially smaller.

The conceptual point, which is the durable one. State is defined by predictions about observables. There is no unobservable posited, so the representation is falsifiable against data. This is exactly the move a JEPA world model makes — define the latent by what it predicts — arrived at independently two decades earlier.

Successor representation and features

Section titled “Successor representation and features”

Tabular SR, as a matrix:

M=∑t=0∞γt Pt=(I−γP)−1,M = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,P^{t} = \big(I - \gamma P\big)^{-1},M=t=0∑∞​γtPt=(I−γP)−1,

the resolvent of the transition matrix. Value becomes V=MrV = M rV=Mr — linear in the reward. Change rrr, recompute in one product.

It obeys a Bellman equation of its own, so it is learnable by TD:

M(s,s′)←M(s,s′)+α[1{s=s′}+γM(st+1,s′)−M(s,s′)].M(s,s') \leftarrow M(s,s') + \alpha\Big[\mathbb{1}\{s = s'\} + \gamma M(s_{t+1}, s') - M(s,s')\Big].M(s,s′)←M(s,s′)+α[1{s=s′}+γM(st+1​,s′)−M(s,s′)].

Successor features. Assume r(s,a,s′)=ϕ(s,a,s′)⊤wr(s,a,s') = \phi(s,a,s')^{\top}wr(s,a,s′)=ϕ(s,a,s′)⊤w. Define

ψπ(s,a)=Eπ[∑t=0∞γtϕt+1 ∣ st=s,at=a],Qwπ(s,a)=ψπ(s,a)⊤w.\psi^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\phi_{t+1}\ \Big|\ s_t=s, a_t=a\right], \qquad Q^{\pi}_{w}(s,a) = \psi^{\pi}(s,a)^{\top} w .ψπ(s,a)=Eπ[t=0∑∞​γtϕt+1​ ​ st​=s,at​=a],Qwπ​(s,a)=ψπ(s,a)⊤w.

Generalised policy improvement. Given policies π1,…,πn\pi_1,\dots,\pi_nπ1​,…,πn​ with known ψπi\psi^{\pi_i}ψπi​, and a new reward www, the policy

π(s)=arg⁡max⁡a max⁡i ψπi(s,a)⊤w\pi(s) = \arg\max_a\ \max_i\ \psi^{\pi_i}(s,a)^{\top}wπ(s)=argamax​ imax​ ψπi​(s,a)⊤w

is guaranteed at least as good as every πi\pi_iπi​ on the new task — with no learning at all. That is a genuine transfer theorem, and it holds whenever the new reward is linear in ϕ\phiϕ.

The limitation is exactly that condition. Rewards outside the span of ϕ\phiϕ get no guarantee, and choosing ϕ\phiϕ is the unsolved part.

Bisimulation metrics

Section titled “Bisimulation metrics”
d(si,sj)=max⁡a∈A[∣R(si,a)−R(sj,a)∣+γ W1(P(⋅∣si,a), P(⋅∣sj,a); d)].d(s_i,s_j) = \max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big[\big|R(s_i,a) - R(s_j,a)\big| + \gamma\,W_1\big(P(\cdot\mid s_i,a),\ P(\cdot\mid s_j,a);\ d\big)\Big].d(si​,sj​)=a∈Amax​[​R(si​,a)−R(sj​,a)​+γW1​(P(⋅∣si​,a), P(⋅∣sj​,a); d)].

ddd appears on both sides — it is defined as the fixed point of an operator F\mathcal{F}F. Ferns et al. showed F\mathcal{F}F is a contraction with modulus γ\gammaγ, so by Banach the fixed point exists and is unique, and value iteration on the metric converges.

The value bound is what makes it useful:

∣V∗(si)−V∗(sj)∣  ≤  d(si,sj)1−γ.\big|V^{*}(s_i) - V^{*}(s_j)\big| \;\leq\; \frac{d(s_i,s_j)}{1-\gamma}.​V∗(si​)−V∗(sj​)​≤1−γd(si​,sj​)​.

States close in bisimulation distance have close optimal values, so collapsing them costs a bounded amount. This is a guarantee no reconstruction-based representation offers.

DBC’s practical version learns ϕ\phiϕ minimising

using a Gaussian latent dynamics model so the W2W_2W2​ term has a closed form. The encoder is trained so that latent distance equals behavioural distance — task- irrelevant variation is then provably compressed away, because it contributes nothing to either term.

Value equivalence, formally

Section titled “Value equivalence, formally”

For model m=(P,R)m = (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R})m=(P,R), policy set Π\PiΠ and value set V\mathcal{V}V, define

M(Π,V)={ m : Tmπv=Tm∗πv∀π∈Π, v∈V },\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\mathcal{V}) = \big\{\, m\ :\ \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m} v = \mathcal{T}^{\pi}_{m^{*}} v\quad \forall \pi\in\Pi,\ v\in\mathcal{V} \,\big\},M(Π,V)={m : Tmπ​v=Tm∗π​v∀π∈Π, v∈V},

the set of models value-equivalent to the true one.

Monotonicity. Π⊆Π′\Pi \subseteq \Pi'Π⊆Π′ and V⊆V′\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{V}'V⊆V′ imply M(Π′,V′)⊆M(Π,V)\mathcal{M}(\Pi',\mathcal{V}')\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\Pi,\mathcal{V})M(Π′,V′)⊆M(Π,V). Fewer constraints, more admissible models.

The two extremes are informative. With Π\PiΠ all policies and V\mathcal{V}V all value functions, M\mathcal{M}M contains only the true model — you are back to exact modelling. With small Π,V\Pi, \mathcal{V}Π,V, the set is large and contains models far simpler than the truth that nonetheless induce the same decisions.

So the practical claim is a capacity argument: with limited capacity, aim at the value-equivalent set rather than at the true model, because members of that set can be represented and the truth cannot. This is the formal statement of why MuZero’s latent is allowed to be unlike the world.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Self-predictive representations are the practical default. Predicting your own future latents — SPR, and the whole JEPA family — gives dynamics-aware representations without a decoder, without negatives, and without requiring reward. This converged from two directions: the RL representation literature and the self-supervised vision literature arrived at the same objective.

Bisimulation is the best theory and the weakest practice. The value bound is exactly the guarantee you want, and estimating Wasserstein distances between learned transition distributions from samples is noisy enough that DBC-style methods are finicky. Nobody has closed the gap between the theory’s cleanliness and the estimator’s variance.

Augmentation remains a suspiciously strong baseline. DrQ’s result has been replicated and is still not fully absorbed — many representation-learning papers in RL do not report an augmentation-only control, and they should.

Object-centric representations are stuck at the natural-image boundary. Slot Attention and successors work well on synthetic scenes and degrade on real ones, because “object” is not a well-posed notion outside curated data. This is the main obstacle to the compositional generalisation the approach was meant to deliver.

Successor features are having a quiet revival in the context of transfer and of reward-free pretraining, because the linear-reward assumption is much more palatable when ϕ\phiϕ is a large pretrained feature vector.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Choosing the abstraction level automatically. Nothing decides how much to discard. It is a design choice made by sweep, and the correct answer depends on a reward family you may not know in advance.

  • Reward-free representations that transfer. Value-equivalence needs reward; self-prediction does not but gives no guarantee about decision-relevance. A representation that is both reward-free and provably sufficient for control is not available.

  • The binding problem. Deciding which features belong to which object, in natural scenes, remains unsolved. Every object-centric method assumes a segmentation the world does not provide.

  • Estimating behavioural metrics from samples. Bisimulation distance requires comparing distributions; the estimators are high-variance in exactly the high-dimensional settings where the metric would be most useful.

  • Non-stationarity. All of this assumes a fixed environment. When dynamics change, a representation tuned to old dynamics is wrong, and there is no established way to detect that from the representation alone.

Choosing one

Section titled “Choosing one”

A decision procedure

Low-dimensional, well-understood system? Hand-engineered features. Genuinely. Do not learn what you can write down.

Partial observability with a known model? Belief state — it is the correct object. Approximate it if exact planning is intractable.

Pixels, one task, reward available? Value-equivalent or self-predictive, plus augmentation. Run the augmentation-only baseline first.

Pixels, many tasks, one environment? Successor features, if rewards are plausibly linear in your features. The transfer guarantee is real.

Distractors and task-irrelevant visual variation? Bisimulation-based, if you can make the estimator behave.

Reward-free data only? Self-predictive / JEPA. It is the only family that trains without reward and still learns dynamics.

Need compositional generalisation over objects? Object-centric — accepting that it currently works on clean scenes.
