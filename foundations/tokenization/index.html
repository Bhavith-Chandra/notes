Tokenization

A language model never sees text. It sees integers, and something upstream decided which integers. That decision is made once, before training, by a compression algorithm from 1994 that knows nothing about language — and it propagates into everything downstream: why models miscount letters, why some languages cost four times more to serve, why certain strings make models behave bizarrely, and why “how many r’s in strawberry” was ever hard.

What you should walk away with

  • The tokenizer is frozen before the model exists. It is fitted on a corpus, its vocabulary is fixed, and every downstream property inherits from it. You cannot fix a tokenization problem by training longer.
  • BPE is a compression algorithm, not a linguistic one. It merges frequent adjacent pairs. Nothing in it knows about morphemes, words or meaning — that it produces roughly morpheme-like units for English is a side effect of English statistics.
  • Models cannot see inside tokens. strawberry may be three tokens, none of which is a letter. Asking how many r’s it contains is asking about information the model never received, and this explains a whole family of “surprisingly stupid” failures.
  • Tokenizers are unfair across languages, measurably. The same sentence can take 1.5× to over 4× more tokens in some languages than in English — which directly multiplies cost, latency, and effective context length for those speakers.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The problem

Section titled “The problem”

Neural networks do arithmetic on numbers. Text is a sequence of characters. Something has to bridge that, and there are only three real options.

  1. One number per word. Small sequences, and the vocabulary is unbounded — there are always new words, names, typos and compounds. Anything unseen becomes <UNK> and its information is destroyed.

  2. One number per character. Vocabulary of ~100, nothing is ever unknown, and sequences become enormously long. Since attention is quadratic, long sequences are expensive.

  3. One number per frequent chunk. Common words stay whole; rare words split into pieces. Bounded vocabulary, moderate sequence length, nothing unrepresentable.

Option 3 is subword tokenization, and essentially every modern model uses it.

How the pieces are chosen

Section titled “How the pieces are chosen”

The dominant algorithm is byte-pair encoding, and it is startlingly simple.

Start with every character as its own token. Then repeat: find the most frequent adjacent pair in your corpus, and merge it into a single new token. Do that 50,000 times. The merges you performed, in order, are your tokenizer.

That is the whole algorithm. Notice what is absent — no grammar, no dictionary, no notion of a word or a morpheme. It is pure frequency counting, and it was invented in 1994 as a data compression algorithm, then repurposed for NLP two decades later.

Why it produces sensible-looking pieces anyway

Frequent character sequences in English are mostly morphemes — ing, tion, un, re recur constantly because English builds words from them. So BPE rediscovers morphology by counting, without representing it. That is elegant and it is also why the pieces get much worse for languages whose statistics differ.

What this costs you

Section titled “What this costs you”

Models cannot see inside a token. If berry is one token, the model receives one integer. The letters are not in there. Asking it to count the r’s in strawberry is asking about information that was discarded before the model saw anything.

This is not a reasoning failure. It is a representation failure, and it explains a cluster of otherwise baffling behaviours: character counting, reversing strings, rhyming, and some arithmetic.

Numbers tokenize badly. Depending on the tokenizer, 1234 might be 1234, or 123|4, or 12|34. The digit grouping is inconsistent and does not align with place value, so the model must learn arithmetic across pieces that do not respect the structure of arithmetic. Modern tokenizers often force single-digit splitting for exactly this reason.

Some languages cost far more. The tokenizer was fitted on a corpus that was mostly English. A language poorly represented in that corpus gets split into more, smaller pieces — so the same meaning costs more tokens, more money, more latency, and consumes more of the context window.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The three algorithms

Section titled “The three algorithms”
  • BPE
  • WordPiece
  • Unigram
  • SentencePiece
  1. Split the corpus into characters.
  2. Count every adjacent pair.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol; record the merge.
  4. Repeat until you reach the target vocabulary size.

Encoding applies the recorded merges in the same order — deterministic and fast.

Used by: GPT-2 onward, Llama, most open models.

Its known weakness is that greedy frequency is a poor proxy for usefulness. BPE will happily merge across a morpheme boundary because the pair is common, and the merge order is a hard commitment made once.

Same greedy structure, different scoring. Instead of raw frequency, pick the pair maximising

P(merged)P(first) P(second)\frac{P(\text{merged})}{P(\text{first})\,P(\text{second})}P(first)P(second)P(merged)​

which is the pointwise mutual information between the two pieces.

What this changes. Raw frequency favours pairs where either half is itself common. PMI favours pairs that co-occur more than chance would predict — pieces that genuinely belong together. th is extremely frequent and not especially informative; PMI down-weights it relative to a rarer but more cohesive pair.

Used by: BERT, DistilBERT, and the encoder-only family generally.

Inverts the procedure. Start with a large candidate vocabulary, define a probabilistic model over segmentations, and iteratively remove the pieces whose deletion loses the least likelihood.

What the inversion buys. You end with p(piece)p(\text{piece})p(piece) for every piece, so a given string has many possible segmentations, each with a probability. You can find the best one by Viterbi — or sample among them.

That sampling is subword regularisation: use a different segmentation of the same text on each training epoch. It is data augmentation for text, it measurably improves robustness, and BPE cannot do it because its segmentation is fixed.

Used by: T5, ALBERT, XLNet, and multilingual models generally.

Not an algorithm — an implementation that runs BPE or Unigram, and the thing that made tokenization language-agnostic.

The key design choice: treat the input as a raw stream including spaces, with the space encoded as a visible character (▁). Previous tokenizers pre-split on whitespace, which assumes the language has whitespace-delimited words. Japanese, Chinese and Thai do not.

The consequence is that detokenization becomes exactly reversible — concatenate the pieces and replace ▁ with a space. No language-specific rules, no lossy round-trip.

Byte-level fallback

Section titled “Byte-level fallback”

What happens when text contains a character the tokenizer never saw — an unusual emoji, a rare script, corrupted bytes?

The old answer was <UNK>, which destroys the information irrecoverably.

GPT-2’s answer was byte-level BPE: run BPE over raw UTF-8 bytes rather than Unicode characters. Since there are only 256 possible bytes and all of them are in the base vocabulary, every possible string is representable. There is no out-of-vocabulary case, by construction.

The cost. A character outside the tokenizer’s training distribution splits into its individual UTF-8 bytes — up to four tokens for one character, none of which is meaningful on its own. So coverage is universal and efficiency is not.

This is where multilingual unfairness comes from

Petrov et al. measured tokenized length for the same content across languages and found ratios exceeding 4× relative to English for some languages, with a long tail of worse cases.

That number is not abstract. API pricing is per token, latency scales with tokens, and context windows are measured in tokens. A speaker of a poorly-tokenized language pays several times more for the same conversation and gets a fraction of the effective context. It is a design decision made once, at tokenizer-fitting time, that produces a persistent cost asymmetry between language communities.

Glitch tokens

Section titled “Glitch tokens”

A genuinely strange failure mode, and instructive about how tokenizers and models interact.

The tokenizer is fitted on one corpus; the model is trained on another. If a string was frequent enough in the tokenizer’s corpus to earn a token, but essentially absent from the model’s training data, that token’s embedding is barely trained — it sits wherever initialisation put it.

Feed such a token to the model and behaviour becomes erratic: refusal to repeat it, hallucination, evasion, or unrelated output. The SolidGoldMagikarp case (a Reddit username that entered a tokenizer’s corpus via scraped data) became the canonical example.

Land & Bartolo turned this into a detection method — under-trained tokens can be found automatically by examining embedding statistics, without needing to discover them by accident. The general lesson: a mismatch between tokenizer corpus and model corpus leaves detectable dead weight in the vocabulary.

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

BPE, formally

Section titled “BPE, formally”

Complexity. Naively, recounting all pairs each step is O(N)O(N)O(N) for corpus length NNN, giving O(kN)O(kN)O(kN). The standard implementation maintains a priority queue over pair counts and updates only the counts affected by the merge, which is O(N+klog⁡N)O(N + k\log N)O(N+klogN) in practice — this is why fitting a tokenizer on a large corpus is minutes rather than hours.

Greedy, therefore not optimal. BPE maximises the immediate count reduction at each step, and the resulting vocabulary is not the one minimising total encoded length for its size. Finding that vocabulary is combinatorial, and nobody does it.

Encoding is a different algorithm from training. Training learns an ordered merge list; encoding applies it. Since the merges are applied in learned order, BPE encoding is deterministic and there is exactly one segmentation per string.

Training is EM over a fixed candidate set:

  1. E-step. Compute expected counts of each piece via the forward-backward algorithm over segmentation lattices.
  2. M-step. Set p(x)p(x)p(x) proportional to its expected count.
  3. Prune. For each piece, compute the loss ℓx\ell_xℓx​ — the drop in total corpus log-likelihood if xxx were removed and its occurrences re-segmented. Drop the bottom fraction by ℓx\ell_xℓx​.

BPE-dropout is the analogous trick for BPE: randomly skip merges during encoding with probability ppp. Cruder, and it recovers some of the benefit.

Fertility, and measuring a tokenizer

Section titled “Fertility, and measuring a tokenizer”

The standard metric is fertility — tokens per word:

F=number of tokensnumber of words.F = \frac{\text{number of tokens}}{\text{number of words}} .F=number of wordsnumber of tokens​.

F=1F = 1F=1 means every word is one token. English on a modern tokenizer sits around 1.3; poorly-supported languages can exceed 4.

Why compression is not the whole story. It is tempting to treat lower fertility as strictly better — fewer tokens, cheaper inference. Schmidt et al. tested this directly and found compression correlates with downstream performance only weakly. A tokenizer can compress well by merging across morpheme boundaries, producing pieces that are efficient and semantically incoherent.

What else matters, empirically: alignment of token boundaries with morpheme boundaries, consistency of a given word’s segmentation across contexts, and even distribution of token frequencies (a vocabulary where a few tokens dominate wastes capacity).

Vocabulary size, as a trade-off

Section titled “Vocabulary size, as a trade-off”

Let VVV be vocabulary size, ddd the model dimension, L(V)L(V)L(V) the expected sequence length. Two costs move in opposite directions:

embedding + unembedding params=2Vd⏟grows with Vattention cost∝L(V)2⏟shrinks with V\underbrace{\text{embedding + unembedding params} = 2Vd}_{\text{grows with } V} \qquad \underbrace{\text{attention cost} \propto L(V)^2}_{\text{shrinks with } V}grows with Vembedding + unembedding params=2Vd​​shrinks with Vattention cost∝L(V)2​​

Larger VVV means shorter sequences (cheaper attention, more content per context window) and more parameters spent on the vocabulary — parameters that see very sparse gradients, since rare tokens appear rarely.

The empirical sweet spot has moved upward over time: 30k–50k was standard around 2019, and 100k–260k is common now. The driver is multilinguality — covering many scripts requires more pieces — plus the observation that the softmax over VVV is a small share of total compute in a large model.

The sparse-gradient problem is real. A token appearing once per billion tokens receives essentially no gradient signal, and its embedding stays near initialisation. That is precisely the glitch-token mechanism, viewed from the optimisation side.

Why arithmetic is hard

Section titled “Why arithmetic is hard”

Consider adding two numbers. Place value requires aligning digits by position. If the tokenizer produces

1234→[ 123, 4 ],5678→[ 56, 78 ],1234 \to [\,\texttt{123},\ \texttt{4}\,], \qquad 5678 \to [\,\texttt{56},\ \texttt{78}\,],1234→[123, 4],5678→[56, 78],

the model must recover digit positions from tokens whose boundaries do not correspond to any place value, and the grouping differs between the two operands. The information is present but the structure is adversarial.

The fix is a tokenizer constraint, not a modelling one: force each digit to be its own token. Several modern tokenizers do this explicitly, and arithmetic performance improves measurably. Note what this means — a persistent “reasoning” weakness was substantially a preprocessing bug.

The same argument covers character-level tasks. Counting letters, reversing strings, and detecting rhymes all require sub-token information. The model can sometimes reconstruct it from context (it has seen spellings discussed in text) but it is inference, not perception.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Tokenizer-free models exist and have not displaced subwords. ByT5 operates on raw bytes and is genuinely more robust to noise, misspellings and unseen scripts — at a substantial compute cost, because byte sequences are several times longer and attention is quadratic.

Hierarchical byte models are the serious attempt. MEGABYTE splits the sequence into patches, runs a large model over patches and a small one within them, recovering most of the efficiency. Byte Latent Transformer (2024) goes further: patch boundaries are determined dynamically by the entropy of a small byte-level model — allocating more compute where the text is unpredictable and less where it is not. That is a genuinely different idea from a fixed vocabulary, and its scaling results are competitive with BPE-based models at matched compute.

Whether tokenization survives is a live question. The argument for removal is that every problem on this page is a tokenizer artefact. The argument against is that fixed vocabularies are enormously efficient and the efficiency gap has not closed. As of 2026 production models are overwhelmingly still subword-based.

Multilingual unfairness is acknowledged and partly addressed. Newer tokenizers are fitted on more balanced corpora and are measurably fairer than GPT-2-era ones. The asymmetry has narrowed and has not disappeared, and it is now standard to report fertility across languages rather than only aggregate compression.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No principled way to choose vocabulary size. It is swept. The interaction with model size, data volume and language coverage is not characterised.

  • Tokenizers cannot be changed after training. Improving the tokenizer means retraining from scratch, so a model is permanently stuck with a decision made before it existed. Vocabulary-transfer methods exist and are lossy.

  • Compression is a poor objective and we keep optimising it. Schmidt et al. showed the correlation with downstream quality is weak, and no better fitting objective has replaced it.

  • Domain mismatch. A tokenizer fitted on web text handles code, mathematics and protein sequences badly. Domain-specific tokenizers help and fragment the ecosystem.

  • Under-trained tokens are detectable and not routinely audited. The tooling exists; checking for them is not standard practice before release.

Practical guidance

Section titled “Practical guidance”

Working with tokenizers

Always inspect the actual tokenization of representative inputs before debugging model behaviour. Many “the model can’t do X” reports dissolve on looking at the token IDs.

Check fertility on your domain. If your text tokenizes at 3× the tokenizer’s design point, your costs and effective context are 3× worse than the headline numbers suggest.

For arithmetic-heavy work, prefer a model whose tokenizer splits digits individually. This is checkable in one line.

Never assume token count equals word count for pricing, context budgeting or truncation logic. Measure it.

If you fine-tune on a new domain, the tokenizer is unchanged — added vocabulary requires embedding surgery, and new tokens start untrained.
