Probability for ML

ML papers assume a narrow, specific slice of probability theory — not a course, about six ideas. This page covers exactly those: why almost every objective is an expectation, what KL divergence measures and why its asymmetry is a design choice, how to differentiate through a sampling step, and how variational bounds turn intractable quantities into optimisable ones. If you can read an ELBO derivation comfortably, skip this page.

What you should walk away with

  • Nearly every ML objective is an expectation you cannot compute. You have samples, not distributions, so every loss you actually optimise is a Monte Carlo estimate — and the variance of that estimate often matters more than its form.
  • KL divergence is not a distance, and its asymmetry is load-bearing. Forward KL makes your approximation cover everything; reverse KL makes it commit to one mode. Which one you use determines whether your model blurs or hallucinates.
  • The reparameterisation trick is the reason VAEs are trainable. Not the ELBO — the ELBO was known. Moving randomness onto a parameter-free variable cuts gradient variance by orders of magnitude.
  • A variational bound converts “I can’t compute this” into “I can optimise a lower bound”. The gap is itself a KL divergence, which tells you exactly when the bound is tight.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Everything is an average you can’t take

Section titled “Everything is an average you can’t take”

Almost every loss in machine learning has this shape:

L(θ)=Ex∼pdata[ℓ(x;θ)].\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ell(x; \theta)\big].L(θ)=Ex∼pdata​​[ℓ(x;θ)].

“The average loss over all possible data.” You cannot compute it — you do not have all possible data, and you do not know pdatap_{\text{data}}pdata​. You have a finite sample, so you compute

L^(θ)=1N∑i=1Nℓ(xi;θ),\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell(x_i; \theta),L^(θ)=N1​i=1∑N​ℓ(xi​;θ),

and hope it is close. That hope is what statistical learning theory formalises, and it is the reason minibatch sizes, variance, and estimator design keep coming up.

Comparing two distributions

Section titled “Comparing two distributions”

You have a true distribution and an approximation. How wrong is the approximation?

The standard answer is KL divergence: on average, how surprised are you when you predict with qqq and reality follows ppp? It is zero when they match and grows as they diverge.

It is not symmetric: KL(p∥q)≠KL(q∥p)\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q\|p)KL(p∥q)=KL(q∥p). That looks like a defect and is actually the most useful thing about it, because the two directions produce visibly different failure modes.

How to remember which is which

Look at where the expectation is taken. KL(p∥q)=Ep[log⁡p/q]\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log p/q]KL(p∥q)=Ep​[logp/q] averages under ppp, so it is punished wherever ppp has mass and qqq doesn’t — qqq must cover ppp. KL(q∥p)\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)KL(q∥p) averages under qqq, so it is punished wherever qqq has mass and ppp doesn’t — qqq must stay inside ppp. The distribution in the first slot is the one you must not miss.

Differentiating through randomness

Section titled “Differentiating through randomness”

Here is a problem that stops the obvious approach dead.

You want to train a network that samples — output a distribution, draw from it, do something with the sample. To train it you need the gradient of the loss with respect to the distribution’s parameters. But “draw a random number” has no derivative. The chain rule hits the sampling step and stops.

The fix is a change of variable. Instead of “sample zzz from a Gaussian with mean μ\muμ and std σ\sigmaσ”, write “sample ϵ\epsilonϵ from a standard normal, then compute z=μ+σϵz = \mu + \sigma\epsilonz=μ+σϵ”.

Same distribution. But now the randomness sits in ϵ\epsilonϵ, which has no parameters, and zzz is an ordinary differentiable function of μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ. Gradients flow straight through.

That is the reparameterisation trick, and it is the technical reason VAEs work.

Expectation and the two estimators

Section titled “Expectation and the two estimators”
Ex∼p[f(x)]=∫f(x) p(x) dx  ≈  1N∑i=1Nf(xi),xi∼p.\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \quad x_i \sim p .Ex∼p​[f(x)]=∫f(x)p(x)dx≈N1​i=1∑N​f(xi​),xi​∼p.

Unbiased, with variance Var⁡[f]/N\operatorname{Var}[f]/NVar[f]/N. The 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​ convergence is why enormous batches give diminishing returns.

When you need ∇θEpθ[f(x)]\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)]∇θ​Epθ​​[f(x)] — the parameters are in the distribution, not the function — there are exactly two standard routes.

Score function (REINFORCE). Using ∇θpθ=pθ∇θlog⁡pθ\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta∇θ​pθ​=pθ​∇θ​logpθ​:

∇θEpθ[f(x)]=Epθ[f(x) ∇θlog⁡pθ(x)].\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[f(x)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\big].∇θ​Epθ​​[f(x)]=Epθ​​[f(x)∇θ​logpθ​(x)].

Works for any pθp_\thetapθ​ you can sample and score, including discrete ones. Its variance is high, because f(x)f(x)f(x) multiplies the score without any information about how fff changes — which is why practical RL spends so much effort on baselines and control variates.

Pathwise (reparameterisation). If x=gθ(ϵ)x = g_\theta(\epsilon)x=gθ​(ϵ) with ϵ∼p(ϵ)\epsilon \sim p(\epsilon)ϵ∼p(ϵ) parameter-free:

∇θEpθ[f(x)]=Ep(ϵ)[∇θf(gθ(ϵ))]=Ep(ϵ)[f′ ⁣(gθ(ϵ)) ∇θgθ(ϵ)].\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\big[\nabla_\theta f\big(g_\theta(\epsilon)\big)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{p(\epsilon)}\big[f'\!\big(g_\theta(\epsilon)\big)\,\nabla_\theta g_\theta(\epsilon)\big].∇θ​Epθ​​[f(x)]=Ep(ϵ)​[∇θ​f(gθ​(ϵ))]=Ep(ϵ)​[f′(gθ​(ϵ))∇θ​gθ​(ϵ)].

Much lower variance, because the gradient of fff carries local information about the direction of improvement. Requires xxx to be a differentiable function of θ\thetaθ — so it does not apply to discrete variables without a relaxation like Gumbel-Softmax.

This is the real content of the reparameterisation trick

Both estimators are unbiased. The trick is not about correctness — it is about variance. In practice the pathwise estimator has variance orders of magnitude lower, and that gap is the difference between a VAE that trains and one that doesn’t.

KL divergence

Section titled “KL divergence”
KL(p ∥ q)=Ex∼p ⁣[log⁡p(x)q(x)]=∫p(x)log⁡p(x)q(x) dx.\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] = \int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx .KL(p∥q)=Ex∼p​[logq(x)p(x)​]=∫p(x)logq(x)p(x)​dx.

Properties worth having memorised:

  • KL(p∥q)≥0\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \geq 0KL(p∥q)≥0, with equality iff p=qp = qp=q almost everywhere. (Jensen applied to −log⁡-\log−log, which is convex.)
  • Not symmetric, and does not satisfy the triangle inequality — it is a divergence, not a metric.
  • KL(p∥q)=∞\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \inftyKL(p∥q)=∞ if q(x)=0q(x) = 0q(x)=0 where p(x)>0p(x) > 0p(x)>0. Absolute continuity is required, and violating it is a common source of NaN.

Closed form for diagonal Gaussians against a standard normal — the one you will actually type:

KL(N(μ,diag⁡(σ2)) ∥ N(0,I))=−12∑j=1k(1+log⁡σj2−μj2−σj2).\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(1 + \log\sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\Big).KL(N(μ,diag(σ2))∥N(0,I))=−21​j=1∑k​(1+logσj2​−μj2​−σj2​).

Note it is zero exactly when μ=0,σ=1\mu = 0, \sigma = 1μ=0,σ=1 — which is why an encoder that ignores its input scores perfectly on this term. That is posterior collapse, read straight off the formula.

Relation to cross-entropy. H(p,q)=H(p)+KL(p∥q)H(p, q) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\|q)H(p,q)=H(p)+KL(p∥q). Since H(p)H(p)H(p) does not depend on your parameters, minimising cross-entropy is minimising forward KL. Every classifier you have trained was doing variational inference in this sense.

Jensen’s inequality and where bounds come from

Section titled “Jensen’s inequality and where bounds come from”

For convex φ\varphiφ: φ(E[X])≤E[φ(X)]\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \leq \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]φ(E[X])≤E[φ(X)]; the inequality reverses for concave φ\varphiφ. Since log⁡\loglog is concave:

log⁡E[X]  ≥  E[log⁡X].\log \mathbb{E}[X] \;\geq\; \mathbb{E}[\log X].logE[X]≥E[logX].

This single line generates most variational bounds in ML. Whenever you see a log of an expectation you cannot compute, pushing the log inside gives a tractable lower bound.

Deriving the ELBO

Section titled “Deriving the ELBO”

The canonical application. You want log⁡pψ(x)\log p_\psi(x)logpψ​(x) but it requires marginalising a latent:

log⁡pψ(x)=log⁡∫pψ(x∣z) p(z) dz.\log p_\psi(x) = \log \int p_\psi(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz .logpψ​(x)=log∫pψ​(x∣z)p(z)dz.

Introduce any distribution qθ(z∣x)q_\theta(z \mid x)qθ​(z∣x) with matching support, multiply and divide:

=log⁡ Eqθ(z∣x) ⁣[pψ(x∣z) p(z)qθ(z∣x)]  ≥Jensen  Eqθ(z∣x) ⁣[log⁡pψ(x∣z) p(z)qθ(z∣x)],= \log\, \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\psi(x \mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\right] \;\overset{\text{Jensen}}{\geq}\; \mathbb{E}_{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p_\psi(x\mid z)\,p(z)}{q_\theta(z\mid x)}\right],=logEqθ​(z∣x)​[qθ​(z∣x)pψ​(x∣z)p(z)​]≥Jensen​Eqθ​(z∣x)​[logqθ​(z∣x)pψ​(x∣z)p(z)​], LELBO=Eqθ[log⁡pψ(x∣z)]⏟reconstruction  −  KL(qθ(z∣x) ∥ p(z))⏟regularisation.\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\theta}\big[\log p_\psi(x\mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularisation}}.LELBO​=reconstructionEqθ​​[logpψ​(x∣z)]​​−regularisationKL(qθ​(z∣x)∥p(z))​​.

How tight is it? Exactly:

log⁡pψ(x)−LELBO=KL(qθ(z∣x) ∥ pψ(z∣x)).\log p_\psi(x) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}} = \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\psi(z\mid x)\big).logpψ​(x)−LELBO​=KL(qθ​(z∣x)∥pψ​(z∣x)).

The gap is the KL between your approximate posterior and the true one. So the bound is tight precisely when your encoder recovers the true posterior — which tells you why a more expressive encoder helps, and gives you a target to improve.

  1. Write the intractable quantity as a log of an expectation.
  2. Introduce a tractable qqq by multiplying and dividing.
  3. Apply Jensen to push the log inside.
  4. Identify the gap as a KL, so you know when the bound is tight.

This recipe recurs: it produces the ELBO, the InfoNCE bound, and the information bottleneck’s variational form.

Mutual information

Section titled “Mutual information”
I(X;Y)=KL(p(x,y) ∥ p(x)p(y))=Ep(x,y) ⁣[log⁡p(x,y)p(x)p(y)]=H(X)−H(X∣Y).I(X;Y) = \mathrm{KL}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\right] = H(X) - H(X\mid Y).I(X;Y)=KL(p(x,y)∥p(x)p(y))=Ep(x,y)​[logp(x)p(y)p(x,y)​]=H(X)−H(X∣Y).

Symmetric, non-negative, zero iff independent. And hard to estimate — notoriously so in high dimensions, which is why the contrastive literature uses bounds rather than estimates.

The InfoNCE bound, from the same recipe:

I(X;Y)  ≥  log⁡N−LInfoNCE.I(X;Y) \;\geq\; \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} .I(X;Y)≥logN−LInfoNCE​.

Note the log⁡N\log NlogN ceiling: with N=8192N = 8192N=8192, the bound cannot exceed about 9 nats regardless of the true mutual information. The bound is loose at any realistic batch size, so arguments that lean on it as though it were an estimate are overclaiming. It explains why bigger batches help; it does not establish tightness.

Two more identities worth knowing

Section titled “Two more identities worth knowing”

Law of total expectation. E[X]=EY[E[X∣Y]]\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}_Y\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]E[X]=EY​[E[X∣Y]]. Used constantly to split an expectation over a latent.

Change of variables. For an invertible, differentiable fff with y=f(x)y = f(x)y=f(x):

pY(y)=pX(f−1(y))∣det⁡∂f−1∂y∣.p_Y(y) = p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\left|\det \frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial y}\right| .pY​(y)=pX​(f−1(y))​det∂y∂f−1​​.

This is the whole basis of normalising flows, and the reason they need architectures with tractable Jacobian determinants.

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Practical notes that matter more than they look

Section titled “Practical notes that matter more than they look”

Numerical stability is not optional.

  • Always work in log-space. log_softmax, logsumexp, log1p exist because their naive forms overflow.
  • Have the network output log⁡σ2\log \sigma^2logσ2, never σ\sigmaσ. This makes the positivity constraint automatic and keeps gradients well-scaled.
  • Add ϵ\epsilonϵ inside square roots and logs, not outside.

KL warmup. Training a VAE with the full KL term from step zero usually collapses the posterior — the KL is easy to minimise, reconstruction is not, so the optimiser takes the easy win. Annealing the KL weight from 0 to 1 over the first epochs, or “free bits” (allowing a fixed KL budget per dimension without penalty), both fix it. This is a standard trick and rarely stated in papers.

Sample count. VAEs typically use a single sample per datapoint to estimate the expectation in the ELBO. This works because the reparameterised estimator’s variance is low enough and minibatching averages over datapoints. It surprises people; it is deliberate.

What this page deliberately leaves out

Section titled “What this page deliberately leaves out”

Measure-theoretic foundations, most of the classical concentration inequalities, Bayesian model selection, and MCMC. Not because they are unimportant, but because you can read essentially any modern deep-learning paper without them, and this page is scoped to what the papers assume rather than to what a probability course covers.

If you want the full treatment, the standard references are Murphy’s Probabilistic Machine Learning for breadth and Bishop for the classical Bayesian material.
