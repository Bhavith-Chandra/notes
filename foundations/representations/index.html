Representations

“The network learns a representation” is the most-used and least-examined phrase in machine learning. This page takes it seriously: what is actually being claimed, how you would check it, and why the two obvious ways of checking — read the neurons, train a linear probe — are both misleading. The honest answer is that we can demonstrate representations are useful far more easily than we can say what they contain.

What you should walk away with

  • A representation is a re-coordinatisation, not a compression. The point is not that the vector is smaller; it is that the geometry of the new space aligns with the structure of the problem, so that distance means similarity and simple decision boundaries suffice.
  • Neurons are the wrong unit of analysis. Networks represent more features than they have dimensions, by storing them in overlapping non-orthogonal directions. This is superposition, and it explains why individual neurons look polysemantic.
  • Linear probes measure the probe as much as the representation. High probe accuracy proves information is linearly accessible. Low accuracy proves nothing at all about whether the information is there.
  • Different models converge on similar representations. Larger, better models trained on different data and even different modalities appear to align increasingly well — a striking empirical regularity with no settled explanation.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Coordinates that make the problem easy

Section titled “Coordinates that make the problem easy”

Take a map of a city drawn with north up. Now redraw it with the main road as the horizontal axis. Nothing about the city changed; every building sits where it did. But questions like “is this address on the near side of the road?” went from requiring geometry to requiring a glance at one number.

That is what a representation is. A re-description of the same data in coordinates chosen so that the questions you care about become easy.

Notice what the definition does not say. It says nothing about being smaller. You can represent something in more dimensions than you started with and it can still be a better representation — kernel methods do exactly this. The goal is alignment between the geometry and the task, not compression.

The classic demonstration

Section titled “The classic demonstration”

Train a model to predict nearby words, and the resulting word vectors have a property nobody built in: king − man + woman lands near queen. Directions in the space correspond to concepts — one direction is roughly gender, another roughly plurality, another roughly tense.

Nothing in the training objective mentioned gender. The structure fell out of the requirement to predict context, because words that behave alike in sentences end up nearby, and systematic differences between groups of words end up as systematic displacements.

This example is real and heavily oversold

The analogy arithmetic works far less reliably than the famous examples suggest. Later analyses showed a large share of the effect depends on excluding the three query words from the nearest-neighbour search — without that exclusion the answer is often just king again. Treat it as an illustration that structure can emerge, not as evidence about how cleanly it does.

Why “what does neuron 4,217 mean?” is the wrong question

Section titled “Why “what does neuron 4,217 mean?” is the wrong question”

The tempting picture is one neuron per concept: a cat neuron, a curve neuron, a sarcasm neuron. Interpretability work found some of these, which made the picture more tempting.

It is mostly wrong, for a reason that is simple once stated. A language model has maybe 4,000 dimensions in its residual stream and needs to represent vastly more than 4,000 concepts. It cannot give each one a dimension. What it does instead is give each concept a direction, and pack in far more directions than dimensions by allowing them to be slightly non-orthogonal.

This works because the features are sparse — of the millions of things a model could be tracking, only a handful are present in any given input, so the interference between overlapping directions rarely matters.

The cost is that a single neuron participates in many features and looks incoherent when you inspect it. Neurons look polysemantic because they are the wrong basis, not because the model is confused.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Three things “representation” can mean

Section titled “Three things “representation” can mean”

Distinguishing these prevents most of the confused arguments in this area.

SenseClaimHow you’d check
Decodablethe information is recoverable from the activationstrain a probe
Usedthe model’s output actually depends on itablate or patch it, watch behaviour
Structuredit lives in a geometrically meaningful form (a direction, a subspace)steer along it, check for linear separability

These come apart constantly. A model can decodably contain the author’s nationality while never using it. A feature can be used without being structured as a single direction. Papers frequently establish the first and claim the third.

Probing, and how to read it

Section titled “Probing, and how to read it”

Freeze the network, take activations from some layer, train a small classifier for some property, report accuracy.

  1. Pick a layer and extract activations for a labelled dataset.
  2. Train a probe — usually linear, sometimes an MLP.
  3. Report held-out accuracy against a baseline.

Two asymmetric conclusions, and getting them the wrong way round is the standard error:

  • High accuracy ⟹ the information is linearly accessible. Solid conclusion.
  • Low accuracy ⟹ ??? Could be that the information is absent. Could be that it is encoded non-linearly. Could be that your probe is under-trained or your dataset is small. Low probe accuracy is nearly uninformative.

The capacity confound

A sufficiently expressive probe can extract almost anything from almost any representation — including from random features, which are a surprisingly strong baseline. If your probe is an MLP, you may be measuring the probe’s ability to learn the task rather than the representation’s contribution. Always report the random-features baseline; a result that doesn’t beat it says nothing.

Superposition, concretely

Section titled “Superposition, concretely”

The idea in one sentence: a network with nnn dimensions can represent m≫nm \gg nm≫n features by assigning each a direction and accepting small interference.

Elhage et al.’s toy models made the conditions precise. Superposition appears when:

  • features are sparse (rarely co-occur), and
  • the model has more features to represent than dimensions, and
  • the loss rewards representing more features imperfectly over fewer perfectly.

Under those conditions the model organises features into geometric arrangements — antipodal pairs, triangles, pentagons — that minimise interference for the given count. These are visible in the toy models and are not an artefact.

The practical consequence is that recovering interpretable features means finding an overcomplete, sparse basis rather than reading the neuron basis. That is what sparse autoencoders do: train a wide autoencoder with an ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty on the hidden layer, so it learns many more features than the layer has dimensions, each active rarely. The recovered features are far more monosemantic than neurons.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

Definition

Section titled “Definition”

A representation is a map Φ:X→Z\Phi: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Z}Φ:X→Z. It is good for a task family G\mathcal{G}G if some simple g∈Gg \in \mathcal{G}g∈G composed with Φ\PhiΦ achieves low risk:

R(Φ;G)=min⁡g∈G  E(x,y)[ℓ(g(Φ(x)),y)].R(\Phi;\mathcal{G}) = \min_{g \in \mathcal{G}}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[\ell\big(g(\Phi(x)), y\big)\big].R(Φ;G)=g∈Gmin​E(x,y)​[ℓ(g(Φ(x)),y)].

The quantifier structure carries the content. Quality is defined relative to a hypothesis class G\mathcal{G}G. Take G\mathcal{G}G to be all measurable functions and the identity map is optimal, so the definition becomes vacuous. Restrict G\mathcal{G}G to linear maps and you recover linear probing, with all its limitations. There is no representation quality independent of what you plan to do with it, and this is the formal root of the evaluation problem.

The information bottleneck, and the argument about it

Section titled “The information bottleneck, and the argument about it”

Tishby’s framing: find ZZZ minimising

LIB=I(Z;X)−β I(Z;Y),\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}} = I(Z; X) - \beta\, I(Z; Y),LIB​=I(Z;X)−βI(Z;Y),

compressing XXX while preserving what predicts YYY. The endpoints are instructive: β→0\beta \to 0β→0 gives ZZZ constant (all compression), β→∞\beta \to \inftyβ→∞ gives Z=XZ = XZ=X (no compression).

Tishby & Zaslavsky (2015) proposed that deep networks traverse an information plane in two phases — a fitting phase where I(Z;Y)I(Z;Y)I(Z;Y) rises, then a longer compression phase where I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) falls — and argued the compression phase explains generalisation.

Saxe et al. (2018) contested this substantially, and the criticism is worth knowing because it is a good case study in measurement artefacts:

  • The observed compression depended on using saturating nonlinearities. ReLU networks often showed no compression phase while generalising fine.
  • I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) is infinite for deterministic networks with continuous inputs. The measured quantity depended entirely on the binning scheme used to estimate it, and the “compression” partly tracked activations spreading across bins.
  • Compression and generalisation could be decoupled experimentally.

How to hold the IB

As a framing — a representation trades off retaining input information against retaining task-relevant information — it is genuinely clarifying and organises the whole encoder literature. As an empirical theory of why deep learning generalises, it did not survive scrutiny. Both statements are true at once, and papers citing Tishby often blur them.

Probing, formally

Section titled “Probing, formally”
accG(Φ,y)=max⁡g∈G E[1{g(Φ(x))=y}].\text{acc}_{\mathcal{G}}(\Phi, y) = \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}}\ \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{1}\{g(\Phi(x)) = y\}\big].accG​(Φ,y)=g∈Gmax​ E[1{g(Φ(x))=y}].

Two properties, in tension:

Monotone in G\mathcal{G}G. If G1⊆G2\mathcal{G}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{G}_2G1​⊆G2​ then accG1≤accG2\text{acc}_{\mathcal{G}_1} \leq \text{acc}_{\mathcal{G}_2}accG1​​≤accG2​​. Bigger probes always score at least as high — so probe accuracy alone cannot be compared across papers using different probes.

Not monotone in information. Φ1\Phi_1Φ1​ can contain strictly more information than Φ2\Phi_2Φ2​ about yyy while probing worse, if Φ2\Phi_2Φ2​ has it in a linearly accessible form. Probing measures accessibility, and accessibility is not information.

The proposed fixes each have costs. Control tasks (Hewitt & Liang) probe for a random relabelling and report selectivity — the gap between real-task and control accuracy — which corrects for probe capacity. Minimum description length probing scores how quickly the probe learns rather than its final accuracy, which penalises probes that memorise. Both are better than raw accuracy and neither is standard.

Section titled “Superposition, formally”

Model a layer as recovering mmm sparse features from n<mn < mn<m dimensions. With feature vector f∈Rmf \in \mathbb{R}^mf∈Rm (kkk-sparse) and dictionary W∈Rn×mW \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}W∈Rn×m:

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Sparse autoencoders are the main interpretability tool and they have known problems. They recover far more monosemantic features than the neuron basis, and they also suffer feature splitting — increase the dictionary size and one feature resolves into several finer ones, with no principled stopping point. The number of “true” features is not defined by the method, and reconstruction error versus sparsity is a trade-off curve rather than an optimum.

The linear representation hypothesis holds better than expected but not universally. Many concepts are single directions, and steering along them works. But circular and multi-dimensional representations have been found — days of the week arranged on a circle, for instance — so “features are directions” is a good default rather than a law.

Representational convergence is a live and surprising result. Huh et al.’s Platonic Representation Hypothesis observes that as models get larger and more capable, their representation spaces align increasingly well across architectures and even across modalities. The proposed explanation — that all are approximating the same underlying statistical structure of reality — is speculative. The measurements are real and worth watching.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No task-agnostic quality measure, and the formalism says there cannot be one. Quality is defined relative to G\mathcal{G}G. This is not a gap waiting to be filled; it is a property of the definition, and the field mostly proceeds as if it weren’t.

  • Feature splitting has no principled resolution. SAE dictionary size is a free parameter that changes what counts as a feature.

  • Decodable ≠ used. Probing tells you information is present. Establishing the model uses it requires causal intervention, which is more work and much rarer in published results.

  • Cross-model comparison is unsolved. Two models trained identically from different seeds produce incomparable spaces. Methods exist (CKA, SVCCA, Procrustes) and they disagree with each other, which is not reassuring.

  • We cannot predict what a training setup will represent. Given an architecture, data, and objective, nothing forecasts which features emerge. Every finding here is post-hoc.

What to read next, in order

Section titled “What to read next, in order”
  1. Elhage et al. (2022), Toy Models of Superposition — the most important conceptual contribution here in a decade. Read the whole thing; it is well-written and the interactive figures carry the argument.
  2. Alain & Bengio (2016) — short, and it inoculates you against over-reading probes.
  3. Saxe et al. (2018) — read alongside Tishby & Zaslavsky, in that order. A model example of how a compelling theory meets careful measurement.
  4. Huh et al. (2024), Platonic Representation Hypothesis — for the convergence result, held at appropriate arm’s length.
