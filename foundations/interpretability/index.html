Interpretability

Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse-engineer what a network actually computes — not which inputs correlate with which outputs, but the algorithm implemented in the weights. It has produced genuine results: induction heads, factual recall localised to specific MLP layers, millions of interpretable features extracted from a production model. It has also run into a structural problem — features do not live in neurons — that shapes everything the field currently does.

What you should walk away with

  • Correlation is not mechanism. A neuron firing on cats does not mean the model uses it to detect cats. Establishing that requires intervention — change the activation, watch the output — which is why causal methods dominate.
  • The neuron basis is the wrong basis. Superposition means a model represents more features than it has dimensions, packed as overlapping directions. Individual neurons therefore look polysemantic, and reading them is a category error.
  • Sparse autoencoders find the right basis, at a price. Train a wide, sparse autoencoder on activations and you recover far more monosemantic features. But dictionary size is a free parameter, and increasing it splits features indefinitely with no principled stopping point.
  • Induction heads are the field’s best result. A two-head circuit that finds a previous occurrence of the current token and predicts what followed it. Mechanistically characterised, causally verified, and correlated with the phase change where in-context learning appears.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Two questions that get confused

Section titled “Two questions that get confused”

“What makes this model output X?” is a question about behaviour. You can answer it with correlations — find inputs that produce X, look for patterns.

“How does this model compute X?” is a question about mechanism. It asks what algorithm the weights implement, and it cannot be answered by correlation at all.

Mechanistic interpretability is the second question. The distinction matters because the first is much easier and is routinely reported as though it answered the second.

Why looking at neurons fails

Section titled “Why looking at neurons fails”

The intuitive approach is to find inputs that make a neuron fire, and name the neuron after them.

This runs into two problems, and the second is fatal.

Correlation without causation. A neuron might reliably fire on images of cats while playing no role in the model’s cat classification — it could be a byproduct of something else that happens to co-occur. To know it matters you have to intervene: clamp it and see whether behaviour changes.

Polysemanticity. Most neurons respond to several unrelated things — a neuron that fires on cat faces, the front of cars, and legal citations. This looks like confusion and is not. A model with 4,000 dimensions needs to represent far more than 4,000 concepts, so it assigns each concept a direction and packs in more directions than dimensions, accepting slight overlap.

This works because concepts are sparse — very few are active at once — so the interference rarely bites. The cost is that any single neuron sits at the intersection of many feature directions, and reading it shows a mixture.

The reframing that organises the field

Features are directions, not neurons. The neuron basis is arbitrary — an accident of how the network was parameterised. Interpretability’s central technical problem is finding the right basis, and sparse autoencoders are the current best attempt at doing so automatically.

What a circuit is

Section titled “What a circuit is”

A circuit is a subgraph of the network that implements an identifiable computation — a small set of attention heads and MLP layers, and the specific way information flows between them, that together perform some function.

The canonical example is the induction head, and it is worth stating precisely because it is the field’s cleanest result.

Given text like ... Ms Dursley ... Ms D, the model should predict ursley. Two heads, in two different layers, cooperate:

  1. A previous-token head in an early layer writes, at each position, information about the token that came before it.
  2. An induction head in a later layer looks for earlier positions whose “previous token” matches the current token, then copies whatever came after those positions.

That is a complete algorithm — match-and-copy — implemented in attention weights, verified by intervention, and it appears in essentially every transformer above a certain size.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Activation patching

Section titled “Activation patching”

The workhorse method, and the reason findings can claim causality rather than correlation.

  1. Clean run. Prompt that produces the behaviour. Cache every activation.
  2. Corrupted run. Minimally different prompt that changes the answer.
  3. Patched run. Run corrupted, but overwrite one component with its cached clean value.
  4. Measure recovery. How much of the clean behaviour returns.
  5. Sweep. Repeat for every layer and position to localise the computation.

The choice of corruption is a modelling decision. Swapping a token, adding noise to embeddings, or using a different prompt entirely all define different counterfactuals, and they can give different answers. Zhang & Nanda showed that metric and corruption choices materially change conclusions — this is a live methodological problem, not a settled recipe.

Sparse autoencoders

Section titled “Sparse autoencoders”

If features are directions and there are more of them than dimensions, you need an overcomplete basis. Train a wide autoencoder on the model’s activations with a sparsity penalty:

  • The method
  • Feature splitting
  • Open dictionaries

Take activations xxx from some layer. Train an encoder into a much wider space (8× to 100× the layer width) with an ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty forcing few features active, then a decoder reconstructing xxx.

The learned dictionary directions turn out to be far more monosemantic than neurons. Scaling Monosemanticity extracted millions of features from a production model — features for the Golden Gate Bridge, for code errors, for sycophantic praise, for internal conflict — and demonstrated they are causal by steering: clamp a feature high and the model’s behaviour changes accordingly.

The unresolved problem. Increase the dictionary size and a feature resolves into several finer ones: “bird” becomes “bird”, then “seabird” and “raptor”, then specific species.

None of these is wrong. There is no principled number of features, and no criterion telling you when to stop. Reconstruction error versus sparsity is a trade-off curve, not an optimum — so “we found N features” is a statement about your hyperparameter as much as about the model.

Gemma Scope released SAEs trained on every layer of an open model, which changed the field’s accessibility — before it, this work required either training your own dictionaries at significant cost or working inside a frontier lab.

Attribution graphs

Section titled “Attribution graphs”

The 2025 development. Rather than analysing one component, use the SAE features as nodes and trace the computational graph between them for a specific prompt — which features activated, which caused which, and how they combined to produce the output.

This moved the field from “here is an interesting component” to “here is the computation this model performed on this input”, and it surfaced behaviours worth knowing about: planning ahead in poetry (the model selects a rhyme before writing the line leading to it), and cases where the stated chain-of-thought does not match the computation actually performed.

Motivated reasoning, caught mechanistically

The circuit-tracing work found instances where a model produces a plausible argument for an answer it had already arrived at by other means — the reasoning shown to the user was not the reasoning that determined the output. This matters directly for anyone treating chain-of-thought as an explanation. It is a report of the process, and the report is not always accurate.

The residual stream decomposition

Section titled “The residual stream decomposition”

Elhage et al.’s framework starts from writing a transformer’s computation as a sum of paths. The residual stream at layer ℓ\ellℓ is

For an attention-only model this becomes an explicit sum over paths from input to output, each path a product of the head matrices along it. The decomposition separates each head into two circuits:

Induction heads, formally

Section titled “Induction heads, formally”

A two-layer composition. Let the current token at position ttt be AAA, and suppose the sequence contains an earlier occurrence [A][B] at positions j,j+1j, j+1j,j+1.

Layer 1 — previous-token head. Attends from each position to the one before, so position j+1j+1j+1 accumulates information about token AAA:

xj+1+=WOV(1) e(A).x_{j+1} \mathrel{+}= W_{OV}^{(1)}\, e(A).xj+1​+=WOV(1)​e(A).

Layer 2 — induction head. Its query at position ttt is built from e(A)e(A)e(A); its key at position j+1j+1j+1 was built from the AAA-information written in layer 1. So the QK score

(WQ(2)e(A))⊤(WK(2)WOV(1)e(A))\big(W_Q^{(2)} e(A)\big)^{\top}\big(W_K^{(2)} W_{OV}^{(1)} e(A)\big)(WQ(2)​e(A))⊤(WK(2)​WOV(1)​e(A))

is large exactly at positions following a previous AAA. The head then copies via WOV(2)W_{OV}^{(2)}WOV(2)​ the token at that position — which is BBB.

This is K-composition: layer 2’s keys are constructed from layer 1’s output rather than from the embedding directly. The algorithm is match-and-copy, it is readable off the weights, and it is causally verified by ablation.

Why it matters beyond itself. Olsson et al. showed induction heads form during a narrow window of training that coincides with a visible bump in the loss curve and with the sudden appearance of in-context learning. That is a mechanistic account of an emergent capability — currently the field’s strongest such result.

Two details that are easy to skip and matter:

The ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ term is weighted by decoder column norms. Without it, the model shrinks hhh and grows WdW_dWd​ to cheat the penalty while leaving the reconstruction unchanged. Either this weighting or an explicit unit-norm constraint on decoder columns is required.

Subtracting bdb_dbd​ before encoding centres the input, which measurably improves feature quality — activations have a large mean component that would otherwise be absorbed into many features.

The fundamental tension. Raising λ\lambdaλ gives sparser, more interpretable features and worse reconstruction; lowering it does the reverse. Since there is no ground truth for “the right number of features”, the operating point is chosen by inspection. This is the feature-splitting problem in its formal dress.

Variants — TopK SAEs that fix the active count directly, JumpReLU with a learned threshold — address the ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ penalty’s tendency to shrink activations, and none resolves the dictionary-size question.

Causal mediation

Section titled “Causal mediation”

Activation patching is causal mediation analysis applied to networks. For a mediator MMM (some component) and treatment TTT (clean vs corrupted input), the indirect effect through MMM is

IE=E[Y(T=0, M=M(T=1))]−E[Y(T=0)],\text{IE} = \mathbb{E}\big[Y\big(T=0,\ M = M(T{=}1)\big)\big] - \mathbb{E}\big[Y(T=0)\big],IE=E[Y(T=0, M=M(T=1))]−E[Y(T=0)],

i.e. run with the corrupted input but MMM set to its clean value. That is exactly the patched run.

The direct effect holds MMM fixed and varies the input, and the two decompose the total effect. Reporting both distinguishes a component that carries the information from one that merely correlates with it.

Meng et al.’s ROME used this to localise factual associations to mid-layer MLPs at the subject token position — and then went further, editing the MLP’s weights with a rank-one update to change the stored fact. That the edit works is strong evidence the localisation was correct: prediction, intervention, confirmation.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Sparse autoencoders scaled to production models and the dictionary problem remains. Millions of interpretable features extracted from a frontier model, demonstrably causal via steering. Feature splitting means the feature count is still a hyperparameter, and there is no principled criterion for stopping.

Attribution graphs moved the unit of analysis from components to computations. Tracing feature-to-feature causation on specific prompts produces something much closer to “what the model did” than any previous method. It is labour-intensive and currently applied to selected examples rather than at scale.

The linear representation hypothesis mostly holds, with exceptions. Many concepts are single directions and steering along them works. Circular representations — days of the week, months — have been documented, so “features are directions” is a strong default rather than a law.

Chain-of-thought is not reliably an explanation. Mechanistic work has caught models constructing post-hoc justifications for answers reached otherwise. Anyone using reasoning traces as evidence of a model’s process should hold that finding in view.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No principled dictionary size. Feature splitting continues indefinitely as you expand. Nothing says how many features a model “has”.

  • Coverage is unknown. SAEs reconstruct activations imperfectly, and what the residual contains is not characterised. Absence of a feature in the dictionary is not evidence of its absence in the model.

  • It does not scale by hand. Circuit analysis is skilled manual work per behaviour. Automating it — using models to interpret models — is the obvious move and is not yet reliable.

  • Interpretability illusions. A component can appear to implement a clean algorithm on your chosen distribution and behave differently off it. Findings validated on narrow prompt sets frequently do not generalise.

  • The gap to safety applications is real. The stated goal is detecting deception, hidden goals, and dangerous capabilities. Current methods find features and small circuits; that is some distance from auditing a model’s intentions, and the field is generally honest about the gap.

What to read next, in order

Section titled “What to read next, in order”
  1. Elhage et al. (2021), Mathematical Framework — the QK/OV decomposition. The foundation everything else builds on, and it repays slow reading.
  2. Olsson et al. (2022), Induction Heads — the field’s cleanest result, including the training-dynamics correlation.
  3. Elhage et al. (2022), Toy Models of Superposition — why the neuron basis is wrong, argued from a solvable model.
  4. Templeton et al. (2024), Scaling Monosemanticity — SAEs on a production model, with steering experiments that establish causality.
  5. Lindsey et al. (2025), On the Biology of a Large Language Model — attribution graphs applied to real behaviours. The most concrete picture available of what a large model does internally.
