Evaluation

Every claim in machine learning rests on a measurement, and the measurements are in worse shape than the models. Benchmarks leak into training data, the standard comparison is reported without error bars, and the metric you choose can manufacture a discontinuity that is not in the underlying model. None of this means evaluation is hopeless — it means reading a number requires knowing how it was produced.

What you should walk away with

  • Contamination is the default assumption, not the exception. If a benchmark predates a model’s training cutoff and exists on the public internet, assume some of it was seen. The burden of proof is on the claim of cleanliness.
  • A 1-point difference on a 1,000-item benchmark is noise. The standard error is around 1.5 points. Most reported model comparisons do not clear their own error bars, and almost none report them.
  • Emergence may be a property of your metric. Exact-match accuracy is discontinuous by construction; swap to a continuous metric and many “sudden” capability jumps become smooth curves. The models may not be doing anything abrupt.
  • LLM judges are cheap, correlate well with humans, and share their biases. They prefer longer answers, favour the first option presented, and rate their own family’s outputs higher. Optimising against a judge is reward hacking one level up.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The exam you accidentally published

Section titled “The exam you accidentally published”

You write an exam to test students. You post it online with the answer key so others can use it. Next year’s students study from the internet.

They ace your exam. Did they learn the subject, or did they memorise your paper?

This is the situation every public benchmark is in. MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K — all published, all scraped, all plausibly inside the training corpus of any model released afterwards. Their scores are some mixture of capability and recall, and the mixture is not measurable from the outside.

Why one number is not enough

Section titled “Why one number is not enough”

“Model A scores 87.3, model B scores 86.9.”

Before treating that as A winning, three questions:

How many items? On a 1,000-question benchmark, the standard error on a score near 87% is about 1.1 points. A 0.4-point gap is well inside the noise.

How was it prompted? Few-shot examples, their order, the answer format, and whether chain-of-thought was allowed can move scores by several points — more than most reported differences between models.

Was it contaminated? If A’s training data included the test set and B’s did not, you have measured data curation, not capability.

The reasonable default

Treat differences under about two points on a standard benchmark as indistinguishable unless the paper reports confidence intervals and a matched prompting protocol. Most do not. This is not cynicism — it is what the sample size supports.

The emergence argument

Section titled “The emergence argument”

A widely-cited observation: some capabilities appear suddenly at scale. Below some model size, near-zero performance; above it, the ability appears.

Schaeffer et al. proposed a deflationary explanation worth taking seriously. Consider a task scored by exact match on a five-digit answer. If per-digit accuracy improves smoothly from 0.70 to 0.95, exact-match accuracy goes

0.705=0.17⟶0.955=0.77,0.70^5 = 0.17 \quad\longrightarrow\quad 0.95^5 = 0.77,0.705=0.17⟶0.955=0.77,

but the curve in between is sharply non-linear — it looks like a phase transition because the metric is a product of five smooth terms, not because anything discontinuous happened inside the model.

Swap to per-digit accuracy or log-likelihood and the sharpness vanishes.

This does not prove emergence is never real. It shows that a discontinuous metric can manufacture the appearance of one, so the burden falls on the claimant to show the discontinuity survives a continuous measure.

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The failure modes

Section titled “The failure modes”

Contamination, and how to detect it

Section titled “Contamination, and how to detect it”

The problem is straightforward and the detection is not, because you generally do not have access to the training corpus.

  1. N-gram overlap. Search the training data for verbatim spans from the test set. Only possible with open data, and defeated by paraphrase.

  2. Canary strings. Benchmarks embed a unique GUID and ask trainers to filter on it. Depends entirely on good faith and on the filter running.

  3. Memorisation probes. Give the model the first half of a test item and see whether it completes the second half verbatim. Suggestive rather than conclusive.

  4. Temporal split. Evaluate only on items created after the model’s training cutoff. The most reliable method available, and it means benchmarks have a shelf life.

  5. Perturbation. Rewrite items while preserving the answer. A large drop suggests the model was matching surface form rather than reasoning.

The structural fix is dynamic benchmarks — continuously refreshed with new items, so there is no fixed set to memorise. SWE-bench is a partial example: it draws from real GitHub issues, and new issues keep appearing.

LLM judges

Section titled “LLM judges”

Using a strong model to score outputs is cheap, fast, and correlates with human preference at roughly 80% agreement — which is close to the agreement rate between humans.

The biases are documented and consistent:

BiasEffectMitigation
Lengthprefers longer responses regardless of qualitycontrol for length; report length alongside score
Positionprefers whichever option came firstevaluate both orders, average
Self-preferencerates its own family’s outputs highernever judge with a model related to one under test
Style over substanceconfident formatting beats correct contentuse reference answers where possible

Judges are a proxy, and proxies get gamed

This is the same structure as reward hacking. If you optimise a model against a judge, you will find the judge’s blind spots — verbosity and confident formatting most reliably. A judge used for measurement is reasonable; a judge used as a training signal needs the same scepticism you would apply to any learned reward model.

Confidence intervals, which almost nobody reports

Section titled “Confidence intervals, which almost nobody reports”

Benchmark accuracy is a binomial proportion. For nnn items and observed accuracy p^\hat pp^​:

SE⁡(p^)=p^(1−p^)n,95% CI≈p^±1.96 SE⁡.\operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p (1 - \hat p)}{n}}, \qquad \text{95\% CI} \approx \hat p \pm 1.96\,\operatorname{SE}.SE(p^​)=np^​(1−p^​)​​,95% CI≈p^​±1.96SE.

so the CI is roughly ±0.6\pm 0.6±0.6 points. For a 1,000-item subject subset:

SE⁡=0.85×0.151000≈0.0113  ⟹  ±2.2 points.\operatorname{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.85 \times 0.15}{1000}} \approx 0.0113 \implies \pm 2.2 \text{ points}.SE=10000.85×0.15​​≈0.0113⟹±2.2 points.

A two-point difference on a thousand-item benchmark is not significant. Read per-subject MMLU breakdowns with this in mind — most of those subsets are a few hundred items, giving error bars of ±4 points or more.

Use a paired test

Section titled “Use a paired test”

Comparing two models on the same items means the observations are paired, and treating them as independent throws away power and inflates the variance estimate.

McNemar’s test uses only the discordant pairs. Let bbb be the count where model A is right and B wrong, ccc the reverse:

χ2=(∣b−c∣−1)2b+c,df=1.\chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c}, \qquad \text{df} = 1.χ2=b+c(∣b−c∣−1)2​,df=1.

Items both models get right or both get wrong carry no information about which is better, and excluding them is why the paired test is more sensitive. For small b+cb+cb+c, use the exact binomial test on b∼Bin⁡(b+c,0.5)b \sim \operatorname{Bin}(b+c, 0.5)b∼Bin(b+c,0.5) instead.

The emergence argument, formalised

Section titled “The emergence argument, formalised”

Let per-token accuracy be a(N)a(N)a(N), smooth and increasing in model size NNN. For a task requiring LLL tokens all correct:

EM⁡(N)=a(N)L.\operatorname{EM}(N) = a(N)^{L}.EM(N)=a(N)L.

Differentiating:

d EM⁡dN=L a(N)L−1 a′(N).\frac{d\,\operatorname{EM}}{dN} = L\, a(N)^{L-1}\,a'(N).dNdEM​=La(N)L−1a′(N).

For small aaa this is near zero (the aL−1a^{L-1}aL−1 factor crushes it); as a→1a \to 1a→1 it approaches L a′(N)L\,a'(N)La′(N). So the exact-match curve is flat, then steep, then saturating — an S-curve — while a(N)a(N)a(N) itself is smooth throughout.

The sharpness scales with LLL: longer required outputs produce sharper apparent transitions from identical underlying improvement.

The diagnostic. Plot a continuous metric — token-level accuracy, edit distance, or log-likelihood of the correct answer — on the same axis. If the sharpness survives, the discontinuity is in the model. If it dissolves, it was in the metric.

Scoring rules

Section titled “Scoring rules”

Accuracy discards the model’s confidence. For a calibrated view use the Brier score:

BS=1n∑i=1n(pi−yi)2,\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(p_i - y_i\big)^2,BS=n1​i=1∑n​(pi​−yi​)2,

which is proper — uniquely minimised by reporting your true belief, so it cannot be gamed by strategic over- or under-confidence. It decomposes as

BS=E[(p−yˉp)2]⏟calibration−E[(yˉp−yˉ)2]⏟resolution+yˉ(1−yˉ)⏟irreducible,\text{BS} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(p - \bar y_{p})^2\big]}_{\text{calibration}} - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\bar y_{p} - \bar y)^2\big]}_{\text{resolution}} + \underbrace{\bar y(1-\bar y)}_{\text{irreducible}},BS=calibrationE[(p−yˉ​p​)2]​​−resolutionE[(yˉ​p​−yˉ​)2]​​+irreducibleyˉ​(1−yˉ​)​​,

separating “are your stated probabilities honest” from “do they discriminate”. Two models can share an accuracy and differ enormously on both.

Elo and its assumptions

Section titled “Elo and its assumptions”

Chatbot Arena ranks models by pairwise human preference using an Elo-style rating. Under the Bradley–Terry model — the same one behind reward modelling — the probability A beats B is

P(A≻B)=11+10(RB−RA)/400.P(A \succ B) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}.P(A≻B)=1+10(RB​−RA​)/4001​.

Three assumptions worth surfacing, because they are load-bearing and often violated:

  • Transitivity. If A beats B and B beats C then A beats C. Real preferences on heterogeneous tasks are not reliably transitive.
  • A single latent dimension. One number per model, so “better at code, worse at prose” is not representable.
  • A representative query distribution. The rating reflects what Arena users happen to ask, which is not what your users ask.

Arena is the most robust preference signal available and it measures preference under a particular distribution — not capability in general.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Static benchmarks saturate and then mislead. MMLU, HumanEval and GSM8K are all in the range where remaining headroom is dominated by label noise and ambiguous items. Improvements at the top are partly fitting the errors in the test set.

The field has moved toward held-out, dynamic, and agentic evaluation. Benchmarks built from post-cutoff material, continuously refreshed item pools, and tasks with programmatic verification. SWE-bench-style evaluation — real issues, real test suites — is now more informative than any multiple-choice benchmark.

Arena-style human preference is the most trusted single signal, and it is measuring preference on a particular query distribution, with all the caveats above. It is not a capability measure and is frequently read as one.

LLM judges are ubiquitous for development and under-validated for publication. They are appropriate for iteration; using one as the primary evidence in a model comparison requires reporting agreement with human labels on the specific task.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No good measure of contamination. Every detection method is heuristic and most require training-data access nobody has.

  • Benchmarks measure what is easy to measure. Multiple choice, short answers, verifiable outputs. The things people actually want — judgement, taste, reliability over long horizons — resist automatic scoring entirely.

  • Construct validity is rarely examined. Does MMLU measure “massive multitask language understanding”? It measures the ability to answer those particular multiple-choice questions. The gap between the two is assumed away.

  • Agentic evaluation is stochastic and expensive. The same task can succeed or fail across seeds. Getting a stable estimate needs many runs, and many runs cost real money, so most reported agent numbers rest on very few samples.

  • No standard reporting protocol. Prompting, few-shot count, decoding temperature, and answer extraction all vary between papers and all move scores by more than the differences being claimed.
