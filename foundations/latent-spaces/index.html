Latent Spaces

A latent space is where a model keeps its understanding — the vectors between encoder and decoder, or between input and output head. Almost every intuition people import about it is wrong: it is not a nice isotropic cloud, it does not have one meaning per dimension, its usable dimensionality is far below its nominal one, and the pictures you have seen of it are mostly artefacts of the plotting algorithm. This page builds the correct picture from nothing, and ends at the results that are still being argued about.

What you should walk away with

  • The space is not shaped how you imagine. Learned embeddings occupy a narrow anisotropic cone, not a ball. Random pairs of BERT embeddings have cosine similarity around 0.6 — near-identical by the metric everyone uses — which makes raw cosine similarity close to meaningless without correction.
  • Directions carry meaning; dimensions usually do not. A concept is a direction in the space, and it is almost never axis-aligned. Reading dimension 4,217 is a category error, and it is the single most common mistake people make about latent spaces.
  • Unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive bias — this is a theorem. Locatello et al. proved it and then demonstrated it empirically across 12,000 models. Any paper claiming purely unsupervised disentanglement is either assuming something unstated or measuring badly.
  • Your t-SNE plot is not a picture of the space. Cluster sizes, inter-cluster distances and empty space are all artefacts. It preserves local neighbourhoods and nothing else, and people routinely read global structure off it.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Where the model keeps its understanding

Section titled “Where the model keeps its understanding”

Feed an image into a network. Somewhere in the middle, before it produces an answer, it holds a list of numbers — say 768 of them. That list is what the model has decided the image is, in its own terms.

That list is a point in a 768-dimensional space, and the latent space is the set of all such points for all possible inputs.

Everything interesting follows from one question: what is the shape of that set?

Why shape is the right question

Section titled “Why shape is the right question”

If the space were arranged sensibly, then:

  • Similar inputs would sit close together, so distance would mean similarity.
  • Moving in a straight line between two points would pass through sensible intermediates.
  • A direction would correspond to a concept, so you could add “make it older” as a vector.

All three of these do happen, sometimes, in some spaces. And all three fail in ways that catch people out. The rest of this page is about which is which.

The three intuitions people arrive with, and their status

Section titled “The three intuitions people arrive with, and their status”

  1. “Each dimension means something.” — Mostly false. Concepts are directions, and directions are almost never aligned to the coordinate axes you happened to get. See superposition.

  2. “Distance means similarity.” — Conditionally true. It depends entirely on what trained the space. A contrastive encoder was explicitly optimised to make cosine distance meaningful. A VAE was not, and using cosine on VAE latents is a silent error.

  3. “I can see the space in a 2-D plot.” — Largely false. t-SNE and UMAP preserve local neighbourhoods and deliberately distort everything else. The distances between clusters in your plot carry no information.

The one-line correction that fixes most confusion

Latent spaces are shaped by their training objective, not by the data and not by the architecture. Ask “what did the loss force this space to do?” and the answers follow. Contrastive losses shape angles. Reconstruction losses shape reachability. Classification losses collapse classes to points. Nothing shapes what you did not ask for.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Property 1 — Anisotropy

Section titled “Property 1 — Anisotropy”

The single most practically important fact, and the least known outside NLP.

Take BERT, embed a large sample of random tokens, and compute the average cosine similarity between random pairs. In an isotropic space this would be near 0. In practice, for contextual embeddings from later layers, it is far higher — Ethayarajh measured values approaching 0.99 in GPT-2’s upper layers.

Everything occupies a narrow cone. A cosine similarity of 0.85 sounds like strong similarity and may be below average.

Why it happens. Gao et al. traced it to the softmax output layer: tokens that appear rarely have their embeddings pushed in a common direction by the negative gradient from every step in which they do not appear. The result is a shared dominant component that dwarfs the semantic variation.

The fixes, in increasing order of effort:

  1. Subtract the mean. Removes the dominant common component. Free, and often the bulk of the improvement.
  2. All-but-the-top. Remove the mean, then project out the top few principal components. Mu et al. showed this improves word-similarity benchmarks substantially.
  3. Whitening. Transform so the covariance is identity — mean-centre and multiply by Σ−1/2\Sigma^{-1/2}Σ−1/2. Makes the space isotropic by construction.
  4. Train for it. A contrastive objective’s uniformity term does this during training rather than after.

Check this before you trust a similarity score

Embed 10,000 random items from your corpus, compute pairwise cosine similarities, and plot the histogram. If it is centred well above zero, your raw similarities are dominated by a common component and your retrieval or clustering is degraded. This costs five minutes and is routinely skipped.

Property 2 — Intrinsic dimension

Section titled “Property 2 — Intrinsic dimension”

Your embedding has 768 dimensions. It is not using 768 of them.

Intrinsic dimension is the number of degrees of freedom the data actually varies along. Pope et al. estimated it for common image datasets and found values in the range of roughly 20 to 40 — for images with tens of thousands of pixels.

The consequence they demonstrated is the useful part: learning difficulty tracks intrinsic dimension, not ambient dimension. Datasets with lower intrinsic dimension need fewer samples to reach a given accuracy, and this holds across architectures.

How to measure it. The maximum-likelihood estimator on nearest-neighbour distances (Levina–Bickel), or the two-nearest-neighbour method, or simply the number of principal components needed for 95% of variance — the last is crude, because it only detects linear dimension and the manifold is curved.

Property 3 — The manifold hypothesis

Section titled “Property 3 — The manifold hypothesis”

The claim: real data lies on (or near) a low-dimensional manifold embedded in the high-dimensional ambient space.

Why it must be roughly true. Sample uniformly from all 256224×224×3256^{224\times224\times3}256224×224×3 possible images and you will never in the lifetime of the universe produce anything that looks like a photograph. Photographs occupy a vanishingly small, highly structured subset.

And it is testable. Fefferman, Mitter & Narayanan gave an algorithm that decides, from finite samples, whether data lies near a manifold of bounded dimension and curvature — turning a widely-assumed hypothesis into a checkable one.

Why it matters here. If the data manifold is ddd-dimensional and your latent is kkk-dimensional with k>dk > dk>d, the model has spare capacity and the extra dimensions will encode something — noise, dataset artefacts, or nothing at all. If k<dk < dk<d you are forced to discard real structure. Neither is catastrophic, and knowing which regime you are in explains a lot of otherwise mysterious behaviour.

Property 4 — Directions and arithmetic

Section titled “Property 4 — Directions and arithmetic”

The famous result: king − man + woman ≈ queen.

It is real and heavily oversold. Linzen showed that a large part of the reported accuracy depends on excluding the three query words from the nearest-neighbour search. Without that exclusion, the nearest vector to king − man + woman is frequently just king again — the offset is small relative to the distance between words, so you land back near where you started.

What survives the critique. Directions do carry meaning; the analogy task is simply a poor way to demonstrate it. Better evidence:

  • Steering. Add αv\alpha vαv to activations and behaviour changes predictably in the direction vvv encodes. This is causal, not correlational.
  • GANSpace. Run PCA on a GAN’s intermediate latents and the top components are interpretable controls — pose, lighting, age — with no supervision at all.
  • Linear probes. If a concept is linearly decodable, a direction encodes it.

Property 5 — Interpolation, and the sphere

Section titled “Property 5 — Interpolation, and the sphere”

Walk in a straight line between two latent points and decode along the way. In a well-behaved space you get a smooth semantic transition.

Whether it works depends on the objective. A VAE’s KL term pulls the aggregate posterior toward the prior, filling the space — so the midpoint decodes to something plausible. A plain autoencoder has holes, and the midpoint decodes to noise.

The high-dimensional trap. Linear interpolation between two samples from a Gaussian passes through a region of atypically small norm. In ddd dimensions a standard Gaussian concentrates on a shell of radius d\sqrt{d}d​, and the midpoint of two random points on that shell has expected norm about d/2\sqrt{d/2}d/2​ — well inside the shell, in a region the model effectively never saw during training.

The fix is spherical interpolation (slerp), which moves along the great circle and preserves norm:

slerp⁡(z1,z2;t)=sin⁡((1−t)Ω)sin⁡Ωz1+sin⁡(tΩ)sin⁡Ωz2,Ω=arccos⁡ ⁣(z1⋅z2∥z1∥∥z2∥).\operatorname{slerp}(z_1,z_2;t) = \frac{\sin\big((1-t)\Omega\big)}{\sin\Omega}z_1 + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}z_2,\qquad \Omega = \arccos\!\Big(\tfrac{z_1\cdot z_2}{\|z_1\|\|z_2\|}\Big).slerp(z1​,z2​;t)=sinΩsin((1−t)Ω)​z1​+sinΩsin(tΩ)​z2​,Ω=arccos(∥z1​∥∥z2​∥z1​⋅z2​​).

If your interpolations look washed out in the middle, this is almost certainly why.

Property 6 — Disentanglement, and the impossibility result

Section titled “Property 6 — Disentanglement, and the impossibility result”

The dream: one latent dimension per generative factor. Dimension 1 is rotation, dimension 2 is colour, and they vary independently.

β\betaβ-VAE and relatives appeared to achieve this by up-weighting the KL term.

Then Locatello et al. proved it cannot be done. For any generative model p(x,z)p(x,z)p(x,z) with a factorised prior, there exists an invertible transformation qqq such that z′=q(z)z' = q(z)z′=q(z) is completely entangled while inducing exactly the same distribution over xxx. Since the observed data is identical, no unsupervised objective can distinguish them.

Then they trained over 12,000 models and confirmed empirically:

  • Disentanglement metrics correlate poorly with each other.
  • Random seed affects the score more than the choice of method.
  • Better disentanglement scores did not yield better downstream performance.

What the theorem does and does not say

It says purely unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive bias. It does not say disentanglement is impossible. With weak supervision, paired observations, temporal structure, or access to interventions, identifiability results do exist — iVAE proves identifiability given an auxiliary observed variable that the latents depend on.

So the correct reading is: the assumptions must be made explicit. Any method that appears to disentangle from raw observations alone is relying on an unstated architectural bias, and the field’s job is to name it.

Property 7 — Comparing two spaces

Section titled “Property 7 — Comparing two spaces”

Two models trained on the same data produce different latent spaces. Are they representing the same thing?

Naive comparison fails immediately — the spaces have different dimensions, and any rotation of a space is equivalent to it. You need a similarity measure invariant to the things that do not matter and sensitive to the things that do.

MethodInvariant toProblem
CCAinvertible linear transformstoo permissive — finds correlation in noise directions
SVCCAas CCA, after truncating to top singular vectorstruncation threshold is arbitrary

CKA (RBF)as above, non-linearkernel bandwidth is another knob

CKA is the standard, and Kornblith et al.’s argument for it is precisely that invariance to all invertible linear transforms is too strong: such transforms can destroy the structure you were trying to measure, so a measure invariant to them tells you nothing. Invariance to orthogonal transforms and isotropic scaling is the right amount.

Property 8 — Neural collapse

Section titled “Property 8 — Neural collapse”

At the end of training a classifier — past the point where training error is zero, in the terminal phase — something startling happens.

Papyan, Han & Donoho documented four properties emerging together:

  1. Within-class variability collapses. All examples of a class map to essentially the same point — the class mean.
  2. Class means form a simplex equiangular tight frame. Maximally separated, equal pairwise angles.
  3. Classifier weights align with class means (self-duality).
  4. Classification becomes nearest-class-mean.

Why it matters for latent spaces. A supervised classifier’s penultimate layer, trained to convergence, has thrown away nearly all within-class information. Two different cats are the same point.

This is the geometric reason to prefer self-supervised features for transfer. A supervised representation is optimised to be maximally uninformative about everything except the label — which is exactly wrong for a downstream task that cares about something else.

Anisotropy, measured

Section titled “Anisotropy, measured”

Define the expected cosine similarity between random pairs of embeddings:

Intrinsic dimension estimators

Section titled “Intrinsic dimension estimators”

Levina–Bickel MLE. For point xix_ixi​ with distances Tk(xi)T_k(x_i)Tk​(xi​) to its kkk-th nearest neighbour, model the number of points within radius rrr as a Poisson process on a ddd-manifold. The likelihood gives

d^k(xi)=[1k−1∑j=1k−1log⁡Tk(xi)Tj(xi)]−1,\hat d_k(x_i) = \left[\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\log \frac{T_k(x_i)}{T_j(x_i)}\right]^{-1},d^k​(xi​)=[k−11​j=1∑k−1​logTj​(xi​)Tk​(xi​)​]−1,

averaged over points. Sensitive to kkk — too small and noise dominates, too large and curvature violates the local-flatness assumption.

TwoNN. Uses only the two nearest neighbours. With μi=r2(xi)/r1(xi)\mu_i = r_2(x_i)/r_1(x_i)μi​=r2​(xi​)/r1​(xi​), the ratio follows a Pareto distribution with parameter ddd, so

d^=n∑ilog⁡μi.\hat d = \frac{n}{\sum_i \log \mu_i}.d^=∑i​logμi​n​.

Fewer assumptions about scale, and the trade is higher variance.

The key empirical result. Pope et al. found generalisation error scales with intrinsic rather than extrinsic dimension: for a fixed architecture, sample complexity to reach a target accuracy grew with d^\hat dd^ and was essentially independent of pixel count. This is why models work at all on images — the apparent dimensionality is not the effective one.

The disentanglement impossibility theorem

Section titled “The disentanglement impossibility theorem”

Theorem (Locatello et al.). Let z∼p(z)z \sim p(z)z∼p(z) with p(z)=∏ip(zi)p(z) = \prod_i p(z_i)p(z)=∏i​p(zi​) factorised. Then for d>1d > 1d>1 there exist infinitely many invertible maps q:supp⁡(z)→supp⁡(z)q: \operatorname{supp}(z)\to\operatorname{supp}(z)q:supp(z)→supp(z) such that

∂qi(u)∂uj≠0∀i,j(completely entangled)\frac{\partial q_i(u)}{\partial u_j} \neq 0 \quad \forall i,j \qquad\text{(completely entangled)}∂uj​∂qi​(u)​=0∀i,j(completely entangled)

while q(z)q(z)q(z) has the same marginal distribution as zzz, i.e. P(z≤u)=P(q(z)≤u)P(z \leq u) = P(q(z)\leq u)P(z≤u)=P(q(z)≤u).

The construction. Map each coordinate through its own CDF to uniform, apply a suitable measure-preserving mixing map on the unit cube, and map back. Since the resulting z′=q(z)z' = q(z)z′=q(z) has the same distribution, the models (q,p)(q, p)(q,p) and (id,p)(\text{id}, p)(id,p) are observationally identical — they induce the same p(x)p(x)p(x).

The conclusion. No objective that depends only on p(x)p(x)p(x) can prefer the disentangled solution. Disentanglement requires either supervision or an inductive bias that breaks the symmetry, and the bias must be stated.

iVAE’s escape route. Khemakhem et al. showed identifiability is recovered given an auxiliary variable uuu (time index, class label, environment id) with

p(z∣u)=∏iQi(zi)Zi(u)exp⁡[∑j=1kTi,j(zi) λi,j(u)],p(z\mid u) = \prod_i \frac{Q_i(z_i)}{Z_i(u)}\exp\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k} T_{i,j}(z_i)\,\lambda_{i,j}(u)\right],p(z∣u)=i∏​Zi​(u)Qi​(zi​)​exp[j=1∑k​Ti,j​(zi​)λi,j​(u)],

a conditionally factorised exponential family. Given sufficient variability in uuu, the latents are identifiable up to permutation and componentwise transformation. The auxiliary variable is the inductive bias, made explicit — which is exactly what the impossibility theorem demands.

Linear representations, stated carefully

Section titled “Linear representations, stated carefully”

The linear representation hypothesis: a concept WWW is represented as a direction ℓˉW\bar\ell_WℓˉW​ such that the model’s behaviour depends on the projection ℓˉW⊤γ\bar\ell_W^{\top} \gammaℓˉW⊤​γ of the representation γ\gammaγ.

Park, Choe & Veitch’s contribution is to distinguish two notions that get conflated:

  • Embedding representation. A concept is a direction in the input embedding space — the space where you would add a steering vector.
  • Unembedding representation. A concept is a direction in the output space — the space that determines logits.

They are related by the model’s internal geometry, and not the same direction in general. The reconciliation uses a causal inner product: define

⟨γ1,γ2⟩C=γ1⊤ Cov⁡(γ)−1 γ2,\langle \gamma_1, \gamma_2\rangle_{C} = \gamma_1^{\top}\,\operatorname{Cov}(\gamma)^{-1}\,\gamma_2 ,⟨γ1​,γ2​⟩C​=γ1⊤​Cov(γ)−1γ2​,

i.e. the Mahalanobis inner product under the unembedding covariance. Under this inner product, causally separable concepts become orthogonal, and the embedding and unembedding representations become duals of each other.

The practical consequence. Steering vectors should be applied under the whitened geometry, not the raw Euclidean one — the correct notion of “orthogonal concepts” depends on the covariance, and ignoring it is why naive steering sometimes produces unintended side effects.

Alignment and uniformity as a coordinate system

Section titled “Alignment and uniformity as a coordinate system”

For ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​-normalised embeddings on Sd−1S^{d-1}Sd−1:

Lalign(f;α)=E(x,x+)[∥f(x)−f(x+)∥2α],\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}(f;\alpha) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^{+})}\big[\|f(x)-f(x^{+})\|_2^{\alpha}\big],Lalign​(f;α)=E(x,x+)​[∥f(x)−f(x+)∥2α​], Lunif(f;t)=log⁡ Ex,y iid[e−t∥f(x)−f(y)∥22].\mathcal{L}_{\text{unif}}(f;t) = \log\ \mathbb{E}_{x,y\ \text{iid}}\big[e^{-t\|f(x)-f(y)\|_2^{2}}\big].Lunif​(f;t)=log Ex,y iid​[e−t∥f(x)−f(y)∥22​].

Neural collapse, formally

Section titled “Neural collapse, formally”

NC3 (self-duality). W⊤∝MW^\top \propto MW⊤∝M, the classifier weights aligning with the class-mean matrix.

The implication for transfer, stated precisely. NC1 says within-class information is destroyed. A downstream task requiring a distinction within a training class — breed of dog, when trained on “dog” — has had its signal removed from the representation by convergence itself. Training longer makes this worse, which is the counterintuitive part and the reason early-stopped or self-supervised features often transfer better.

Visualisation, and what it does not show

Section titled “Visualisation, and what it does not show”

t-SNE minimises

KL(P ∥ Q)=∑i≠jpijlog⁡pijqij,\mathrm{KL}\big(P\,\|\,Q\big) = \sum_{i\neq j} p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}},KL(P∥Q)=i=j∑​pij​logqij​pij​​,

with ppp from Gaussian neighbourhoods in high dimensions and qqq from a Student-ttt in two dimensions.

The asymmetry of forward KL is the whole story. Recall from probability that forward KL is heavily penalised when pijp_{ij}pij​ is large and qijq_{ij}qij​ is small — that is, when nearby points are placed far apart. It is barely penalised in the reverse case. So t-SNE aggressively preserves local neighbourhoods and freely distorts global structure.

Three concrete things your plot does not show:

  • Cluster sizes are meaningless. The Student-ttt heavy tail expands dense regions and contracts sparse ones. A large blob is not a diverse class.
  • Inter-cluster distances are meaningless. Nothing in the objective constrains them. Two clusters at opposite ends may be adjacent in the real space.
  • Perplexity changes the answer. Different values produce qualitatively different pictures from identical data.

UMAP preserves somewhat more global structure through its fuzzy-simplicial-set construction, and the same warnings apply in weaker form.

What to do instead. Report quantitative structure: intrinsic dimension, the singular value spectrum, alignment/uniformity, kkk-NN retrieval accuracy. Use the 2-D plot as an illustration of a claim you established another way, never as the evidence.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Sparse autoencoders are the current best tool for reading a latent space. By learning an overcomplete, sparse basis they recover far more monosemantic directions than the neuron basis — millions of interpretable features from production models. Feature splitting remains unresolved: enlarge the dictionary and features subdivide indefinitely, with no principled stopping criterion. See interpretability.

The linear representation hypothesis holds better than expected, with documented exceptions. Many concepts are single directions and steering works. Circular representations — days of the week, months — have been found, so “features are directions” is a strong default rather than a law. Park et al.’s causal inner product is the current best account of which geometry makes the hypothesis precise.

Representational convergence is a live and genuinely surprising result. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis reports that as models get larger and more capable, their representation spaces align increasingly well — across architectures, training objectives, and even modalities. Vision and language models trained separately end up with measurably similar structure. The proposed explanation (all are approximating the same statistical structure of reality) is speculative; the measurements are not, and they are worth watching.

Anisotropy is understood and still bites people. The mechanism is known, the fixes are cheap, and a great deal of deployed retrieval runs on raw cosine similarity over uncorrected embeddings.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No task-agnostic quality measure, and the formalism says there cannot be one. Representation quality is defined relative to a downstream hypothesis class. This is a property of the definition, not a gap awaiting a solution.

  • Feature splitting. SAE dictionary size is a free parameter that changes what counts as a feature. Nothing says how many features a model “has”.

  • Cross-model comparison remains unsettled. CKA, SVCCA and Procrustes methods disagree with one another, and there is no principled way to adjudicate.

  • Non-linear feature geometry is under-explored. Circular and manifold-valued representations have been documented, and no systematic account exists of when concepts are directions versus something else.

  • Predicting what a training setup will represent. Given an architecture, data and objective, nothing forecasts which features emerge. Every finding is post-hoc.

A working checklist

Section titled “A working checklist”

Diagnosing a latent space you did not train

1. Histogram random-pair cosine similarities. Centred well above zero means anisotropy is dominating your metric. Mean-centre, then consider whitening.

2. Plot the singular value spectrum of a batch of embeddings. A sharp cliff means dimensional collapse; a slow decay is healthy.

3. Estimate intrinsic dimension (TwoNN is two lines of code). It tells you how much capacity is actually in use.

4. Measure alignment and uniformity if you have positive pairs. The 2×2 table above diagnoses four distinct failure modes the loss value cannot.

5. Run kkk-NN retrieval as your quality metric before running a linear probe — it needs no training and makes no linearity assumption.

6. Only then plot it, and treat the picture as illustration rather than evidence.
