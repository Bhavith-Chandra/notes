Causal Inference

Machine learning answers “what is associated with what”. Almost every question people actually ask a model — did the treatment work, will this change help, what would have happened otherwise — is causal, and no amount of observational data answers a causal question by itself. You need assumptions the data cannot check. The discipline of causal inference is about making those assumptions explicit, knowing when they suffice, and only then estimating.

What you should walk away with

  • Identification comes before estimation, and it is not a statistical question. Whether the effect is recoverable from your data depends on the causal structure you assume. Once identification holds, ML is excellent at the estimation. If it fails, no model and no sample size rescues you.
  • The fundamental problem: you never see both outcomes. A person either received the treatment or did not. The other outcome is a counterfactual that was never observed, so every causal estimate is an imputation problem in disguise.
  • Controlling for more variables is not safer. Conditioning on a collider — a common effect of treatment and outcome — creates a spurious association where none existed. “Adjust for everything you measured” is actively wrong advice.
  • Double machine learning is the bridge. Use flexible ML for the nuisance functions, then a bias-corrected score with cross-fitting, and you recover n\sqrt{n}n​-consistent effect estimates with valid confidence intervals despite slowly-converging ML components.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The question ML cannot answer

Section titled “The question ML cannot answer”

A model trained on hospital data finds that patients given a certain drug die more often. Should you stop prescribing it?

Obviously not — not on that evidence. The drug is given to the sickest patients. The correlation is real, the prediction is accurate, and the causal conclusion is backwards.

No amount of additional data fixes this, because the data is consistent with both stories: the drug kills people, or the drug is given to people who were already dying. Distinguishing them requires knowing something about how the data came to be — and that knowledge is not in the data.

The ladder

Section titled “The ladder”

Pearl’s framing, and it is the most useful organising idea here.

  1. Association — P(y∣x)P(y \mid x)P(y∣x). What do I see together? Answered by any predictive model. “Patients on this drug die more.”

  2. Intervention — P(y∣do(x))P(y \mid do(x))P(y∣do(x)). What happens if I act? Requires a causal model or an experiment. “If I give the drug, does survival change?”

  3. Counterfactual — P(yx∣x′,y′)P(y_x \mid x', y')P(yx​∣x′,y′). What would have happened? Requires a fully specified structural model. “Would this specific patient, who took the drug and died, have survived without it?”

The rungs are strictly ordered. You cannot answer a rung-2 question with only rung-1 information, no matter how much of it you have or how good your model is. This is a theorem, not a difficulty.

What this means for machine learning specifically

A large model trained on observational text has, in this framing, seen rung-1 information about the world plus rung-2 and rung-3 claims other people wrote down. It can repeat causal knowledge; it did not derive it. That distinction matters when you ask a model whether an intervention will work.

Why more controls can hurt

Section titled “Why more controls can hurt”

The instinct “adjust for everything you measured” is common and wrong, and the reason is worth seeing.

Suppose talent and beauty are independent in the population, and either one gets you into acting. Now look only at actors. Among them, someone who is not beautiful is probably talented — otherwise how did they get in? So within the selected group, talent and beauty are negatively correlated.

You created an association by conditioning. The variable you conditioned on (being an actor) is a collider: both talent and beauty point into it.

So which variables to adjust for is a causal question, not a statistical one. You need the structure to know, and the structure is an assumption.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Three structures, three different rules

Section titled “Three structures, three different rules”

The potential outcomes framework

Section titled “The potential outcomes framework”

The other main formalism, equivalent in expressive power and often easier to compute with.

Each unit iii has two potential outcomes: Yi(1)Y_i(1)Yi​(1) if treated, Yi(0)Y_i(0)Yi​(0) if not. The individual effect is τi=Yi(1)−Yi(0)\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)τi​=Yi​(1)−Yi​(0).

You observe exactly one of them. Yiobs=TiYi(1)+(1−Ti)Yi(0)Y_i^{\text{obs}} = T_i Y_i(1) + (1-T_i) Y_i(0)Yiobs​=Ti​Yi​(1)+(1−Ti​)Yi​(0). The other is a counterfactual, and it is missing not at random — it is missing because of the treatment assignment.

This is why Holland called it the fundamental problem of causal inference: individual effects are not identified, ever. Only averages are, and only under assumptions.

The assumptions you cannot test

Section titled “The assumptions you cannot test”

  1. Consistency. Yiobs=Yi(Ti)Y_i^{\text{obs}} = Y_i(T_i)Yiobs​=Yi​(Ti​) — the observed outcome is the potential outcome for the treatment received. This requires the treatment to be well-defined; “obesity” fails, because losing weight by exercise and by illness are different treatments.

  2. Ignorability / no unmeasured confounding. (Y(0),Y(1))⊥T∣X\big(Y(0), Y(1)\big) \perp T \mid X(Y(0),Y(1))⊥T∣X. Given the covariates, assignment is as good as random. This is the big one, and it is untestable — by definition you cannot check for a confounder you did not measure.

  3. Positivity / overlap. 0<P(T=1∣X=x)<10 < P(T=1\mid X=x) < 10<P(T=1∣X=x)<1 for all xxx with support. If nobody with a certain covariate profile ever gets treated, you have no evidence about them and estimation there is extrapolation.

  4. SUTVA. One unit’s treatment does not affect another’s outcome. Violated by vaccines (herd immunity), by network effects, by market prices.

Positivity fails silently and is checkable

Unlike ignorability, positivity leaves a trace: plot the estimated propensity scores by treatment group. If they barely overlap, your estimator is extrapolating into regions with no data, and a flexible ML model will do so confidently and wrongly. Always plot this. It is the cheapest diagnostic in the field and it is routinely skipped.

When randomisation is unavailable

Section titled “When randomisation is unavailable”
  • Adjustment
  • Instrumental variables
  • Difference-in-differences
  • Regression discontinuity

Condition on enough covariates to close all backdoor paths, then compare treated and untreated within strata:

E[Y∣do(T=1)]=∑xE[Y∣T=1,X=x]P(X=x).\mathbb{E}\big[Y \mid do(T{=}1)\big] = \sum_{x} \mathbb{E}\big[Y \mid T{=}1, X{=}x\big]P(X{=}x).E[Y∣do(T=1)]=x∑​E[Y∣T=1,X=x]P(X=x).

Requires: measuring all confounders. The strongest assumption and the most common one.

Find a variable ZZZ that affects TTT, affects YYY only through TTT, and is unconfounded with YYY. Then variation in ZZZ acts like randomisation.

The canonical example is randomised encouragement: you cannot force people to take a drug, but you can randomly assign who gets a reminder.

Requires: the exclusion restriction — ZZZ has no direct path to YYY — which is untestable and frequently implausible. Also, IV estimates a local effect: the LATE, on the subpopulation whose behaviour the instrument actually changed. That subgroup is not identified, so you do not know whose effect you measured.

Compare the change over time in a treated group with the change in an untreated group. Any time-invariant confounding differences out.

Requires: parallel trends — absent treatment, both groups would have moved together. Untestable for the treatment period, though pre-treatment trends are suggestive evidence.

When treatment is assigned by a threshold on a running variable — a test score, an income cutoff — compare units just above and just below.

Requires: no precise manipulation of the running variable near the cutoff. Checkable by looking for bunching in the density.

The most credible design here, because near the threshold assignment really is close to arbitrary, and it estimates only a local effect at the cutoff.
Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

The do-operator and identification

Section titled “The do-operator and identification”

P(y∣do(x))P(y \mid do(x))P(y∣do(x)) is defined by graph surgery: delete every edge into XXX, set X=xX = xX=x, and compute in the mutilated graph. This is not the same as conditioning:

P(y∣do(x))≠P(y∣x)in general.P(y \mid do(x)) \neq P(y \mid x) \quad\text{in general}.P(y∣do(x))=P(y∣x)in general.

Conditioning selects a subpopulation; intervening changes the data-generating process. They coincide only when nothing causes XXX — which randomisation arranges by construction.

The backdoor criterion. A set ZZZ is admissible for (X,Y)(X, Y)(X,Y) if

  1. no node in ZZZ is a descendant of XXX, and
  2. ZZZ blocks every path from XXX to YYY that begins with an arrow into XXX.

Then

P(y∣do(x))=∑zP(y∣x,z) P(z).P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_{z} P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z).P(y∣do(x))=z∑​P(y∣x,z)P(z).

Condition 1 excludes mediators and colliders on causal paths; condition 2 closes the confounding routes. Note it says which variables — this is the formal content of “adjusting for everything is wrong”.

The front-door criterion handles a case adjustment cannot: identification through a fully-mediating variable even with an unmeasured confounder between XXX and YYY:

P(y∣do(x))=∑mP(m∣x)∑x′P(y∣m,x′)P(x′).P(y\mid do(x)) = \sum_{m}P(m\mid x)\sum_{x'}P(y\mid m, x')P(x').P(y∣do(x))=m∑​P(m∣x)x′∑​P(y∣m,x′)P(x′).

That an effect can be identified despite unmeasured confounding was genuinely surprising, and it is the clearest demonstration that identification is a structural question rather than a data-quantity question.

The ATE and its estimators

Section titled “The ATE and its estimators”
τATE=E[Y(1)−Y(0)].\tau_{\text{ATE}} = \mathbb{E}\big[Y(1) - Y(0)\big].τATE​=E[Y(1)−Y(0)].

Under ignorability and positivity, with μt(x)=E[Y∣T=t,X=x]\mu_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T{=}t, X{=}x]μt​(x)=E[Y∣T=t,X=x] and propensity e(x)=P(T=1∣X=x)e(x) = P(T{=}1\mid X{=}x)e(x)=P(T=1∣X=x):

Outcome regression (G-computation).

τ^=1n∑i[μ^1(Xi)−μ^0(Xi)].\hat\tau = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\Big[\hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i)\Big].τ^=n1​i∑​[μ^​1​(Xi​)−μ^​0​(Xi​)].

Consistent if μ^\hat\muμ^​ is correct. Biased if it is misspecified.

Inverse propensity weighting.

τ^=1n∑i[TiYie^(Xi)−(1−Ti)Yi1−e^(Xi)].\hat\tau = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\left[\frac{T_i Y_i}{\hat e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)Y_i}{1 - \hat e(X_i)}\right].τ^=n1​i∑​[e^(Xi​)Ti​Yi​​−1−e^(Xi​)(1−Ti​)Yi​​].

Consistent if e^\hat ee^ is correct. High variance when propensities approach 0 or 1 — a single unit with e^=0.01\hat e = 0.01e^=0.01 receives weight 100 and dominates the estimate. This is the positivity problem showing up numerically.

Augmented IPW (doubly robust).

τ^AIPW=1n∑i[μ^1(Xi)−μ^0(Xi)+Ti(Yi−μ^1(Xi))e^(Xi)−(1−Ti)(Yi−μ^0(Xi))1−e^(Xi)].\hat\tau_{\text{AIPW}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\left[\hat\mu_1(X_i) - \hat\mu_0(X_i) + \frac{T_i\big(Y_i - \hat\mu_1(X_i)\big)}{\hat e(X_i)} - \frac{(1-T_i)\big(Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i)\big)}{1 - \hat e(X_i)}\right].τ^AIPW​=n1​i∑​[μ^​1​(Xi​)−μ^​0​(Xi​)+e^(Xi​)Ti​(Yi​−μ^​1​(Xi​))​−1−e^(Xi​)(1−Ti​)(Yi​−μ^​0​(Xi​))​].

Doubly robust: consistent if either μ^\hat\muμ^​ or e^\hat ee^ is correct, not necessarily both. The regression provides the estimate; the weighted residual term corrects it. If μ^\hat\muμ^​ is right the residuals are mean-zero and the correction vanishes; if e^\hat ee^ is right the weighting fixes whatever bias μ^\hat\muμ^​ left.

Double machine learning

Section titled “Double machine learning”

The result that makes flexible ML usable for causal estimation.

The problem it solves. Plugging an ML estimate of μ\muμ into the outcome regression introduces regularisation bias — ML estimators are biased by design, trading bias for variance — and that bias does not vanish at n\sqrt{n}n​, so confidence intervals are invalid.

The two ingredients.

Neyman orthogonality. Construct a score ψ\psiψ whose expectation is insensitive to first-order errors in the nuisance functions η=(μ,e)\eta = (\mu, e)η=(μ,e):

∂∂r E[ψ(W; τ, η0+r(η−η0))]∣r=0=0.\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\psi\big(W;\ \tau,\ \eta_0 + r(\eta - \eta_0)\big)\Big]\right|_{r=0} = 0 .∂r∂​ E[ψ(W; τ, η0​+r(η−η0​))]​r=0​=0.

The AIPW score has this property. Nuisance error then enters the estimate only at second order, so a nuisance rate of n−1/4n^{-1/4}n−1/4 — achievable by many ML methods — gives a product term of n−1/2n^{-1/2}n−1/2, which is exactly the rate that vanishes relative to the estimator’s own n\sqrt{n}n​ convergence.

Cross-fitting. Split the sample into KKK folds; estimate nuisances on K−1K-1K−1 folds and evaluate the score on the held-out fold. This removes overfitting bias — using the same data to fit μ^\hat\muμ^​ and to evaluate residuals against it creates a correlation that biases the estimate, and it is the reason naive plug-in with a flexible model fails even when the model is good.

Together:

n(τ^DML−τ0) →d N(0, σ2),\sqrt{n}\big(\hat\tau_{\text{DML}} - \tau_0\big) \ \xrightarrow{d}\ \mathcal{N}\big(0,\ \sigma^2\big),n​(τ^DML​−τ0​) d​ N(0, σ2),

so you get valid confidence intervals while using gradient boosting, random forests or neural networks for the nuisances. This is the technical bridge between ML and causal inference, and it is why “use ML for causal effects” became respectable.

Heterogeneous effects

Section titled “Heterogeneous effects”

Often the average is not the question. The conditional average treatment effect is

τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0) ∣ X=x].\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}\big[Y(1) - Y(0)\ \big|\ X = x\big].τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0) ​ X=x].

Meta-learners reduce CATE estimation to standard supervised problems:

  • S-learner. One model with treatment as a feature: $\hat\tau(x) = \hat\mu(x,1)
    • \hat\mu(x,0)$. Simple, and regularisation can shrink the treatment coefficient toward zero — biasing the effect toward no effect, which is the worst direction to be biased.
  • T-learner. Separate models per arm. Avoids that shrinkage, and cannot share structure, so it is inefficient when the arms are similar and one is small.
  • X-learner. Impute each unit’s counterfactual using the other arm’s model, then regress the imputed effects, then combine weighted by the propensity. Substantially better when treatment groups are very unbalanced.

Causal forests adapt random forests by splitting to maximise heterogeneity in the treatment effect rather than in the outcome, and use honest estimation — one subsample chooses the splits, a disjoint one estimates the effects in each leaf. Honesty is what buys asymptotic normality and therefore valid pointwise confidence intervals, which ordinary random forests do not have.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

DML and causal forests are standard practice in tech-company experimentation and increasingly in economics. The methodology is settled; the bottleneck has moved to identification, which is where it belongs.

Causal representation learning is ambitious and immature. The goal — recovering causal variables from raw observations rather than assuming them given — connects directly to representations and to world models. Identifiability results exist under strong assumptions (interventions available, sparse mechanisms) and general recovery from purely observational data is provably impossible.

LLMs and causality is a live and confused question. Models reproduce causal claims from their training data well. Whether they reason causally, or retrieve memorised causal statements, is contested — and existing benchmarks largely cannot distinguish those, since the answers are in the training corpus.

The assumption-checking culture is improving. Sensitivity analysis — how strong would an unmeasured confounder need to be to overturn this conclusion? — is increasingly expected rather than optional, which is the right response to untestable assumptions.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Unmeasured confounding is undetectable in principle. Sensitivity analysis bounds the damage; nothing rules it out. This is a hard limit, not a gap.

  • Causal discovery from observational data alone. Structure is identifiable only up to a Markov equivalence class without interventions or strong parametric assumptions. Many published discovery results quietly rely on those assumptions.

  • Interference and networks. SUTVA fails whenever units interact — social networks, marketplaces, epidemics — which is most interesting settings. Methods exist and are far less mature.

  • Continuous and high-dimensional treatments. Most theory assumes binary treatment. Dose-response and multi-dimensional interventions are much harder.

  • Transportability. An effect estimated in one population need not hold in another. Formal transport conditions exist and require knowing how the populations differ structurally.
