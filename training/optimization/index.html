Optimization

Optimization in deep learning is not the study of finding minima — the loss surface is non-convex and nobody finds the global minimum. It is the study of making the descent well-conditioned enough that a first-order method works at all. Adaptive step sizes, normalization layers, learning-rate schedules and gradient clipping are four different attacks on the same problem: the curvature of the loss varies wildly across parameters and across training.

What you should walk away with

  • Adam gives every parameter its own effective learning rate, estimated from the running magnitude of its gradients. That is the whole idea; the bias corrections are bookkeeping for the fact that the running averages start at zero.
  • AdamW is a bug fix, not a variant. L2 regularisation added to the loss gets divided by Adam’s per-parameter scaling, so parameters with large gradients get less decay — the opposite of intended. Decoupling restores the intent.
  • Warmup exists because early gradient statistics are garbage. Adam’s variance estimate is built from a handful of samples at step 10; trusting it produces enormous steps. Pre-norm transformers need warmup much less, which tells you the two are addressing overlapping problems.
  • Normalization’s benefit is not what its paper claimed. “Internal covariate shift” did not survive scrutiny. The durable explanation is that it smooths the loss landscape and decouples weight magnitude from function.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Descending a valley you cannot see

Section titled “Descending a valley you cannot see”

Gradient descent is walking downhill in fog: feel the slope, step downhill, repeat.

The problem is that the terrain is not uniform. In some directions the valley is a narrow steep canyon — step too far and you overshoot the far wall. In others it is a broad shallow plain — step timidly and you make no progress for thousands of steps.

A single learning rate must serve both, so it gets set small enough for the canyon and is then hopelessly slow on the plain. This mismatch — bad conditioning — is the central practical problem, and every technique on this page addresses it.

The adaptive idea

Section titled “The adaptive idea”

Adam’s answer: give each parameter its own step size, inferred from its own history.

Keep a running average of each parameter’s recent gradient magnitudes. If a parameter’s gradients have been consistently large, it is in a steep direction — take smaller steps. If consistently tiny, it is on a plain — take larger ones. Divide the step by that magnitude estimate and both directions move at a comparable rate.

Add momentum — a running average of gradient direction, so you keep rolling through small bumps rather than reacting to every noisy minibatch — and you have Adam.

Two averages, two jobs

Adam tracks the first moment (mean gradient — which way) and the second moment (mean squared gradient — how steep). Momentum smooths direction; the second moment normalises scale. Almost everything about Adam’s behaviour follows from holding those two roles apart.

Why the learning rate has to move

Section titled “Why the learning rate has to move”

Even with per-parameter scaling, the global rate needs to change over training, in two phases.

Warmup, at the start. At step 10, Adam’s estimate of gradient magnitude comes from ten noisy samples. If it happens to underestimate, you divide by a small number and take a huge step, potentially into a region the model never recovers from. Warmup ramps the rate up from near zero, buying time for the statistics to become trustworthy.

Decay, at the end. Early on you want to travel; late on you want to settle. Large steps near the end just bounce around the minimum without converging. Cosine decay to near zero is the standard.

The AdamW fix

Section titled “The AdamW fix”

This is the single most useful thing on this page, because the failure is silent.

Classic weight decay means adding λ2∥θ∥2\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^22λ​∥θ∥2 to the loss. Its gradient is λθ\lambda\thetaλθ, which gets added to gtg_tgt​ before Adam’s machinery runs. So it flows through the second-moment division:

effective decay for θi  ∝  λθiv^i.\text{effective decay for } \theta_i \;\propto\; \frac{\lambda \theta_i}{\sqrt{\hat v_i}} .effective decay for θi​∝v^i​​λθi​​.

Parameters with large gradients get less regularisation. That is backwards — those are exactly the parameters doing the most work and most at risk of growing too large. With SGD this problem does not arise, because there is no per-parameter scaling, which is why the bug went unnoticed for years.

AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive step:

θt=θt−1−η(m^tv^t+ϵ+λ θt−1).\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\left(\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon} + \lambda\,\theta_{t-1}\right).θt​=θt−1​−η(v^t​​+ϵm^t​​+λθt−1​).

Two things people get wrong

Do not decay biases, or normalization gains and offsets. Decaying a LayerNorm gain toward zero is shrinking the layer’s output scale for no principled reason. Every serious training script excludes them via a parameter group; many tutorials do not.

Check what your framework means by weight_decay. In torch.optim.Adam it is the coupled L2 version — the buggy one. In AdamW it is decoupled. The argument has the same name and different semantics.

Schedules

Section titled “Schedules”
  • Warmup + cosine
  • WSD
  • Large batch

The default for transformer training. Linear ramp for the first 1–5% of steps, then cosine decay to some floor (commonly 10% of peak):

ηt={ηmax⁡ tTwarmt<Twarmηmin⁡+12(ηmax⁡−ηmin⁡)(1+cos⁡π(t−Twarm)T−Twarm)t≥Twarm\eta_t = \begin{cases} \eta_{\max}\, \dfrac{t}{T_{\text{warm}}} & t < T_{\text{warm}} \\[6pt] \eta_{\min} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\Big(1 + \cos\dfrac{\pi (t-T_{\text{warm}})}{T - T_{\text{warm}}}\Big) & t \geq T_{\text{warm}} \end{cases}ηt​=⎩⎨⎧​ηmax​Twarm​t​ηmin​+21​(ηmax​−ηmin​)(1+cosT−Twarm​π(t−Twarm​)​)​t<Twarm​t≥Twarm​​

Cosine has a trap. The schedule depends on TTT, the total number of steps, so it must be decided in advance. Stop early and you stop mid-decay at a systematically worse point than the schedule intended. This is exactly the confound that made the Kaplan scaling-law analysis disagree with Chinchilla — fixed-length schedules penalised the longer runs.

Warmup–Stable–Decay avoids the cosine trap. Warm up, hold the rate constant for as long as you like, then decay sharply over the final ~10% of whatever budget you end up using.

The advantage is that the stable phase can be extended arbitrarily, so you can continue training a checkpoint without having committed to a horizon, and you can branch multiple decay runs from one stable trunk. Increasingly the choice for large runs where the total budget is not fixed upfront.

As batch size grows, gradients get less noisy, so you can take larger steps. The two standard heuristics:

  • Linear scaling. Multiply the learning rate by kkk when multiplying batch size by kkk. Works up to a point.
  • Square-root scaling. Multiply by k\sqrt{k}k​. Better justified for Adam, since gradient noise scales as 1/B1/\sqrt{B}1/B​.

Both break beyond some critical batch size, past which more parallelism buys wall clock but not sample efficiency. LARS and LAMB address this with layer-wise trust-ratio scaling, normalising each layer’s update by the ratio of its weight norm to its update norm.

Gradient clipping

Section titled “Gradient clipping”

Cheap and load-bearing. Compute the global norm over all parameters; if it exceeds a threshold, rescale:

g←g⋅min⁡(1,c∥g∥2).g \leftarrow g \cdot \min\left(1, \frac{c}{\|g\|_2}\right).g←g⋅min(1,∥g∥2​c​).

Typically c=1.0c = 1.0c=1.0. This prevents a single pathological batch from destroying a run — one bad example produces an enormous gradient, one enormous step lands somewhere terrible, and the loss never recovers.

Clip the global norm, not per-parameter values. Per-parameter clipping changes the direction of the update, which is a different and less benign intervention. Global-norm clipping preserves direction and only bounds magnitude.

Zhang & Sennrich found the re-centring contributes little while costing a pass over the data, so RMSNorm is slightly faster with no quality loss. Nearly universal in modern LLMs.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

Adam

Coupled versus decoupled decay, precisely

Section titled “Coupled versus decoupled decay, precisely”

Coupled (L2 in the loss). Set g~t=gt+λθt−1\tilde g_t = g_t + \lambda\theta_{t-1}g~​t​=gt​+λθt−1​. Then

θt=θt−1−η m~^tv~^t+ϵ,\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\, \frac{\hat{\tilde m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\tilde v}_t}+\epsilon},θt​=θt−1​−ηv~^t​​+ϵm~^t​​,

and both moment estimates now contain the decay term. The effective shrinkage per step is

Δθidecay≈−η λθiv^i,\Delta\theta_i^{\text{decay}} \approx -\eta\,\frac{\lambda \theta_i}{\sqrt{\hat v_i}},Δθidecay​≈−ηv^i​​λθi​​,

inversely proportional to that parameter’s gradient scale.

Decoupled (AdamW).

θt=θt−1−η m^tv^t+ϵ−ηλ θt−1,\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\,\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon} - \eta\lambda\,\theta_{t-1},θt​=θt−1​−ηv^t​​+ϵm^t​​−ηλθt−1​,

so the shrinkage is −ηλθi-\eta\lambda\theta_i−ηλθi​, uniform across parameters as intended.

A practical corollary: because the decay term is multiplied by η\etaη, its strength follows the learning-rate schedule. If you want decay independent of the schedule, you need the “fully decoupled” variant where λ\lambdaλ is not multiplied by η\etaη — some frameworks do this, some don’t, and it changes the meaning of your hyperparameter.

Why normalization helps

Section titled “Why normalization helps”

Ioffe & Szegedy attributed BatchNorm’s benefit to reducing “internal covariate shift” — the changing distribution of layer inputs during training. Subsequent work largely dismantled this: injecting noise after BatchNorm to deliberately reintroduce distribution shift did not remove the benefit.

The better-supported account has two parts.

Loss landscape smoothing. Normalization improves the Lipschitz constants of the loss and its gradient, making the landscape more predictable and permitting larger stable step sizes.

Scale invariance. For any a>0a > 0a>0,

Norm(a Wx)=Norm(Wx),\text{Norm}(a\,W x) = \text{Norm}(W x),Norm(aWx)=Norm(Wx),

so the function is invariant to the magnitude of the preceding weights — only direction matters. Differentiating gives

∇(aW)L=1a∇WL,\nabla_{(aW)} \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{a}\nabla_{W}\mathcal{L},∇(aW)​L=a1​∇W​L,

an automatic learning-rate adaptation: as weights grow, their effective step shrinks. This also explains the otherwise-odd interaction between weight decay and normalized networks — decay there controls the effective learning rate rather than model complexity in any classical sense.

Second-order methods

Section titled “Second-order methods”

Full Newton needs H−1H^{-1}H−1, which is O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) memory and O(n3)O(n^3)O(n3) to invert — impossible at billions of parameters. The practical family approximates the preconditioner with structure.

Shampoo maintains per-dimension preconditioners for each weight tensor. For W∈Rm×nW \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}W∈Rm×n it keeps L∈Rm×mL \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}L∈Rm×m and R∈Rn×nR \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}R∈Rn×n accumulating GG⊤GG^\topGG⊤ and G⊤GG^\top GG⊤G, and updates with

W←W−η L−1/4GR−1/4.W \leftarrow W - \eta\, L^{-1/4} G R^{-1/4}.W←W−ηL−1/4GR−1/4.

This is a Kronecker-factored approximation to the full preconditioner: O(m2+n2)O(m^2+n^2)O(m2+n2) rather than O(m2n2)O(m^2n^2)O(m2n2). SOAP stabilises it by running Adam in Shampoo’s eigenbasis, which reduces the frequency of the expensive eigendecompositions.

Muon takes a different angle: orthogonalise the momentum matrix (via a few Newton–Schulz iterations) before applying it, so the update has uniform singular values. Applied to hidden weight matrices only — embeddings and the output head stay on AdamW. Reported wall-clock speedups of roughly 2× over AdamW at scale, and it has been used in production LLM training runs, which is a meaningful bar.

How much of this is settled

AdamW has been the default for a decade and remains hard to beat robustly. The matrix-preconditioner family (Shampoo, SOAP, Muon) shows real gains in careful head-to-head comparisons at scale, and adoption is still early. Treat “AdamW has been superseded” as premature and “nothing beats AdamW” as out of date.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

AdamW is still the default and is no longer unchallenged. Muon in particular has moved from a blog post to production LLM training with published scaling results. The gains are on wall-clock time to a given loss, which is the metric that matters for large runs.

WSD schedules are displacing cosine for large training. Not because they reach better final loss, but because they decouple the schedule from a pre-committed horizon — you can extend a run, or fork several decay phases from one trunk.

Warmup is better understood as an Adam problem than an architecture problem. Pre-norm transformers need far less warmup than post-norm ones, and variance- rectified variants of Adam reduce the need further. Both point at early second-moment estimation as the underlying cause.

Precision interacts with optimization more than people expect. bf16 training with fp32 optimizer states is standard; pure low-precision optimizer states cause subtle divergence. Adam’s ϵ\epsilonϵ and the second moment are particularly sensitive, and several reported training instabilities have traced back to precision rather than to hyperparameters.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No theory predicts a good learning rate. It is found by sweep, and the transfer of hyperparameters across scale is only partly solved (μP and relatives help and are not universal).

  • Why Adam beats SGD on transformers is unresolved. On CNNs, well-tuned SGD with momentum matches or beats Adam. On transformers it does not, by a wide margin, and there is no accepted explanation — proposals include heavy-tailed gradient noise and the conditioning of attention layers.

  • Loss spikes. Large runs experience sudden loss increases that sometimes recover and sometimes do not. Mitigations exist (skip the batch, lower the rate, clip harder); the cause is not understood.

  • The batch-size ceiling is empirical. There is a critical batch size beyond which more parallelism gives no sample-efficiency benefit. Estimating it in advance is not solved.

  • Second-order methods are hard to evaluate fairly. Comparisons are confounded by tuning budget, and an under-tuned baseline makes any new optimizer look good. Read optimizer papers with the same scepticism you would apply to a benchmark leaderboard.
