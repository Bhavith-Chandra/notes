In-Context Learning

Show a language model three examples of a task in its prompt and it does the task — with no gradient step, no weight change, nothing persisted. That should not obviously be possible, and explaining it has produced four competing accounts that are all partially right. The most useful finding for practitioners is also the most deflating: the input–label mappings in your examples matter far less than the format, and models often perform nearly as well when the labels are wrong.

What you should walk away with

  • No weights change. Ever. The “learning” happens entirely in the forward pass, in activations that vanish when the context does. Whatever this is, it is not learning in the sense the rest of machine learning uses the word.
  • Correct labels contribute surprisingly little. Min et al. replaced demonstration labels with random ones and performance dropped only slightly. What the examples supply is the label space, the input distribution, and the format — not the mapping.
  • There are four explanations and they are not mutually exclusive. Induction heads (mechanistic), implicit Bayesian inference (statistical), implicit gradient descent (algorithmic), and task vectors (representational). Each is well-supported in its own regime.
  • A prompt compresses to a single vector. Task and function vector work shows the demonstrations produce an activation that can be extracted, transplanted into an unrelated context, and made to induce the task on its own.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The phenomenon

Section titled “The phenomenon”

Put this in a prompt:

The model outputs fromage. It was not trained on this task, no gradient was computed, and the model’s parameters are bit-for-bit identical before and after.

Nothing was learned in the usual sense. Ask the same model the same question in a fresh conversation and it has no memory of having done it.

Why this is strange

Section titled “Why this is strange”

Every other form of learning in machine learning changes something persistent. Training updates weights. Fine-tuning updates weights. Even retrieval changes an index.

In-context learning changes nothing. The adaptation lives entirely in the activations of a single forward pass and is discarded when the pass ends.

So either the model already knew how to do the task and the examples merely selected it, or the forward pass is running something that deserves to be called a learning algorithm. Both turn out to be partly true, and the argument over the proportions is the interesting part.

The result that reframes it

Section titled “The result that reframes it”

The obvious hypothesis: the model reads the examples, infers the input→output rule, and applies it. Min et al. tested this directly and the finding is deflating.

They replaced every label with a random one — sea otter -> menthe poivrée — and measured performance. It dropped only a little.

If the model were inferring a mapping, corrupting the mapping should destroy performance. It did not. So the demonstrations are mostly supplying something else.

What the examples actually provide

Min et al. isolated four things demonstrations carry, and found the mapping is the least important:

  1. The label space — which outputs are even on the table.
  2. The input distribution — what kind of thing you are asking about.
  3. The format — that the answer follows an arrow, is one word, is lowercase.
  4. The input–label mapping — which turns out to contribute least.

The practical reading: your examples are configuring the output channel, not teaching the task. Getting format and label space right matters more than hand-curating perfect examples — which is the opposite of most prompt-engineering advice.

Four explanations

Section titled “Four explanations”

  1. Induction heads. A concrete circuit — find an earlier occurrence of the current token, copy what followed it. Mechanistically verified, and it appears in training at exactly the moment ICL ability appears.

  2. Implicit Bayesian inference. The model has learned a mixture over latent “topics” or tasks from pretraining. The prompt is evidence, and the model is doing posterior inference over which task it is in.

  3. Implicit gradient descent. The forward pass through attention layers computes something equivalent to gradient steps on a small regression problem defined by the examples.

  4. Task vectors. The demonstrations compress into a single activation vector that encodes the task; the query is then processed under that vector’s influence.

These are compatible. A mechanism (1) can implement an algorithm (3) that performs an inference (2) via a representation (4). The disagreement is about which level explains the observed behaviour in real models on real tasks.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Induction heads

Section titled “Induction heads”

The mechanistic account, and the best-verified one. Covered in full on interpretability.

A two-head circuit across two layers implements match-and-copy: a previous-token head writes, at each position, information about what preceded it; an induction head then attends to positions following an earlier occurrence of the current token and copies what came next.

Why this counts as an explanation of ICL. Generalise “copy the literal token” to “copy the behaviour that followed a similar context”, and you have few-shot prompting. The prompt establishes a pattern; the induction mechanism continues it.

The evidence linking it to ICL is the strongest in this area:

  • Induction heads form during a narrow window of training.
  • That window coincides with a visible bump in the loss curve.
  • ICL ability appears at the same moment.
  • Ablating induction heads degrades ICL disproportionately.

Correlational plus causal, which is more than the other accounts have.

Implicit Bayesian inference

Section titled “Implicit Bayesian inference”

Xie et al.’s account. Pretraining data contains many latent “concepts” — a document is about some topic, in some style, following some implicit task. The model learns p(next token∣context)p(\text{next token} \mid \text{context})p(next token∣context) marginalised over these concepts.

At inference, the prompt is evidence about which concept you are in. The model implicitly computes a posterior over concepts and predicts under it.

p(y∣x,prompt)=∫p(y∣x,θ) p(θ∣prompt) dθp(y \mid x, \text{prompt}) = \int p(y\mid x, \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \text{prompt})\,d\thetap(y∣x,prompt)=∫p(y∣x,θ)p(θ∣prompt)dθ

Why this predicts the Min et al. result. If the prompt is evidence for which task, then the format and label space are highly informative — they identify the task — while individual label correctness is weak evidence that can be outweighed. The model is not learning the mapping; it is locating a mapping it already has.

Where it is weak. It explains selection among known tasks, and not learning genuinely novel ones. Which brings us to the case where models demonstrably do learn something new.

Implicit gradient descent

Section titled “Implicit gradient descent”

The most surprising line, and it starts with a clean experiment.

Garg et al. asked whether transformers can learn function classes in-context that have nothing to do with language. Train a transformer on sequences of (x1,f(x1),x2,f(x2),… )(x_1, f(x_1), x_2, f(x_2), \dots)(x1​,f(x1​),x2​,f(x2​),…) where fff is drawn from some class — linear functions, sparse linear, decision trees, small MLPs — and test on unseen fff.

They can, and they match the appropriate classical algorithm. For linear functions the transformer’s in-context predictions match ordinary least squares. For sparse linear it matches Lasso. It is not retrieving a memorised task; it is fitting a new function from examples in the forward pass.

Then two papers explained how. Akyürek et al. and von Oswald et al. showed — independently, with different methods — that a linear self-attention layer can implement one step of gradient descent on a least-squares objective, and that transformers trained on these tasks converge to weights that do approximately this.

So a kkk-layer transformer can implement kkk steps of gradient descent on a regression problem defined by the in-context examples. The forward pass is running an optimisation.

How far this transfers to real models

These results are on linear self-attention and synthetic function classes. Whether a production language model doing sentiment classification is running implicit gradient descent is a much stronger claim, and the evidence is circumstantial.

The honest summary: transformers are capable of implementing learning algorithms in their forward pass, and this is demonstrated constructively. Whether that is what they do on natural tasks is open, and probably varies by task — retrieval-like tasks look Bayesian, novel-function tasks look algorithmic.

Task vectors

Section titled “Task vectors”

The representational account, and the most practically striking.

Hendel et al. and Todd et al. showed — concurrently — that the demonstrations compress into a single activation vector. The procedure:

  1. Run the model on a prompt containing demonstrations.
  2. Extract the activation at a particular layer and position — typically the last token of the demonstrations.
  3. Run the model on a fresh prompt with no demonstrations at all, and patch that activation in.
  4. The model performs the task.

The demonstrations are not needed at inference once you have the vector. The task has been reduced to a point in activation space.

Why this is more than a curiosity. It says ICL factorises: the prompt’s job is to produce a task representation, and the query’s job is to be processed under it. Those are separable stages, which is why the vector transplants. It also connects directly to the linear representation hypothesis — tasks, like concepts, are directions.

Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

The setup

Section titled “The setup”

Given a prompt of kkk demonstrations and a query:

P=(x1,y1,x2,y2,…,xk,yk,xquery),P = \big(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2, \dots, x_k, y_k, x_{\text{query}}\big),P=(x1​,y1​,x2​,y2​,…,xk​,yk​,xquery​),

the model produces pθ(y∣P)p_\theta(y \mid P)pθ​(y∣P) with θ\thetaθ fixed. Define in-context learning as the property that accuracy improves with kkk:

E[A(k)]>E[A(0)],\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{A}(k)\big] > \mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{A}(0)\big],E[A(k)]>E[A(0)],

typically with a roughly log-linear improvement over a range of kkk before saturating.

The Bayesian account

Section titled “The Bayesian account”

Model pretraining as generating documents from a mixture over latent concepts θ\thetaθ:

p(doc)=∫p(doc∣θ) p(θ) dθ.p(\text{doc}) = \int p(\text{doc}\mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta .p(doc)=∫p(doc∣θ)p(θ)dθ.

A model trained to convergence on this distribution computes the posterior predictive:

p(y∣xquery,P)=∫p(y∣xquery,θ) p(θ∣P) dθ.p\big(y \mid x_{\text{query}}, P\big) = \int p\big(y\mid x_{\text{query}},\theta\big)\,p\big(\theta\mid P\big)\,d\theta .p(y∣xquery​,P)=∫p(y∣xquery​,θ)p(θ∣P)dθ.

As kkk grows, if the demonstrations are informative about θ⋆\theta^{\star}θ⋆, the posterior concentrates:

p(θ∣P)⟶δθ⋆,p\big(\theta \mid P\big) \longrightarrow \delta_{\theta^{\star}},p(θ∣P)⟶δθ⋆​,

recovering the task-specific predictor. Improvement with kkk is posterior concentration.

Why the prompt works despite low likelihood. Xie et al.’s key technical point: the prompt is not a typical sample from the pretraining distribution — the transitions between independent examples are unlikely under any single concept. The argument is that this “distribution mismatch” contributes an O(k)O(k)O(k) penalty that is dominated by the O(k)O(k)O(k) evidence for θ⋆\theta^\starθ⋆, provided the signal exceeds the mismatch. So the posterior still concentrates correctly, and the analysis predicts ICL should fail when the mismatch term dominates — which matches the empirical brittleness to prompt format.

So one linear attention layer = one gradient step, and LLL layers implement LLL steps. von Oswald et al. then showed empirically that transformers trained on these tasks converge to weights close to this construction, and that layer-wise in-context predictions track the corresponding GD iterates.

Akyürek et al.’s complementary result is a capacity statement: transformers of a given depth can implement ridge regression and exact least squares, and the algorithm the trained model implements shifts with problem dimension and depth — behaving like ridge in some regimes and like exact OLS in others.

Read this correctly. These establish that the function class of a transformer contains these algorithms and that training finds them on synthetic tasks. Neither establishes that a production LLM classifying sentiment is doing this.

Induction heads, formally

Section titled “Induction heads, formally”

From the interpretability treatment: the circuit uses K-composition, where layer 2’s keys are built from layer 1’s output rather than from the embedding.

For a sequence containing [A][B] … [A], the induction head’s attention score at the current position is

(WQ(2)e(A))⊤(WK(2)WOV(1)e(A)),\big(W_Q^{(2)} e(A)\big)^{\top}\big(W_K^{(2)} W_{OV}^{(1)} e(A)\big),(WQ(2)​e(A))⊤(WK(2)​WOV(1)​e(A)),

which is large exactly at positions following an earlier AAA, since layer 1 wrote AAA-information into those positions. The head then copies via WOV(2)W_{OV}^{(2)}WOV(2)​.

The phase-change evidence. Olsson et al. define an ICL score as the difference in loss between predicting the 500th token and the 50th token of a context — how much the model benefits from more context. Plotting it against training step shows a sharp improvement in a narrow window, coinciding with induction head formation and with a visible bump in the training loss.

That is a mechanistic account of an emergent capability, and it remains the field’s strongest such result.

Task vectors

Section titled “Task vectors”

Formally, let h−1(ℓ)(P)h^{(\ell)}_{-1}(P)h−1(ℓ)​(P) be the activation at layer ℓ\ellℓ, final demonstration position, for prompt PPP. The task vector is

vT=EP∼T[h−1(ℓ)(P)],v_{\mathcal{T}} = \mathbb{E}_{P\sim\mathcal{T}}\Big[h^{(\ell)}_{-1}(P)\Big],vT​=EP∼T​[h−1(ℓ)​(P)],

averaged over prompts for task T\mathcal{T}T. Then run the model on a demonstration-free query and patch:

h−1(ℓ)(xquery)←vT.h^{(\ell)}_{-1}\big(x_{\text{query}}\big) \leftarrow v_{\mathcal{T}}.h−1(ℓ)​(xquery​)←vT​.

The model performs T\mathcal{T}T.

Two properties worth noting. The vectors are causal — patching changes behaviour, so this is not a correlational probe. And they are compositional in limited ways: Todd et al. found vector arithmetic over function vectors can compose tasks, echoing the linear representation hypothesis.

The layer matters. Effectiveness peaks at intermediate layers. Too early and the task has not been formed; too late and the computation conditioned on it has already happened.

Scaling with the number of examples

Section titled “Scaling with the number of examples”

Empirically, accuracy grows roughly logarithmically over a range:

A(k)≈a+blog⁡k,\mathcal{A}(k) \approx a + b\log k,A(k)≈a+blogk,

before saturating. The saturation point moved substantially with long-context models.

Many-shot ICL (Agarwal et al., 2024) revisited this with hundreds or thousands of examples and found continued gains well past the few-shot regime, plus two things that are practically useful:

  • Reinforced ICL — replace human-written rationales with model-generated ones filtered for correctness, which removes the human bottleneck on example count.
  • Unsupervised ICL — supply only inputs, no outputs, and still see gains, which is further evidence that the input distribution rather than the mapping is doing much of the work.

Many-shot also reduces sensitivity to example ordering and can override pretraining priors that few-shot cannot — suggesting the regimes are qualitatively different, not just quantitatively.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

The explanations have stratified rather than converged. Induction heads explain the mechanism and the training-time phase change. The Bayesian account explains task selection and the robustness to wrong labels. Implicit gradient descent explains genuine in-context function fitting in controlled settings. Task vectors explain the representational format. The field has largely stopped treating these as competitors.

Many-shot changed the practical picture. With long contexts, hundreds of examples are affordable, and the resulting behaviour is closer to lightweight fine-tuning than to the classic few-shot phenomenon — including overriding pretraining priors, which few-shot could not do.

Task vectors are a genuine capability, not just an analysis tool. Extracting a task into a vector means you can apply it without spending context on demonstrations, which is directly useful when context is the scarce resource.

Reasoning models complicate the story. Models trained with RL on verifiable rewards do much of the adaptation through generated reasoning tokens rather than through the prompt’s demonstrations. Whether that is still in-context learning, or a different capability wearing the same clothes, is not settled.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • We cannot predict when ICL will work. Performance is highly sensitive to format, ordering, and example selection, and no theory predicts the sensitivity in advance.

  • The gap between toy and real remains. The implicit-gradient-descent results are on linear attention and synthetic functions. Nothing establishes the same mechanism operates on natural language tasks.

  • Ordering effects are unexplained. Permuting the same demonstrations can swing accuracy substantially. Recency and primacy effects are documented, and no account predicts their magnitude.

  • The relationship to fine-tuning is unclear. ICL and fine-tuning sometimes reach similar performance by apparently different routes. Whether they induce similar internal computations is open — task vectors suggest partly yes.

  • Nothing persists. Every conversation starts from zero. Whether that is a limitation to fix or a property to preserve is a design question the field has not settled.

Practical guidance

Section titled “Practical guidance”
  • Fix the format first. It contributes more than the labels. Consistent separators, consistent casing, consistent answer shape.
  • Cover the label space. Every possible output should appear at least once among the demonstrations.
  • Match the input distribution to your real queries. This matters more than choosing “good” examples.
  • Test ordering. It is a real variable; if performance is borderline, permuting is a cheap thing to try.
  • If context is scarce, consider extracting a task vector rather than spending tokens on demonstrations.
  • If context is plentiful, use many more examples than the classic 3–5. The gains continue much further than the few-shot literature suggested.

Reading path

Section titled “Reading path”
