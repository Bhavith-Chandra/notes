Fine-Tuning and PEFT

Full fine-tuning of a 70B model needs roughly a terabyte of GPU memory, almost none of it for the weights themselves — the optimizer states dominate. LoRA sidesteps this by freezing the model and training a low-rank update, cutting trainable parameters by three to four orders of magnitude with little quality loss. The reason it works is a hypothesis about adaptation being intrinsically low-rank, and that hypothesis also explains what fine-tuning cannot do.

What you should walk away with

  • Optimizer state, not weights, is what makes full fine-tuning expensive. Adam keeps two fp32 moments per parameter. For a 70B model that is roughly 560 GB before you have stored a single activation.
  • LoRA trains BABABA instead of ΔW\Delta WΔW. With rank 16 on a 4096×4096 matrix you train 131k parameters instead of 16.8M — a 128× reduction — and merge them back at inference for zero added latency.
  • Fine-tuning changes behaviour far more readily than knowledge. It is excellent at format, tone, and task framing; it is an unreliable and expensive way to install facts. For facts, retrieval usually wins.
  • Catastrophic forgetting is proportional to how much you move the weights. LoRA’s constrained update is partly why it forgets less than full fine-tuning — a side benefit that is often the deciding factor.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Where the memory actually goes

Section titled “Where the memory actually goes”

The intuition that a 70B model needs 140 GB (at fp16) to fine-tune is wrong by roughly a factor of eight. The accounting:

ComponentBytes per parameter70B model
Weights (fp16)2140 GB
Gradients (fp16)2140 GB
Adam first moment (fp32)4280 GB
Adam second moment (fp32)4280 GB
Total12~840 GB

Plus activations. The optimizer alone is four times the size of the model, and this is the number that decides whether fine-tuning is possible on your hardware.

So the useful question is not “how do we shrink the model?” but “how do we shrink the set of parameters we optimise?” Freeze the weights and only a small trainable set carries gradients and optimizer state.

The low-rank bet

Section titled “The low-rank bet”

Here is the hypothesis LoRA rests on.

A pretrained model already knows an enormous amount. Adapting it to summarise legal documents does not require rebuilding that knowledge — it requires a comparatively small adjustment. So the weight change ΔW\Delta WΔW, though it has the same shape as WWW, should be much simpler in structure than WWW itself.

Concretely: express ΔW\Delta WΔW as the product of two thin matrices, BBB (d×rd \times rd×r) and AAA (r×dr \times dr×d), with rrr tiny — 8, 16, 64. Their product has the full shape but rank at most rrr. You train BBB and AAA; WWW never moves.

For d=4096d = 4096d=4096 and r=16r = 16r=16 that is 131,072 trainable parameters where full fine-tuning would have 16,777,216. And since only those carry optimizer state, the memory problem largely evaporates.

Two properties that make LoRA practical rather than merely clever

No inference cost. BABABA can be added into WWW after training, giving a single merged weight matrix. Unlike adapter layers, there is nothing extra to run.

Swappable. A rank-16 LoRA is a few megabytes. You can keep dozens for different tasks and hot-swap them against one base model in memory — which is why the entire open-source fine-tuning ecosystem is built on it.

What fine-tuning is good and bad at

Section titled “What fine-tuning is good and bad at”

Worth stating early, because it is the most common misconception in applied work.

Good at: output format, tone and persona, task framing, following a specific schema, domain-specific style, refusing or not refusing particular categories.

Bad at: installing new facts. It can be done with enough data and epochs, and it is unreliable — the model may reproduce the fact in the training format and fail to use it in conversation, or overwrite adjacent knowledge in the process.

If you want the model to know your documents, retrieve them. If you want the model to behave differently, fine-tune. Reaching for fine-tuning to inject knowledge is the standard expensive mistake.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The LoRA layer

Section titled “The LoRA layer”

Choices that matter

Section titled “Choices that matter”

  1. Which matrices. The original paper adapted only attention WQW_QWQ​ and WVW_VWV​. Later practice found adapting all linear layers — including the MLP — works better, and the MLP layers matter more than the attention ones for most tasks. Default to all.

  2. Rank rrr. 8–16 handles style and format. 64–128 for harder domain shifts. Beyond that the returns are thin and you should ask whether full fine-tuning is the honest answer.

  3. Alpha. The update is scaled by α/r\alpha/rα/r. Setting α=2r\alpha = 2rα=2r is a common default. The scaling exists so that changing rrr does not change the effective learning rate — which means you should not re-tune the learning rate every time you change rank.

  4. Learning rate. LoRA tolerates and wants much higher rates than full fine-tuning — typically 1e-4 to 3e-4 versus 1e-5. Using a full-fine-tuning learning rate is the most common reason a LoRA run underperforms.

  5. Initialisation. AAA random, BBB zero. So BA=0BA = 0BA=0 at step zero and the model is exactly the pretrained one. Initialising both randomly injects noise into a well-trained model and degrades the start.

QLoRA

Section titled “QLoRA”

Three ideas stacked, which together put 65B fine-tuning on a single 48 GB GPU.

4-bit NormalFloat (NF4). A quantisation format whose levels are the quantiles of a normal distribution. Since pretrained weights are approximately normally distributed, this is information-theoretically closer to optimal than uniform 4-bit levels for the same bit budget.

Double quantisation. The quantisation constants themselves are quantised, saving roughly another 0.37 bits per parameter. Small, and it adds up at 65B.

Paged optimizers. Optimizer state pages to CPU memory on GPU pressure, using unified memory, which prevents the out-of-memory crashes that otherwise occur at long sequence lengths.

The base model is stored in 4-bit and dequantised to bf16 on the fly for each matmul. LoRA adapters remain in bf16. Gradients flow through the dequantised weights to the adapters; the 4-bit weights never update.

QLoRA's real cost is speed, not quality

Dequantising on every forward pass costs compute. QLoRA is typically noticeably slower per step than LoRA on a model that already fits. It is a memory solution. If your model fits in bf16, use plain LoRA.

Why α/r\alpha/rα/r

Section titled “Why α/r\alpha/rα/r”

Without the scaling, changing rrr changes the magnitude of a typical update, and you would have to re-tune the learning rate. With B=0B=0B=0 initially, early updates give ΔW≈αr ΔB A\Delta W \approx \frac{\alpha}{r}\,\Delta B\, AΔW≈rα​ΔBA, and since AAA has rrr rows contributing to each output, the sum over the rank index grows like rrr under standard initialisation. Dividing by rrr cancels that growth, so the effective step size on ΔW\Delta WΔW is approximately rrr-independent.

Practical consequence: α\alphaα and the learning rate are redundant knobs. Fix α=2r\alpha = 2rα=2r and tune the learning rate only. Tuning both is a common waste of a sweep.

The intrinsic dimension argument

Section titled “The intrinsic dimension argument”

LoRA’s justification is the intrinsic dimensionality hypothesis: the minimum number of parameters needed to reach within ϵ\epsilonϵ of full fine-tuning performance is far smaller than the parameter count. Empirically, for many tasks a few thousand well-chosen directions suffice.

If adaptation lives in a low-dimensional manifold, then restricting ΔW\Delta WΔW to rank rrr loses little — provided the manifold is approximately linear in weight space, which is the assumption that can fail.

Where it fails. Tasks requiring genuinely new capability — a new language, a new modality, substantially new knowledge — need higher rank, sometimes ranks where LoRA’s advantage disappears. The rank at which LoRA stops matching full fine-tuning is a rough measure of how far the task sits from the pretraining distribution, and it is a useful diagnostic: if you need r=512r = 512r=512, the low-rank assumption is telling you it does not hold for your task.

qi=12(Φ−1 ⁣(i17)+Φ−1 ⁣(i+117)),i=1,…,16,q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left(\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{i}{17}\right) + \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{i+1}{17}\right)\right), \quad i = 1,\dots,16,qi​=21​(Φ−1(17i​)+Φ−1(17i+1​)),i=1,…,16,

Quantisation is applied block-wise (typically 64 weights per block) with a per-block scale, so local variation in weight magnitude is handled. Double quantisation then quantises those per-block scales, saving roughly

Catastrophic forgetting

Section titled “Catastrophic forgetting”

Fine-tuning on a narrow distribution degrades performance elsewhere. A useful frame: the loss on the original distribution grows roughly with the distance moved through weight space, to second order,

Lorig(W0+ΔW)≈Lorig(W0)+12 ΔW⊤H ΔW,\mathcal{L}_{\text{orig}}(W_0 + \Delta W) \approx \mathcal{L}_{\text{orig}}(W_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta W^{\top} H\, \Delta W,Lorig​(W0​+ΔW)≈Lorig​(W0​)+21​ΔW⊤HΔW,

since ∇Lorig(W0)≈0\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{orig}}(W_0) \approx 0∇Lorig​(W0​)≈0 at convergence. Damage is therefore quadratic in ∥ΔW∥\|\Delta W\|∥ΔW∥ weighted by the Hessian.

LoRA constrains ΔW\Delta WΔW to a rank-rrr subspace, bounding how far you can travel and — more importantly — restricting which directions you can travel in. This is a large part of why LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning, and it is a benefit that rarely appears in the headline comparison but often decides the choice in practice.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

LoRA is the default and the “adapt all layers” correction is settled. The original attention-only recommendation is outdated; adapting MLP layers matters more for most tasks. Any implementation still defaulting to WQ,WVW_Q, W_VWQ​,WV​ only is leaving quality on the table.

The LoRA-versus-full-fine-tuning question depends on task distance. For instruction tuning, style, and format — near the pretraining distribution — LoRA matches full fine-tuning at a fraction of the cost. For new languages or genuinely new capabilities, full fine-tuning still wins, and the required rank tells you which regime you are in.

Serving many adapters concurrently is solved. Batched multi-LoRA serving lets a single base model handle requests for different adapters in one batch, which is what makes per-customer fine-tuning economically viable.

Fine-tuning is losing ground to prompting and retrieval for knowledge tasks, and gaining ground for behaviour. Long context and good retrieval removed most of the “fine-tune it on our documents” use case; what remains is genuinely about changing how the model acts.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No principled way to pick the rank. It is swept. The relationship between task difficulty, dataset size, and required rank is not characterised.

  • LoRA on MoE models is unreliable. Adapting a routed model can shift routing in ways that destabilise it, and the interaction is poorly understood.

  • Composing adapters does not work well. Adding two LoRAs trained separately rarely gives you both behaviours; they interfere. Methods to merge them exist and none is robust.

  • Knowledge injection remains unreliable. The mechanism by which fine-tuning installs a fact — and why it often fails to generalise beyond the training phrasing — is not understood. Retrieval is the pragmatic answer and it is a workaround, not an explanation.

  • The alignment tax is real and under-measured. Fine-tuning on a narrow task measurably degrades safety behaviours, including when the fine-tuning data is benign. This has direct deployment consequences and is not routinely checked.
