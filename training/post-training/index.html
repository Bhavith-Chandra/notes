Post-Training and Alignment

A pretrained language model predicts what text is likely, not what text is useful. Post-training closes that gap. The classical recipe learns a reward model from human preference comparisons and optimises against it with RL; DPO showed the reward model can be skipped entirely; and the 2025 shift is toward rewards that are verifiable — where a program, not a human or a learned proxy, decides whether the answer is right.

What you should walk away with

  • Pretraining and helpfulness are different objectives. A base model asked a question may continue with more questions, because that is what the internet does. Post-training is what makes “answer it” the likely continuation.
  • Preferences are used because absolute ratings are unreliable. Humans agree poorly on “rate this 1–10” and much better on “which of these two is better”. The Bradley–Terry model turns those comparisons into a scalar reward.
  • DPO’s result is an identity, not an approximation. The optimal policy under a KL-constrained reward objective has a closed form, which can be inverted to express reward in terms of the policy — so you can optimise the preference likelihood directly and never build a reward model.
  • Every learned reward model gets gamed. Optimise hard enough against a proxy and you find its errors rather than the intent. The KL penalty is a leash on how far you may stray, and it is a mitigation rather than a fix.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The base model problem

Section titled “The base model problem”

Pretrain on the internet and you get a system that continues text plausibly. Ask it “What is the capital of France?” and a perfectly good continuation is:

What is the population of Germany? What is the largest city in Italy?

That is not a failure. Lists of quiz questions exist on the internet, so this is a likely continuation. It is simply not a useful one, and the model was never told the difference.

Post-training installs that difference. It shifts the model from “what usually follows?” to “what would a helpful assistant say?”.

Why comparisons rather than scores

Section titled “Why comparisons rather than scores”

The first instinct is to have humans rate responses out of ten. This works badly. Different annotators anchor differently; the same annotator drifts over a session; nobody can articulate why a 7 is not a 6.

Ask instead: “Which of these two responses is better?” Agreement rates jump dramatically. People are poor at absolute judgement and good at relative judgement — a robust finding well outside machine learning.

So the pipeline collects pairwise comparisons and fits a model that assigns scores consistent with them. This is the same statistical problem as ranking chess players from match outcomes, and it uses the same century-old model.

The three stages, and why the third is hard

Section titled “The three stages, and why the third is hard”

  1. Supervised fine-tuning. Show the model thousands of high-quality demonstrations of assistant behaviour. Ordinary next-token training on curated data. This does most of the format-learning work.

  2. Reward modelling. Collect comparisons, train a model that scores responses in agreement with human preference.

  3. Reinforcement learning. Optimise the language model to score highly under that reward model.

Stage 3 is where it gets difficult, for a reason worth stating plainly: the reward model is a proxy, and optimisers exploit proxies. Push hard enough and the policy stops producing better answers and starts producing answers that trip the reward model’s blind spots — excessive length, sycophancy, confident formatting over correct content. This is Goodhart’s law with a gradient.

What the KL penalty is really doing

Every RLHF objective includes a term penalising divergence from the pre-RL model. It is not there for regularisation in the usual sense — it is a leash. It bounds how far the policy may travel into regions where the reward model has never seen data and its predictions are fiction. Remove it and the model reliably degenerates into reward-hacking gibberish.

What PPO actually costs

Section titled “What PPO actually costs”

The classical RLHF setup holds four models in memory: the policy being trained, a frozen reference for the KL term, the reward model, and a value model for advantage estimation. Plus generation during training, which is sequential and slow.

This is the practical reason DPO landed so hard. It needs two models (policy and reference), no generation, and no RL machinery — it is a classification loss on pre-collected pairs.

  • PPO
  • DPO
  • GRPO
  • RLVR

Policy-gradient with a clipped surrogate objective. The clipping prevents any single update from moving the policy too far, which is what makes on-policy RL stable enough to use here.

Where it still wins: PPO is on-policy — it generates fresh responses and learns from the reward assigned to them. DPO only ever sees the fixed preference dataset. When the policy drifts away from the distribution that dataset covers, DPO has nothing to say and PPO does. Careful head-to-head comparisons have generally found well-tuned PPO ahead on hard tasks, at substantially more engineering cost.

The insight: for the KL-constrained reward-maximisation objective, the optimal policy has a closed form in terms of the reward. Invert it, and the reward has a closed form in terms of the policy. Substitute that into the Bradley–Terry preference likelihood, and the reward model cancels out.

What remains is a binary classification loss on the log-probability ratios of chosen versus rejected responses. No reward model, no sampling, no value network.

The known failure mode: DPO increases the margin between chosen and rejected responses, and it can do that by pushing the rejected response’s likelihood down rather than the chosen one’s up. Both likelihoods can decrease, with the model drifting to some third distribution neither annotator endorsed. Variants (IPO, KTO, and the SFT-regularised forms) exist to address this.

Introduced in DeepSeekMath, and now widely used for reasoning models.

The observation: PPO’s value network is expensive and hard to train. But if you sample a group of GGG responses to the same prompt, the group’s mean reward is a perfectly serviceable baseline — no learned critic needed. Advantage becomes the response’s reward normalised within its group.

This removes one of the four models and much of the tuning difficulty, which is why GRPO became the default for RL on verifiable tasks where you can afford to sample many completions per prompt.

Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards. The reward is not learned at all — it is computed. Run the generated code against unit tests. Check the maths answer against ground truth. Parse the output format.

This eliminates reward hacking against a learned proxy, because there is no proxy. The reward is the actual thing you wanted.

DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated that RL on verifiable rewards alone — no reward model, no human preference data — induces extended chain-of-thought reasoning. The model learns to think longer because thinking longer produces correct answers, and correct answers are what is rewarded.

The limitation is scope. Verifiable rewards exist for maths, code, and formal logic. They do not exist for “is this essay good”, “is this response kind”, or most of what people actually use assistants for.
Tier 3 · Formal

Reward modelling

Section titled “Reward modelling”

Under the Bradley–Terry model, the probability that response ywy_wyw​ is preferred to yly_lyl​ given prompt xxx is

p(yw≻yl∣x)=exp⁡r(x,yw)exp⁡r(x,yw)+exp⁡r(x,yl)=σ(r(x,yw)−r(x,yl)).p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp r(x,y_w)}{\exp r(x,y_w) + \exp r(x,y_l)} = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\big).p(yw​≻yl​∣x)=expr(x,yw​)+expr(x,yl​)expr(x,yw​)​=σ(r(x,yw​)−r(x,yl​)).

Fit by maximum likelihood:

LRM=− E(x,yw,yl)∼D[log⁡σ(rϕ(x,yw)−rϕ(x,yl))].\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big].LRM​=−E(x,yw​,yl​)∼D​[logσ(rϕ​(x,yw​)−rϕ​(x,yl​))].

The reward is only identified up to an additive constant per prompt. Adding c(x)c(x)c(x) to every response’s reward leaves all differences — and hence the likelihood — unchanged. This is why reward values are not comparable across prompts and why reward-model “scores” reported in isolation are close to meaningless.

DPO’s derivation

Section titled “DPO’s derivation”

Take logarithms of the solution above and solve for rrr:

r(x,y)=βlog⁡π∗(y∣x)πref(y∣x)+βlog⁡Z(x).r(x,y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).r(x,y)=βlogπref​(y∣x)π∗(y∣x)​+βlogZ(x).

r(x,yw)−r(x,yl)=β[log⁡π∗(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−log⁡π∗(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x)].r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) = \beta\left[\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right].r(x,yw​)−r(x,yl​)=β[logπref​(yw​∣x)π∗(yw​∣x)​−logπref​(yl​∣x)π∗(yl​∣x)​].

Replacing π∗\pi^*π∗ with the trainable πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ gives the DPO loss:

 LDPO=− E[log⁡σ ⁣(βlog⁡πθ(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−βlog⁡πθ(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x))] \boxed{\ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]\ } LDPO​=−E[logσ(βlogπref​(yw​∣x)πθ​(yw​∣x)​−βlogπref​(yl​∣x)πθ​(yl​∣x)​)] ​

The cancellation of Z(x)Z(x)Z(x) is the entire trick, and it depends on both responses sharing a prompt. This is exact — DPO is not approximating RLHF, it is optimising the same objective in different coordinates, under the assumption that the preference data is generated by a Bradley–Terry model with the optimal policy’s implicit reward.

Where the equivalence leaks. The derivation assumes the preference dataset is on-policy for πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​. In practice it is collected once, from some other model. As πθ\pi_\thetaπθ​ moves away from that distribution, the implicit reward is being evaluated where no data exists — which is exactly the extrapolation problem RLHF’s KL term guards against, reappearing in a different place.

The gradient makes the failure mode visible:

∇θLDPO=−β E[σ(r^l−r^w)(∇θlog⁡πθ(yw∣x)−∇θlog⁡πθ(yl∣x))],\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\sigma\big(\hat r_l - \hat r_w\big)\Big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big],∇θ​LDPO​=−βE[σ(r^l​−r^w​)(∇θ​logπθ​(yw​∣x)−∇θ​logπθ​(yl​∣x))],

with r^\hat rr^ the implicit rewards. The update raises log⁡πθ(yw)\log\pi_\theta(y_w)logπθ​(yw​) and lowers log⁡πθ(yl)\log\pi_\theta(y_l)logπθ​(yl​), weighted by how wrong the current implicit reward ordering is. Nothing constrains the absolute level of either — hence the observed drift where both fall.

The min⁡\minmin with the clipped term makes the objective pessimistic: when the advantage is positive, gain is capped at 1+ε1+\varepsilon1+ε; when negative, the penalty is not capped below 1−ε1-\varepsilon1−ε. GRPO

Section titled “GRPO”

Drop the value network. For a group of GGG sampled responses to prompt xxx:

A^i=ri−mean⁡(r1,…,rG)std⁡(r1,…,rG),\hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)},A^i​=std(r1​,…,rG​)ri​−mean(r1​,…,rG​)​, LGRPO=− E[1G∑i=1Gmin⁡(ρiA^i, clip⁡(ρi,1−ε,1+ε)A^i)]+β KL(πθ ∥ πref).\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\big(\rho_i \hat A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_i, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\big)\right] + \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big).LGRPO​=−E[G1​i=1∑G​min(ρi​A^i​, clip(ρi​,1−ε,1+ε)A^i​)]+βKL(πθ​∥πref​).

The group mean is an unbiased baseline for the prompt’s expected reward, which is all the value network was providing. Standardising by the group’s standard deviation additionally normalises the advantage scale across prompts of differing difficulty — a prompt where all GGG responses score similarly contributes little gradient, which is sensible.

Trade-off: you need GGG generations per prompt (typically 8–64) instead of one. Cheaper in models and tuning, more expensive in sampling. For verifiable tasks where reward evaluation is a cheap function call, this is clearly the right trade.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

Verifiable rewards are the most significant recent shift. DeepSeek-R1 showed that RL against automatically-checkable rewards induces long chain-of-thought reasoning without any human preference data or reward model — the model discovers that thinking longer improves accuracy, and lengthens its reasoning accordingly. This sidesteps reward hacking entirely for the domains where verification is possible.

DPO won on adoption; PPO retains a quality edge on hard tasks. DPO’s simplicity made it the default for open-source post-training. Careful comparisons at scale still favour well-tuned on-policy RL, and the gap is largest where the preference dataset poorly covers the policy’s output distribution. Iterative and online DPO variants — regenerate preferences from the current policy, retrain — close much of it, at the cost of reintroducing the sampling loop DPO removed.

The scope problem is the field’s real constraint. Verifiable rewards cover maths, code, and formal reasoning. They do not cover helpfulness, tone, honesty, or judgement — which is most of what assistants do. Those still need learned reward models, with all the hacking risk that entails.

Reward hacking is documented and persistent. Length bias is the canonical example: reward models systematically prefer longer responses, so RLHF makes models verbose. Explicit length normalisation helps and does not eliminate it. Sycophancy is the same phenomenon on a different axis — agreeing with the user is rated higher than being right.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Preference data does not capture what people want. Annotators rate what looks good, which correlates imperfectly with what is good. A confidently-worded wrong answer often beats a hedged correct one, and the model learns exactly that.

  • No principled way to set β\betaβ. Too small and the policy hacks the reward; too large and it barely changes. It is tuned by inspection.

  • Multi-objective alignment is unsolved. Helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty conflict. Collapsing them to one scalar reward forces a trade-off nobody explicitly chose. Constitutional AI addresses part of this by generating critiques from written principles, which at least makes the trade-offs legible.

  • Evaluation is circular. Post-training is increasingly evaluated by LLM judges, which have their own biases (including a preference for length and for their own outputs). Optimising against a judge is reward hacking one level up.

  • Distribution shift in DPO. The off-policy assumption breaks as training proceeds, and there is no clean diagnostic for when it has broken.
