Self-Supervised Learning

Self-supervised learning manufactures a supervised problem out of unlabelled data by hiding part of the input and asking the model to recover it. The reason it took over is not elegance — it is a supply curve. Labelled data is bounded by human effort; unlabelled data is bounded by storage. Once a self-supervised objective works at all, it inherits a scaling trajectory that supervised learning cannot match.

What you should walk away with

  • The pretext task is a means, not an end. Nobody wants a model that predicts masked patches. You want the representation it was forced to build in order to do so, and the task is chosen for what it forces, not what it produces.
  • There are three families, distinguished by what they compare. Generative compares to the input, contrastive compares to other samples, predictive compares to the model’s own output. Everything else is a variation.
  • The augmentation set is the specification. In contrastive learning you are declaring what should be ignored. SimCLR’s ablations showed this choice dominates architecture, and it means a wrong choice silently destroys information you needed.
  • Evaluation is the field’s weakest link. Linear probing is standard and known to be a poor proxy — it measures what a linear head can extract, not what the representation contains. Every reported comparison rests on it anyway.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The bargain

Section titled “The bargain”

Supervised learning needs someone to write down the answer. A million labelled images means a million human judgements — expensive, slow, and capped by how many people you can pay.

Self-supervised learning gets the answer for free by hiding part of the data and using it as its own label. Cover a word, ask what it was — the sentence already contains the answer. Cover a patch of an image, ask what was there — the image already contains the answer. No annotator required, so the data supply becomes “everything on the internet” rather than “everything we could afford to label”.

Why hiding things teaches anything

Section titled “Why hiding things teaches anything”

This is the part that deserves a moment, because it isn’t obvious.

Consider: “She poured the coffee into the ____ and carried it to her desk.”

To fill the blank you need to know that coffee is liquid, that liquids go in containers, that the container is portable, that desks are where people work. The blank is trivial to check and hard to answer, and answering it requires assembling a great deal of knowledge about how the world works.

That asymmetry is the entire mechanism. The task is cheap to construct and expensive to solve, and everything the model builds in order to solve it is the thing you actually wanted.

Choose the task for what it forces

A pretext task is good if solving it requires the structure you want. Predicting image rotation was an early attempt and it turned out to be solvable by spotting sky-at-the-top — a shortcut that teaches almost nothing. The design question is always: can this be solved by a cheap trick?

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

Generative: predict the input

Section titled “Generative: predict the input”

The oldest and conceptually simplest. Corrupt, then restore.

Masked language modelling (BERT). Hide 15% of tokens, predict them from both directions. Of the masked positions, 80% become [MASK], 10% a random token, 10% are left unchanged — a detail that exists because [MASK] never appears at fine-tuning time, so training exclusively on it creates a train/test mismatch.

Masked image modelling (MAE). Hide 75% of image patches, reconstruct pixels. The high ratio is the key design choice and the reason it works: images are spatially redundant, so hiding 15% leaves the task solvable by local interpolation. At 75% the model must actually understand content. A secondary benefit is speed — the encoder only processes the visible 25%.

Next-token prediction (GPT). Predict token iii from tokens <i< i<i. Every position is a training example, which makes it the most sample-efficient objective per pass, and it is the reason decoder-only models scaled so well.

Contrastive: predict which sample

Section titled “Contrastive: predict which sample”

Take two augmented views of the same image. Pull their embeddings together. Push embeddings of different images apart.

  1. Augment. Random crop, colour jitter, grayscale, blur. Two independent draws from the same source image.
  2. Encode. Both views through the same encoder.
  3. Project. A small MLP head. Loss is computed on the projection; the representation you keep is the layer before it — this detail is worth several points of accuracy and is easy to get wrong.
  4. Contrast. InfoNCE over the batch: the matching view is the positive, the other 2(N−1)2(N-1)2(N−1) views are negatives.

Augmentations are a specification, and a dangerous one

SimCLR’s ablations found that the composition of augmentations matters more than anything architectural, and that random crop plus colour distortion is the critical pair. The reason is instructive: crops of the same image share a colour histogram, so without colour jitter the model can match views by colour alone and learn nothing else.

The flip side is a real hazard. Colour-invariance is exactly wrong if you are classifying ripeness, diagnosing rashes, or reading resistor bands. You are declaring what the model should be blind to, and if you declare wrong, the information is gone and no amount of downstream fine-tuning brings it back.

The negatives problem. InfoNCE’s quality depends on having many negatives. SimCLR used batches up to 8192, which requires substantial hardware. MoCo’s answer was a queue of embeddings from recent batches plus a momentum-updated encoder to keep those stale embeddings consistent — decoupling the number of negatives from the batch size. This is the practical route if you don’t have a TPU pod.

Predictive: predict your own representation

Section titled “Predictive: predict your own representation”

Covered in depth on the JEPA page; the summary is that a student network predicts the output of an EMA copy of itself, with a stop-gradient on the target. No negatives, no decoder — and the trivial constant solution has to be blocked structurally rather than by the loss.

Masked language modelling

Section titled “Masked language modelling”
LMLM=− Ex∼D EM[∑i∈Mlog⁡pθ(xi∣x∖M)],\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}\left[\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log p_\theta\big(x_i \mid x_{\setminus \mathcal{M}}\big)\right],LMLM​=−Ex∼D​EM​[i∈M∑​logpθ​(xi​∣x∖M​)],

Why 15%, and what the trade-off is. Let ρ\rhoρ be the mask ratio. The conditioning context has (1−ρ)n(1-\rho)n(1−ρ)n tokens and the supervised signal covers ρn\rho nρn positions. Small ρ\rhoρ gives rich context but few gradients per sequence; large ρ\rhoρ gives many gradients but impoverished context. The optimum depends on the redundancy of the modality — and this is exactly why images (highly redundant) use ρ=0.75\rho = 0.75ρ=0.75 while text (much less redundant) uses ρ=0.15\rho = 0.15ρ=0.15. The numbers differ by modality for a principled reason, not by convention.

Compared with causal LM. MLM conditions on both sides and supervises ρn\rho nρn positions; CLM conditions on the left only and supervises all nnn. MLM gets better representations per parameter; CLM gets more gradient per FLOP. Neither dominates, which is why the encoder-versus-decoder question stayed open.

The evaluation problem, stated precisely

Section titled “The evaluation problem, stated precisely”

The standard protocol: freeze the encoder, train a linear classifier on top, report accuracy.

acclinear=max⁡W,b E(x,y)[1{arg⁡max⁡(Wf(x)+b)=y}].\text{acc}_{\text{linear}} = \max_{W,b}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[\mathbb{1}\{\arg\max(W f(x) + b) = y\}\big] .acclinear​=W,bmax​ E(x,y)​[1{argmax(Wf(x)+b)=y}].

The problem is that this measures linear accessibility, not information content. A representation could encode a feature perfectly in a form a linear probe cannot read — via an XOR of two dimensions, say — and score zero. Alain & Bengio introduced probes as a diagnostic and were explicit that probe accuracy is a property of the probe-and-representation pair, not of the representation.

The alternatives are each unsatisfying in their own way:

  • Full fine-tuning — measures the fine-tuning recipe as much as the encoder, and washes out differences between pretraining methods.
  • k-NN classification — no learned head, so cleaner, but assumes Euclidean or cosine geometry is the right metric, which is only true for some families.
  • Alignment/uniformity — better, but defined relative to an augmentation family, which is circular for methods that claim to need no augmentations.

Every headline comparison between SSL methods rests on one of these. That is worth remembering when a paper reports a 0.4-point improvement.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where this stands, as of 2026

Section titled “Where this stands, as of 2026”

In vision, self-distillation is the practical default. DINOv2 features are what most practitioners reach for when they need general-purpose visual representations, and they transfer across a wide range of tasks without fine-tuning. The emergent segmentation behaviour in the DINO line — attention maps that outline objects with no segmentation supervision — remains the most striking qualitative evidence that these objectives capture something structural.

In language, SSL is simply how models are trained, to the point that the term has fallen out of use. Next-token prediction is self-supervised; nobody calls it that any more.

Masked reconstruction refuses to die. MAE remains competitive despite the theoretical argument that pixel reconstruction wastes capacity. The proposed explanation — aggressive masking makes the task semantic — is plausible and unproven, and this is a genuine open disagreement rather than a settled question.

Scaling behaviour is less characterised than for generative models. We know more data helps. We do not have clean compute-optimal curves comparable to Chinchilla, partly because there is no agreed loss to plot against.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Evaluation is unsolved and everything depends on it. No task-agnostic measure of representation quality exists. This is not a minor methodological gripe — it means the field’s ranking of its own methods is built on a proxy known to be biased.

  • Shortcut learning is hard to detect in advance. A pretext task solvable by a spurious cue teaches nothing, and you generally find out only after training. There is no principled way to audit a proposed task.

  • Augmentation design remains hand-crafted. Learning the augmentation distribution is an obvious idea that has not produced a robust method.

  • Modality transfer of recipes is poor. MAE’s 75% mask ratio does not transfer to text; BERT’s 15% does not transfer to images. Each modality needed its own tuning, and there is no theory predicting the right ratio from properties of the data.

  • We cannot say what a representation “contains”. Superposition means features share dimensions non-orthogonally, so neither individual neurons nor linear probes are the right unit of analysis. This connects directly to the interpretability work on sparse autoencoders.
