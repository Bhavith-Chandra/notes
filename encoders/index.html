Encoders

“Encoder” is one word doing three jobs. It names a neural component that compresses data into a vector, a position in an architecture that reads input rather than producing output, and a preprocessing step that turns categories into numbers. These are related but not the same, and conflating them is the most common source of confusion in this corner of ML. This page defines the shared idea, separates the three senses, and tells you which of the three child pages you actually want.

What you should walk away with

  • Every encoder does the same thing: it maps data into a space where the next step’s job is easier. The three senses differ only in what “easier” means and who consumes the output.
  • The output space is the whole design. An encoder is not judged by reconstruction quality but by whether distances, directions, and linear separability in its output space mean something useful.
  • Compression is a side effect, not the goal. A good encoder discards information — but it must discard the right information, and which information is right depends entirely on the downstream task.
  • The three senses have genuinely different failure modes. Neural encoders collapse; architectural encoders bottleneck; feature encoders leak. Knowing which family you are in tells you what to watch for.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The one idea underneath all of it

Section titled “The one idea underneath all of it”

Imagine describing a house to someone over the phone so they can decide whether to buy it. You do not read out the position of every brick. You say: three bedrooms, south-facing garden, twenty minutes from the station, needs a new roof.

You have just encoded the house. You took something enormous and messy and produced a short list of numbers and facts — deliberately throwing away almost everything — such that the specific decision your listener has to make is still possible. Read out the brick positions instead and you would have preserved far more information while making the decision much harder.

That is an encoder. A function that maps something big and awkward into something small and structured, chosen so that the thing you want to do next becomes easy.

The two halves of that sentence matter equally, and beginners usually only hear the first. Compression alone is trivial — you could hash the house to eight bytes and lose nothing about uniqueness, while making every downstream question impossible. The skill is in what you keep.

Why “the space” is the point

Section titled “Why “the space” is the point”

Here is where encoders become genuinely powerful rather than merely tidy.

If you encode houses well, then two houses with similar encodings really are similar houses. You can measure distance. You can cluster. You can draw a straight line that separates “houses I’d buy” from “houses I wouldn’t”, when no such line existed in the raw data. The famous demonstration of this is word2vec, where the encoded vector for king, minus man, plus woman, lands near the vector for queen — a relationship that has no counterpart whatsoever in the raw spelling of the words.

Nobody programmed that. It fell out of training an encoder on a task that had nothing to do with analogies. This is the recurring promise of encoders: train one on a cheap, abundant objective, and get a space where hard problems become easy.

A caveat you'll want later

The king - man + woman ≈ queen result is real but heavily cherry-picked, and later analyses showed the arithmetic often only works because the query vectors are excluded from the nearest-neighbour search. Treat it as an illustration of the idea, not as evidence of how reliably it holds.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

The shared anatomy

Section titled “The shared anatomy”

Strip away the differences and every encoder has the same four parts. Being able to name them for any given encoder is most of what “understanding” it means.

PartWhat it isExample (CLIP image encoder)
Input domainwhat it accepts224×224 RGB images
Output spacewhere it maps to, and its geometry512-dim unit hypersphere
Training signalwhat defines “good”agreement with the paired text encoder
Inductive biaswhat structure is assumedtranslation equivariance (CNN) or patch permutation (ViT)

The training signal is the one that determines everything. Two encoders with identical architectures trained on different signals produce spaces with entirely different properties — a ResNet trained on ImageNet classification and the same ResNet trained with SimCLR share every weight shape and almost no behaviour.

The taxonomy, by training signal

Section titled “The taxonomy, by training signal”

This is the organising axis worth internalising, because it predicts what an encoder will be good at better than architecture does.

The reason the right-hand branches dominate current research is simply arithmetic: labelled data is bounded by human effort, unlabelled data is bounded by storage. Once a self-supervised objective works at all, it inherits a data supply curve that supervised learning cannot match.

Choosing one, practically

Section titled “Choosing one, practically”

A decision procedure that is right most of the time

  1. Do you need to generate or reconstruct the input? → reconstructive (VAE, VQ-VAE, MAE). Nothing else gives you an invertible map.
  2. Do you need to search or match items? → contrastive (CLIP, SBERT). Cosine distance in these spaces is directly meaningful, which is not true of most others.
  3. Do you need general-purpose features for many unknown downstream tasks? → predictive self-supervision (DINO, I-JEPA, BERT). These transfer best.
  4. Do you have abundant labels and exactly one task? → supervised. It will still beat everything else in that narrow setting, and it is much simpler.
  5. Is it a categorical column in a table? → you are in sense 3; go to Feature encoding and mostly stop worrying about neural anything.
Tier 3 · Formal

The objective, the equations, the guarantees.

Definition

Section titled “Definition”

An encoder is a parameterised map

Eθ:X⟶Z,dim⁡(Z)≪dim⁡(X),E_\theta : \mathcal{X} \longrightarrow \mathcal{Z}, \qquad \dim(\mathcal{Z}) \ll \dim(\mathcal{X}),Eθ​:X⟶Z,dim(Z)≪dim(X),

trained so that some downstream functional ggg composed with EθE_\thetaEθ​ achieves low risk:

θ⋆=arg⁡min⁡θ  E(x,y)∼D[ℓ(g(Eθ(x)), y)].\theta^\star = \arg\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}} \big[\ell\big(g(E_\theta(x)),\, y\big)\big].θ⋆=argθmin​E(x,y)∼D​[ℓ(g(Eθ​(x)),y)].

The subtlety, and the reason encoders are interesting rather than trivial, is that in self-supervised settings yyy is unavailable at training time and ggg is unknown. You are optimising a proxy objective and hoping the resulting space serves a family of ggg‘s you have not seen yet. Everything difficult about encoder design follows from that gap.

The information-theoretic frame

Section titled “The information-theoretic frame”

The cleanest formalisation is the information bottleneck. Write XXX for the input, YYY for the (possibly hypothetical) downstream target, Z=Eθ(X)Z = E_\theta(X)Z=Eθ​(X). We want

max⁡θ  I(Z;Y)−β I(Z;X),\max_\theta \; I(Z; Y) - \beta\, I(Z; X),θmax​I(Z;Y)−βI(Z;X),

which says: keep everything about XXX that predicts YYY, discard everything else. The Lagrange multiplier β\betaβ sets the exchange rate.

This single expression organises the whole field:

  • β→0\beta \to 0β→0 — keep everything. The identity function is optimal. Useless, because the downstream problem is exactly as hard as before.
  • β→∞\beta \to \inftyβ→∞ — keep nothing. A constant is optimal. This is representation collapse, and note that it is not a bug in an implementation: it is the global optimum of a badly balanced objective.
  • Intermediate β\betaβ — the useful regime, and every encoder family is a different bet about how to find it without knowing YYY.

Where each family's bet lies

Reconstructive objectives set a floor on I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) by requiring XXX be recoverable — which prevents collapse for free, but forces ZZZ to retain detail that no plausible YYY needs. Contrastive objectives estimate a lower bound on I(Z;Y)I(Z;Y)I(Z;Y) using augmentations as a stand-in for YYY-invariance, with negatives supplying the repulsive force that keeps I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) from collapsing. Predictive objectives drop the negatives and must therefore impose the anti-collapse constraint architecturally — via stop-gradients, EMA targets, or explicit variance/covariance penalties.

Why compression is not the objective

Section titled “Why compression is not the objective”

A common misreading: “an encoder compresses, so more compression is better.”

Formally this is false, and the counterexample is immediate. Let hhh be a perfect hash into 64 bits. Then I(h(X);X)I(h(X); X)I(h(X);X) is essentially maximal — h(X)h(X)h(X) determines XXX within the dataset — while I(h(X);Y)I(h(X); Y)I(h(X);Y) is zero for any non-trivial YYY, because the hash destroys all metric structure. Compression is extreme; utility is nil.

What actually matters is that the geometry of Z\mathcal{Z}Z aligns with the semantics of the task. Two standard measurements:

alignment=E(x,x+)[∥E(x)−E(x+)∥2],uniformity=log⁡E(x,x−)[e−2∥E(x)−E(x−)∥2].\text{alignment} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^+)}\big[\|E(x) - E(x^+)\|^2\big], \qquad \text{uniformity} = \log \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^-)}\big[e^{-2\|E(x)-E(x^-)\|^2}\big].alignment=E(x,x+)​[∥E(x)−E(x+)∥2],uniformity=logE(x,x−)​[e−2∥E(x)−E(x−)∥2].

Alignment says semantically equivalent inputs land close together; uniformity says the embeddings spread out over the space rather than clumping. Wang & Isola showed these two quantities essentially are what the InfoNCE loss optimises, and that measuring them separately predicts downstream performance better than the loss value does. If you only remember one diagnostic for an encoder, make it this pair.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where the three senses stand, as of 2026

Section titled “Where the three senses stand, as of 2026”

Neural encoders — predictive self-supervision has won the argument, not yet the benchmarks. The theoretical case against reconstruction (it spends capacity on irrelevant high-entropy detail) is broadly accepted. But masked autoencoders remain extremely competitive, and the JEPA family’s advantage is clearest at large scale and on transfer, less so on small-data benchmarks. Treat “latent prediction is strictly better” as a directional claim, not a settled one.

Architectural encoders — the encoder-only obituary was premature. Between 2020 and 2023 the field largely abandoned encoder-only models for decoder-only LLMs. That has partly reversed: ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024) demonstrated that encoder-only models had simply been left un-modernised rather than superseded, and controlled comparisons in 2025 — notably the paired encoder/decoder suite in Seq vs Seq — found encoders still ahead on classification and retrieval at matched parameter counts. For embeddings and reranking specifically, encoder-only is currently the better default.

Feature encoding — solved in theory, still routinely botched in practice. The methods have been stable for two decades. The failure is procedural: target encoding computed before the train/test split leaks the label, and the resulting model validates beautifully and fails in production. This is, by a wide margin, the most common serious bug in applied tabular ML.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No task-agnostic metric for encoder quality. Linear probing is the standard proxy and it is known to be a poor one — it rewards linearly accessible information and is blind to structure a non-linear head could use. Alignment and uniformity are better but only defined relative to a chosen augmentation family.

  • Collapse is prevented empirically, not understood. Stop-gradients and EMA targets demonstrably work. Why they work is still debated, and there is no theory that predicts in advance whether a given asymmetry is sufficient.

  • Encoder spaces are not interoperable. Two encoders trained on the same data with the same objective and different seeds produce spaces that are not comparable without an alignment procedure. This blocks modular reuse, and it is the reason systems like HWM must train their levels in a shared latent space rather than stitching two independently trained encoders together.

  • Cardinality still breaks tabular encoding. No categorical-encoding method handles a million-category column well. Hashing collides, target encoding overfits, embeddings need more data than the tail categories have.
