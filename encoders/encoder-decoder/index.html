Encoder–Decoder Architectures

Here “encoder” names a position, not a technique: the half of a model that reads input and produces a representation, as opposed to the half that emits output one token at a time. The whole history of this design is one problem and its fixes — a fixed-size summary cannot carry a long input, attention removed that limit, and the field then spent a decade working out whether you need the encoder half at all. As of 2026 the answer is: for generation, no; for understanding and retrieval, yes.

What you should walk away with

  • The encoder–decoder split exists because input and output are asymmetric. The input is fully available and can be read in any order; the output must be produced left to right. Bidirectional attention on one side and causal masking on the other is the natural consequence.
  • The bottleneck was the original sin, and attention was the fix. Compressing an arbitrarily long sentence into one fixed vector degrades with length. Bahdanau et al. let the decoder look back at every input position — the single most consequential change in the lineage.
  • Encoder-only and decoder-only differ by one thing: the attention mask. Not by size, not by training data, not by architecture family. Bidirectional versus causal is the entire distinction, and everything else follows from it.
  • Decoder-only won generation; encoder-only did not lose understanding. The 2020–2023 consensus that decoders subsume encoders has partly reversed on the strength of matched-parameter comparisons published in 2024–2025.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

Reading versus writing

Section titled “Reading versus writing”

Think about translating a sentence. You do two different things.

First you read the whole source sentence. You can look at any word in any order, jump back to check whether bank meant a river or a vault, re-read the ending before deciding the tense. Nothing stops you, because the whole sentence is sitting there.

Then you write the translation. Now you are constrained: you produce words in order, and when you choose word five you cannot consult word seven, because word seven doesn’t exist yet.

Encoder = the reading half. Decoder = the writing half. That’s it. And the asymmetry in how you’re allowed to look around is exactly the asymmetry that shows up in the architecture, as a difference in attention masking.

The original design and its flaw

Section titled “The original design and its flaw”

The 2014 systems worked like this: the encoder read the source sentence word by word, ending with a single fixed-size vector — the thought vector — meant to capture the whole meaning. The decoder then generated the translation from that vector alone.

It worked, which was remarkable. But there was an obvious problem, and it showed up exactly where you’d expect: long sentences. One vector of 512 numbers has to hold a three-word sentence and a sixty-word sentence equally well. It cannot. Translation quality visibly degraded as sentences got longer, and the degradation was sharp rather than gradual.

Why this history is worth knowing

Attention was not invented as a general-purpose mechanism. It was invented as a patch for one specific failure — long-sentence translation quality — and only later turned out to be the thing worth keeping. Attention Is All You Need is literally the observation that you could throw away the recurrent parts and keep only the patch.

Three shapes, one difference

Section titled “Three shapes, one difference”

Modern transformers come in three configurations, and people talk about them as if they were three different architectures. They are not. They differ in the attention mask.

Tier 2 · Mechanics

How it actually works. Pseudocode, no heavy maths.

What each shape can and cannot do

Section titled “What each shape can and cannot do”

  1. Encoder-only. Every token attends to every other token, in both directions. To decide what bank means, the model can use the word three positions later. This is why encoder representations are better: they are genuinely contextual in both directions. It also means the model cannot be used for autoregressive generation — there is no notion of “next token” when every position already sees the future. Training is masked language modelling: hide 15% of tokens, predict them from both sides.

  2. Decoder-only. Each token attends only to itself and earlier tokens. That restriction is what makes next-token prediction a well-posed objective — the model never sees the answer. Every position is simultaneously a training example, which makes the objective extremely sample-efficient, and this is a large part of why decoder-only scaled so well.

  3. Encoder–decoder. The encoder processes the input bidirectionally. The decoder generates causally, but each decoder layer additionally runs cross-attention: queries from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder output. The input is read with full context; the output is produced in order.

The training-signal asymmetry is underrated

Masked language modelling supervises only the ~15% of positions you masked. Next-token prediction supervises every position. For the same corpus, a decoder-only model gets roughly 6× more gradient signal per pass. This is a real efficiency advantage of decoders — and it is separate from, and often confused with, the question of which produces better representations per parameter.

When to actually use which

Section titled “When to actually use which”
  • Semantic search, RAG retrieval, reranking, deduplication → encoder-only. You need one vector per document and cheap cosine comparison. A decoder-only LLM can produce embeddings, but at matched quality it costs far more.
  • Classification, NER, token tagging → encoder-only, unless you need zero-shot behaviour, in which case an LLM’s prompting ability may outweigh its inefficiency.
  • Anything conversational, open-ended, or requiring in-context learning → decoder-only. Not close.
  • Fixed input → fixed output transformations at scale (translation, summarisation, speech recognition) → encoder–decoder is still competitive and often cheaper at inference, because the encoder runs once rather than being reprocessed at every generated token.

In the pre-attention design the decoder is conditioned on a single vector c=hT∈Rdc = h_T \in \mathbb{R}^{d}c=hT​∈Rd:

Masked language modelling, with M\mathcal{M}M the masked index set:

LCLM=−∑i=1nlog⁡pθ(xi∣x<i).\mathcal{L}_{\text{CLM}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p_\theta\big(x_i \mid x_{<i}\big).LCLM​=−i=1∑n​logpθ​(xi​∣x<i​).

Two structural differences, both consequential:

Conditioning set. MLM conditions on both sides; CLM on the left only. The MLM representation of token iii can encode information from token i+5i+5i+5; the CLM representation cannot. For any task where the answer depends on later context — which is most classification and retrieval — this is a genuine advantage, and it is not recoverable by scale.

Signal density. MLM supervises ∣M∣≈0.15n|\mathcal{M}| \approx 0.15n∣M∣≈0.15n positions per sequence; CLM supervises all nnn. Per unit of compute, CLM extracts more gradient.

These pull in opposite directions, which is precisely why the question “which is better?” has no unconditional answer and why the 2025 controlled comparisons matter more than the earlier informal consensus did.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

The encoder-only revival, as of 2026

Section titled “The encoder-only revival, as of 2026”

Between roughly 2020 and 2023 the field’s working assumption was that decoder-only LLMs subsume encoders: if a model can do everything by prompting, why keep a specialised understanding architecture? Encoder-only research largely stopped, and BERT-family models stayed frozen at their 2019 recipes while decoders absorbed a decade of improvements.

Three results have complicated that picture:

ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024) applied the accumulated decoder-side engineering — rotary position embeddings, 8192-token context, modern data mixtures and training recipes — to an encoder-only architecture. The result was substantially better than BERT and RoBERTa on classification and retrieval at comparable size. The finding is not that encoders are secretly superior; it is that the comparison everyone had been making was against an un-modernised baseline.

Seq vs Seq (Weller et al., 2025) is the controlled experiment the debate needed: paired encoder and decoder models trained on identical data with identical recipes, differing only in objective and mask. At matched parameter counts, encoders led on classification and retrieval; decoders led on generation. Neither subsumed the other, and cross-objective adaptation did not close the gap.

Should We Still Pretrain Encoders with MLM? (Gisserot-Boukhlef et al., 2025) examined whether the MLM objective itself is the source of the advantage or whether CLM pretraining followed by adaptation would do. The nuanced answer — that a CLM-then-MLM sequence can be compute-efficient — suggests the two objectives are complementary rather than competing.

How to hold this

The honest summary is “for retrieval and classification at a fixed parameter budget, encoder-only is currently the better default” — not “encoders beat decoders”. The comparison is budget-dependent, and a sufficiently large decoder-only model will outperform a small encoder on almost anything. The claim is about efficiency, and efficiency claims have to state their budget.

Where encoder–decoder still lives

Section titled “Where encoder–decoder still lives”

Largely outside text-to-text NLP, where the input is a different modality from the output and the asymmetry is unavoidable:

  • Speech recognition — Whisper is encoder–decoder, because audio frames and text tokens are not the same kind of object.
  • Any fixed-input, repeated-decode workload — the encoder runs once and its output is reused for every generated token, which is a real inference saving over a decoder-only model that reprocesses the prompt.
  • Diffusion conditioning — text encoders feed cross-attention in image generators. This is an encoder–decoder structure even though nobody calls it one.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • Nobody has cleanly separated objective from mask. Encoder-only models are bidirectional and MLM-trained; decoder-only are causal and CLM-trained. The 2025 work begins to disentangle these, but the four-way factorial comparison does not exist at scale.

  • Bidirectional generation remains unsolved. Diffusion language models and masked-generative approaches attempt it, and none currently match autoregressive quality. If one did, the encoder/decoder distinction would largely dissolve.

  • Long context has not settled the bottleneck question, only moved it. Attention’s quadratic cost means very long inputs are once again compressed — now into KV caches, sparse patterns, or state-space summaries. The 2014 bottleneck argument applies to those compressions too, and it is not obvious the field has internalised that.

  • Encoder-only models cannot do in-context learning, and there is no known reason this must be so. It may be an artefact of the training objective rather than of bidirectionality.
