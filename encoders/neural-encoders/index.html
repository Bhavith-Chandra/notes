Neural Encoders

A neural encoder is a learned function from raw data to a vector. Everything interesting about one is determined not by its architecture but by the objective that trained it — which is why a ResNet trained on ImageNet and the same ResNet trained with SimCLR share every weight shape and almost no useful behaviour. This page walks the six families in the order they were invented, because each one exists to fix a specific, nameable failure of the one before it.

What you should walk away with

  • The objective is the encoder. Architecture determines what can be represented; the training signal determines what is. When choosing an encoder, read the loss function first and the architecture second.
  • Each family fixes the previous family’s failure. AE overfits to identity → DAE adds noise. DAE’s space has holes → VAE adds a prior. VAE blurs → VQ-VAE discretises. All of them waste capacity on pixels → contrastive and JEPA move the loss into latent space.
  • Reconstruction prevents collapse for free, and that is its real value. Not fidelity — collapse-resistance. Every non-reconstructive method has to buy that resistance some other way, and that purchase is the hard part of modern SSL.
  • Normalise before you use cosine distance. Contrastive encoders produce spaces where cosine similarity is meaningful; reconstructive ones generally do not. Using the wrong metric on the wrong space is a silent, extremely common error.
Tier 1 · Intuition

Plain language. No equations, no prerequisites.

The compression game

Section titled “The compression game”

Here is a game. I show you a photograph. You have to describe it to a friend in exactly 32 numbers, and your friend — who has never seen the photo — has to draw it back. You both practise on a million photographs until you get good.

To win, you are forced to discover structure. Wasting numbers on individual pixel values is hopeless; there are millions of pixels and you have 32 slots. So you start encoding concepts: how bright overall, is there a face, roughly where, is it outdoors. Nobody told you to find those concepts. The bottleneck did.

That bottleneck-forces-abstraction effect is the entire foundation of neural encoders. Everything that follows is a refinement of it.

The six families, and the complaint that produced each

Section titled “The six families, and the complaint that produced each”

You can learn this whole page as a chain of complaints.

  1. Autoencoder. Squeeze data through a narrow layer, reconstruct it. Complaint: with enough capacity it just memorises — it learns to copy, not to understand.

  2. Denoising autoencoder. Corrupt the input, reconstruct the clean original. Copying no longer works, because the thing to output is not the thing you were given. Complaint: the latent space is full of holes. Points between two valid encodings decode to garbage.

  3. Variational autoencoder. Encode to a distribution rather than a point, and pull all those distributions toward a common shape. Now the space is smooth and you can sample from it. Complaint: averaging over a distribution makes outputs blurry.

  4. VQ-VAE. Snap each encoding to the nearest entry in a learned codebook — discrete tokens instead of a continuous blur. Complaint: still reconstructing pixels, so still spending capacity on texture nobody cares about.

  5. Contrastive encoders. Stop reconstructing. Just require that two views of the same thing land close together and two different things land far apart. Complaint: “far apart from what?” requires enormous batches of negative examples.

  6. Joint-embedding predictive (JEPA). Drop the negatives. Predict one part’s representation from another part’s, and prevent the trivial constant solution architecturally. Complaint: preventing collapse is now the whole difficulty, and it is not well understood.

If you only remember one thing

Reconstruction-based encoders answer “can you rebuild it?”. Embedding-based encoders answer “can you tell these two apart?”. The second question is closer to what almost every downstream task actually needs, and that single observation explains the last eight years of the field.

What it does. Encoder squeezes to a bottleneck, decoder rebuilds. Loss is pixel-wise reconstruction error. The denoising variant corrupts the input first (masking, Gaussian noise, dropout) and asks for the clean original back.

Why the denoising trick matters more than it looks. A plain autoencoder with a wide enough bottleneck can learn the identity function and score perfectly while learning nothing. Corruption breaks that shortcut: to fill in a masked region you must know what usually goes there, which is a statement about structure, not about copying. This single idea — corrupt, then restore — is the direct ancestor of BERT’s masked language modelling and of MAE.

Use it when. Anomaly detection (train on normal data; high reconstruction error signals an outlier), denoising, and as a pretraining step when you have lots of unlabelled data and no better idea.

Don’t use it when. You need a smooth or samplable latent space. AE latents are an arbitrary point cloud with gaps.

What it does. The encoder outputs a mean and a variance rather than a point. You sample from that Gaussian, decode, and add a KL penalty pulling every per-input Gaussian toward a standard normal.

Why. The KL term is what fills the holes. It forces the aggregate of all encodings to cover the prior, so any point you sample decodes to something plausible. That is what makes a VAE generative where an AE is not.

The knob that matters. β\betaβ scales the KL term. Raise it and you get more disentangled, more sample-able latents but blurrier reconstructions; lower it and you approach a plain autoencoder. There is no setting that gives you both, and this trade-off is intrinsic rather than a tuning failure.

Use it when. You need to generate, interpolate, or sample. Also when you want an explicit density-ish model for detecting distribution shift.

Don’t use it when. You want sharp outputs, or you only want features. For pure representation quality, contrastive and predictive methods beat VAEs decisively.

What it does. Between encoder and decoder, replace each continuous latent vector with its nearest neighbour from a learned codebook of KKK entries. The latent becomes a grid of discrete integer tokens.

Why this fixes blur. A VAE’s decoder averages over a continuous distribution, and averaging images produces blur. Snapping to a codebook entry removes the averaging — each token has a definite decoded meaning.

Why it mattered historically. Discrete tokens let you model images and audio with the same autoregressive machinery used for text. Almost every image-token generative system descends from this idea.

The characteristic failure. Codebook collapse — most entries go unused and the effective vocabulary is a fraction of KKK. Mitigations include EMA codebook updates, restarting dead codes from encoder outputs, and ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​-normalising the codebook.

Use it when. You want discrete tokens for a downstream sequence model, or you need sharp reconstructions with a compact latent.

These are backbones — the architectural substrate — rather than objectives, and you can train either with any of the objectives on this page.

CNN (e.g. ResNet). Builds in translation equivariance and locality. A cat is a cat wherever it appears, and nearby pixels matter more than distant ones. Those priors are correct for natural images, which is why CNNs are far more sample-efficient on small datasets.

ViT. Splits the image into patches and runs self-attention. Assumes almost nothing about images — which is a liability at small scale and an asset at large scale, because the model can learn priors from data rather than inheriting ones that may be wrong.

The crossover. ViTs underperform CNNs on ImageNet-scale data and overtake them given roughly 100M+ images. If your dataset is small and you are not starting from pretrained weights, a CNN is very likely the better choice, and this is still true in 2026.

What it does. Take two augmented views of the same image (crop, colour jitter, blur). Require their embeddings to be close. Require embeddings of different images to be far. That is the whole method.

What the augmentations really are. They are your specification of what should be ignored. Crop-invariance says position doesn’t matter; colour-jitter says hue doesn’t matter. Choosing augmentations is choosing what the encoder will be blind to — which is a feature for object recognition and a catastrophe for a task where colour is the label. SimCLR’s ablations showed augmentation choice dominates architecture choice, and that finding has held up.

The cost. Negatives. You need many, and they must be in the same batch (SimCLR used batches up to 8192) or in a maintained queue (MoCo). This is the practical barrier to using contrastive methods without a lot of hardware.

Use it when. Retrieval, search, deduplication, matching, or anything where you will compare embeddings with cosine similarity. CLIP-style cross-modal training is the same recipe with image/text pairs as the positive.

What it does. Two encoders — a student and an EMA copy of it called the teacher. Show the student a partial or augmented view, ask it to predict the teacher’s embedding of another view. Gradients flow only through the student (stop-gradient on the teacher).

Why no negatives are needed. The asymmetry does the work. Because the teacher updates slowly and receives no gradient, the student cannot reach the trivial constant solution by dragging the target to meet it. The target is, in effect, a moving but non-cooperative goalpost.

Why this is unsatisfying. It empirically works and there is no theory that predicts in advance whether a given asymmetry suffices. This is stated plainly in the literature and remains true.

I-JEPA specifically. Rather than augmenting, it masks: predict the representations of several target blocks from a single context block, in latent space. No hand-designed augmentations at all, which removes the “you chose the invariances” objection that dogs contrastive methods.

Use it when. You want general-purpose features for unknown downstream tasks and you have a lot of unlabelled data. This is the current best answer for that situation.

The metric mismatch

Cosine similarity is meaningful in contrastive and JEPA spaces because the objective explicitly shapes angular geometry, usually on a normalised sphere. It is not automatically meaningful in AE or VAE latent spaces, where nothing constrained the norms or angles. Running a cosine-similarity nearest-neighbour search over VAE latents is a real and common mistake — it returns something, it just doesn’t mean what you think.

Autoencoder and the PCA connection

Section titled “Autoencoder and the PCA connection”

For an encoder EθE_\thetaEθ​ and decoder DψD_\psiDψ​:

A result worth knowing because it calibrates expectations: a linear autoencoder with squared loss recovers the principal subspace of the data. With E(x)=WxE(x)=WxE(x)=Wx, D(z)=W⊤zD(z)=W^{\top}zD(z)=W⊤z and W∈Rk×dW \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times d}W∈Rk×d, the optimum spans the same subspace as the top-kkk eigenvectors of the covariance — though not necessarily the eigenvectors themselves, since any rotation within the subspace is equally optimal.

The practical reading: the non-linearity is doing all the work. If your autoencoder isn’t beating PCA on your problem, it has not learned anything a linear method couldn’t, and you should check your training rather than your architecture.

Vincent et al. showed this is equivalent to learning the score of the data distribution in the small-noise limit — the same object that modern diffusion models estimate. The lineage from DAE to diffusion is direct.

The reparameterisation trick. Sampling z∼qθ(z∣x)z \sim q_\theta(z\mid x)z∼qθ​(z∣x) is not differentiable in θ\thetaθ. Rewrite it as a deterministic function of a parameter-free noise variable:

Now the randomness sits in ϵ\epsilonϵ, which carries no parameters, and gradients pass straight through μθ\mu_\thetaμθ​ and σθ\sigma_\thetaσθ​. Without this the variance of the gradient estimator is so high that training is impractical — this trick, not the ELBO, is the reason VAEs work.

For diagonal Gaussians against a standard normal prior the KL is closed-form:

KL=−12∑j=1k(1+log⁡σj2−μj2−σj2).\mathrm{KL} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\Big).KL=−21​j=1∑k​(1+logσj2​−μj2​−σj2​).

Posterior collapse. If the decoder is powerful enough to model xxx without using zzz, the optimiser drives qθ(z∣x)→p(z)q_\theta(z\mid x) \to p(z)qθ​(z∣x)→p(z), sending the KL to zero and making the latent carry no information. Look at the expression above: an encoder that outputs μj=0,σj=1\mu_j = 0, \sigma_j = 1μj​=0,σj​=1 for every input scores perfectly on the KL term. Collapse is not a bug; it is a local optimum the objective actively rewards. Mitigations — KL annealing, free bits, weakening the decoder — all amount to preventing the optimiser from taking that shortcut early.

Beta-VAE and the rate–distortion reading

Section titled “Beta-VAE and the rate–distortion reading”
Lβ=Eq[log⁡pψ(x∣z)]−β KL(qθ(z∣x) ∥ p(z)).\mathcal{L}_{\beta} = \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p_\psi(x\mid z)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\theta(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).Lβ​=Eq​[logpψ​(x∣z)]−βKL(qθ​(z∣x)∥p(z)).

The KL term is the rate — bits per sample the latent carries. The reconstruction term is negative distortion. So β\betaβ is a position on a rate–distortion curve, and you cannot be at two positions at once. This reframing is why “β\betaβ-VAE gives disentanglement” is better stated as “β\betaβ-VAE restricts the rate, and under some conditions the surviving bits align with generative factors” — the disentanglement claim has been contested, and the rate–distortion claim has not.

VQ-VAE: quantisation and the straight-through estimator

Section titled “VQ-VAE: quantisation and the straight-through estimator”

Given a codebook e∈RK×D\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{K\times D}e∈RK×D, quantise:

zq(x)=ek,k=arg⁡min⁡j∥ze(x)−ej∥2.z_q(x) = \mathbf{e}_k, \qquad k = \arg\min_j \big\|z_e(x) - \mathbf{e}_j\big\|_2.zq​(x)=ek​,k=argjmin​​ze​(x)−ej​​2​.

The arg⁡min⁡\arg\minargmin has zero gradient almost everywhere, so the decoder’s gradient cannot reach the encoder. VQ-VAE simply copies the gradient across the quantisation step — the straight-through estimator — and adds two terms to keep the two sides from drifting apart:

L=log⁡p(x∣zq(x))⏟reconstruction+∥sg⁡[ze(x)]−e∥22⏟codebook loss+β∥ze(x)−sg⁡[e]∥22⏟commitment loss.\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\log p(x \mid z_q(x))}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \underbrace{\big\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e(x)] - \mathbf{e}\big\|_2^2}_{\text{codebook loss}} + \beta \underbrace{\big\|z_e(x) - \operatorname{sg}[\mathbf{e}]\big\|_2^2}_{\text{commitment loss}}.L=reconstructionlogp(x∣zq​(x))​​+codebook loss​sg[ze​(x)]−e​22​​​+βcommitment loss​ze​(x)−sg[e]​22​​​.

Read the two sg⁡\operatorname{sg}sg placements carefully — they are the whole design. The codebook loss moves codebook entries toward encoder outputs; the commitment loss moves encoder outputs toward codebook entries. Splitting them lets the two move at different rates, with β\betaβ (typically 0.25) controlling how strongly the encoder is held to its assigned code.

Contrastive: InfoNCE

Section titled “Contrastive: InfoNCE”

For an anchor xxx, a positive x+x^+x+, and N−1N-1N−1 negatives xi−x_i^-xi−​:

LInfoNCE=−log⁡exp⁡ ⁣(sim⁡(z,z+)/τ)exp⁡ ⁣(sim⁡(z,z+)/τ)+∑i=1N−1exp⁡ ⁣(sim⁡(z,zi−)/τ),\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z^+)/\tau\big)}{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z^+)/\tau\big) + \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(z, z_i^-)/\tau\big)},LInfoNCE​=−logexp(sim(z,z+)/τ)+∑i=1N−1​exp(sim(z,zi−​)/τ)exp(sim(z,z+)/τ)​,

Two facts about this loss that are worth carrying around:

It bounds mutual information. I(z;z+)≥log⁡N−LInfoNCEI(z; z^+) \geq \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}}I(z;z+)≥logN−LInfoNCE​. Note the log⁡N\log NlogN: the bound is capped by batch size, which is the formal reason large batches help and small ones plateau. It also means the bound is loose at practical NNN, so don’t over-read it.

Temperature controls how hard the hard negatives hit. Small τ\tauτ sharpens the softmax so gradient concentrates on the most similar negatives; large τ\tauτ spreads it. τ\tauτ is a genuinely sensitive hyperparameter, not a formality — typical values sit around 0.07–0.2.

And the decomposition from Wang & Isola, which is the most useful practical handle on this loss:

Lalign=E(x,x+)[∥z−z+∥2α],Lunif=log⁡E(x,y)[e−t∥zx−zy∥22].\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,x^+)}\big[\|z - z^+\|_2^{\alpha}\big], \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{unif}} = \log \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[e^{-t\|z_x - z_y\|_2^2}\big].Lalign​=E(x,x+)​[∥z−z+∥2α​],Lunif​=logE(x,y)​[e−t∥zx​−zy​∥22​].

Asymptotically InfoNCE optimises exactly these two, and measuring them separately diagnoses a failing encoder far better than the loss value does — good alignment with bad uniformity means you are collapsing; the reverse means your positives are too weak.

JEPA: prediction with an asymmetric target

Section titled “JEPA: prediction with an asymmetric target”
LJEPA=∑i∥Pϕ(Eθ(xctx), mi)−sg⁡[Eθˉ(xtgt(i))]∥1,θˉ←λθˉ+(1−λ)θ.\mathcal{L}_{\text{JEPA}} = \sum_{i} \Big\| P_\phi\big(E_\theta(x_{\text{ctx}}),\, m_i\big) - \operatorname{sg}\big[E_{\bar\theta}(x_{\text{tgt}}^{(i)})\big] \Big\|_1, \qquad \bar\theta \leftarrow \lambda\bar\theta + (1-\lambda)\theta .LJEPA​=i∑​​Pϕ​(Eθ​(xctx​),mi​)−sg[Eθˉ​(xtgt(i)​)]​1​,θˉ←λθˉ+(1−λ)θ.

Here mim_imi​ positions the iii-th target block and θˉ\bar\thetaθˉ is the EMA teacher, typically with λ≈0.996→1.0\lambda \approx 0.996 \to 1.0λ≈0.996→1.0 on a schedule.

Why this does not collapse. Consider the degenerate solution Eθ≡cE_\theta \equiv cEθ​≡c. For the loss to be zero the teacher must also output ccc — but the teacher is a lagged average, so it arrives at ccc only after the student has already been constant for many steps, during which the loss is non-zero and gradients push away. The stop-gradient means the student can never shortcut this by pulling the target down. That is the mechanism, and it is an argument about dynamics, not a proof: there is no guarantee, and collapse does occur when λ\lambdaλ is set too low.

Read this as three anti-collapse strategies

Reconstruction forces I(Z;X)I(Z;X)I(Z;X) above a floor by requiring invertibility. Contrastive learning adds an explicit repulsive term. Self-distillation uses temporal asymmetry. Every method on this page uses exactly one of these three, and knowing which tells you immediately what its failure mode is.

Tier 4 · Frontier

Open problems, current results, and what is still contested.

Where things stand, as of 2026

Section titled “Where things stand, as of 2026”

Masked reconstruction did not die, and that is genuinely awkward for the latent-prediction thesis. MAE reconstructs raw pixels with a 75% mask ratio and remains a strong pretraining method. The theoretical argument says it should waste capacity on irrelevant detail; the benchmarks say it is competitive. The reconciliation people generally offer — that an aggressive mask ratio makes the task semantic enough that pixel-level detail stops dominating the gradient — is plausible but not established. Anyone presenting this as settled is overstating.

Encoder-only text models were prematurely written off. After 2020 the field moved to decoder-only LLMs for nearly everything. ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024) showed encoder-only architectures had simply not received the same modernisation — rotary embeddings, longer context, better data — rather than being fundamentally inferior. Controlled 2025 comparisons at matched parameter counts found encoders still ahead on classification and retrieval. For embeddings specifically, encoder-only remains the better default.

Vision encoders have consolidated around self-distillation. DINO-family features are the practical default for general-purpose visual representation, and they exhibit properties nobody trained for — attention maps that segment objects without any segmentation supervision. That emergent behaviour is the strongest qualitative evidence that the objective is capturing something structural.

Open problems

Section titled “Open problems”

  • No good evaluation. Linear probing rewards linearly accessible information and is blind to structure a non-linear head could exploit; full fine-tuning measures the fine-tuning recipe as much as the encoder. Alignment/uniformity is better but is defined relative to a chosen augmentation family, which begs the question.

  • Anti-collapse is empirical. We have three strategies that work and no theory that says in advance whether a proposed asymmetry is sufficient. Every new method’s collapse-resistance is established by running it.

  • Augmentation choice is unprincipled. Contrastive methods require you to specify invariances up front, and getting that wrong silently destroys information you needed. I-JEPA’s masking sidesteps it; whether masking introduces its own implicit invariances is not well characterised.

  • Encoder spaces are not interoperable. Same data, same objective, different seed produces incomparable spaces. This blocks modular reuse and forces hierarchical systems to train levels jointly in a shared space rather than composing independently trained parts.

  • Scaling laws for encoders are poorly mapped compared to generative models. We know roughly that more data helps; we do not have the clean compute-optimal curves that exist for language modelling.

What to read next, in order

Section titled “What to read next, in order”
  1. Kingma & Welling (2013) — for the reparameterisation trick specifically. Section 2.4 is the part that matters.
  2. van den Oord et al. (2017) — read it for the straight-through estimator and the two-term loss split; it is short and the design is unusually legible.
  3. Chen et al. (2020), SimCLR — read the augmentation ablations, not the method. They are the most transferable finding in the paper.
  4. Assran et al. (2023), I-JEPA — the current reference point for augmentation-free representation learning.
